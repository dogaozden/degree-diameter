\begin{abstract}
Written for Gabriela Araujo-Pardo and Ignacio L\'opez. Each construction of the lab is described here only in terms of incidence: points, lines, the lines through a point, the points of a line, flags, anti-flags, collinearity, distances in the incidence graph, and configurations (ovoids, spreads, $m$-ovoids, whiskers, partial ovoids, local dualities) defined by their combinatorial properties. For each construction we say what is chosen in the starting geometry, which incidences are deleted or moved, which new points and lines are added and exactly what they are incident with, which pairs of elements stop being collinear or concurrent and how the new elements repair this, and why the diameter and the degrees are as claimed. Section~\ref{sec:d3} treats the diameter-$3$ constructions in a projective plane: the three hats of Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez and our four hats, side by side and in the same words. Sections~\ref{sec:d4}--\ref{sec:d7} treat diameters $4$, $6$ and $7$ in generalized quadrangles and hexagons, and Section~\ref{sec:d5} treats diameter $5$: copies of a quadrangle joined by bijections, freed incidences and hubs, with an exact combinatorial criterion for which incidences can be freed. Section~\ref{sec:d8} treats G\'omez's diameter-$8$ construction on copies of a generalized hexagon: it says what is his, why as defined its diameter is at least $9$, and what is known about repairing it with a separate labelling of each copy (it works for $q=2,3$, fails for geometric labellings when $q\ge7$, and is open otherwise). The sections can be read independently.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conventions}\label{sec:conv}

An \emph{incidence structure} consists of points, lines and a symmetric incidence relation $\I$ between points and lines. Points and lines are \emph{elements}. A \emph{flag} is an incident point--line pair, an \emph{anti-flag} a non-incident one. The \emph{degree} of an element is the number of elements incident with it. Two distinct points are \emph{collinear} if some line contains both, and two distinct lines are \emph{concurrent} if some point lies on both. We do \emph{not} assume that two points lie on at most one line: after points and lines are added, two points may have several common lines, and two lines may have the same points.

The \emph{incidence graph} has the elements as vertices and the flags as edges; it is bipartite. Distances $d(x,y)$ and the diameter are taken in it. So two points are at distance $2$ when they are collinear, a point and a line are at distance $3$ when they form an anti-flag and some point of the line is collinear with the point, and so on.

A \emph{generalized $n$-gon} is an incidence structure whose incidence graph has diameter $n$ and girth $2n$; elements at distance $n$ are \emph{opposite}. It has \emph{order $(s,t)$} if every line has $s+1$ points and every point is on $t+1$ lines. Projective planes are the generalized $3$-gons, generalized quadrangles the $4$-gons, generalized hexagons the $6$-gons. Write $n_b(\Delta,D)$ for the largest number of elements of an incidence structure with all degrees at most $\Delta$ and diameter at most $D$ (the bipartite degree--diameter problem). A generalized $n$-gon of order $(q,q)$ attains the counting (Moore) bound for $n_b(q+1,n)$. When $q+1$ is not a prime power, no generalized polygon of order $(q+1,q+1)$ is known, and every construction below starts from a generalized polygon of order $(q,q)$ (or $(q-1,q+1)$) and adds points and lines so that the maximum degree becomes $q+2$ (or more) while the diameter stays the same. Table~\ref{tab:overview} is a summary.

Distances can be read off from collinearity alone:

\begin{lemma}[Diameter in incidence terms]\label{lem:diam}
Let $\cS$ be an incidence structure in which every element is incident with at least one element.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\diam\cS\le 3$ if and only if any two points are collinear and any two lines are concurrent.
\item $\diam\cS\le 4$ if and only if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point of $L$ is collinear with $p$.
\item $\diam\cS\le 6$ if and only if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point of $L$ is at distance at most $4$ from $p$.
\item $\diam\cS\le 7$ if and only if any two points, and any two lines, are at distance at most $6$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For an even bound $D$: two points $p\neq p'$ are at distance at most $D$ as soon as $p$ is within $D-1$ of some line $L$ through $p'$ (if $p\I L$ the distance is $2$); two lines likewise. For an odd bound $D$: an anti-flag $(p,L)$ is within distance $D$ as soon as $p$ is within $D-1$ of some point of $L$. Distances between a point and a line are odd and distances between two points or two lines are even, which gives the converses.
\end{proof}

\section{Diameter 3: hats in a projective plane}\label{sec:d3}

Let $\Pi$ be a projective plane of order $q$: any two points are on exactly one line, any two lines meet in exactly one point, and every element has degree $q+1$. For points $u\ne v$ we write $uv$ for the line joining them. Both constructions in this section keep all points and lines of $\Pi$, delete or move a few flags near a chosen anti-flag, and add a few points and lines. By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(a) the diameter stays $3$ exactly when any two points remain collinear and any two lines remain concurrent; this is what has to be checked.

\subsection{The frame}\label{ssec:frame}
Fix an anti-flag $(A,\ell)$. Every line through $A$ meets $\ell$ in one point, and every point of $\ell$ is on one line through $A$. So the $q+1$ flags $(X,AX)$, $X$ a point of $\ell$, are the only incidences between the points of $\ell$ and the lines through $A$, and they match the points of $\ell$ with the lines through $A$. Both constructions change only some of these $q+1$ flags.

Choose distinct \emph{marked} points $X_s$ on $\ell$, indexed by a label set $S$, and put
\[
\lam_s = AX_s \qquad (s\in S).
\]
One label $a\in S$ is distinguished, and $T=S\setminus\{a\}$. We write
\[
\ell^* = \{\text{points of } \ell\}\setminus\{X_t : t\in T\},\qquad \Lambda^* = \{\text{lines through } A\}\setminus\{\lam_t: t\in T\}.
\]
So $\ell^*$ contains $X_a$ and the $q+1-|S|$ unmarked points of $\ell$, and $\Lambda^*$ contains $\lam_a$ and the $q+1-|S|$ lines joining $A$ to them. We call $A$, $\ell$, the $X_s$ and the $\lam_s$ the \emph{frame}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The construction of Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20} (\emph{three hats}) uses three marked points: $S=\{0,1,a\}$, $T=\{0,1\}$.
\item The \emph{four hats} use four: $S=\{0,1,a,c\}$, $T=\{0,1,c\}$; this needs $q\ge 3$.
\end{itemize}
The labels $0,1,a,c$ are only names. The new elements are, for each $s\in S$, a \emph{hat point} $H_s$ and a \emph{hat line} $K_s$, and one more point $Q$ and one more line $M$.

\subsection{The two constructions}\label{ssec:defs}
Both constructions follow the same six rules. They differ in two permutations of $T$: the \emph{links} $\pi$, a permutation of $T$ without fixed points, and the \emph{rotation} $\rho$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:hats}
The incidence structure $\cS$ has as points the points of $\Pi$, the point $Q$ and the hat points $H_s$; as lines the lines of $\Pi$, the line $M$ and the hat lines $K_s$; and exactly the following incidences.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(R\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item\label{r:plane} The incidences of $\Pi$, except the flags $(X_s,\lam_s)$, $s\in S$; and the flags $(X_t,\lam_{\rho(t)})$, $t\in T$.
\item\label{r:hat} $H_s$ lies on every line of $\Pi$ through $X_s$ other than $\ell$ \quad ($q$ lines, $\lam_s$ among them).
\item\label{r:hatline} $K_s$ contains every point of $\lam_s$ other than $A$ \quad ($q$ points, $X_s$ among them).
\item\label{r:M} $M$ contains the points of $\ell^*$ and every hat point $H_s$.
\item\label{r:Q} $Q$ lies on the lines of $\Lambda^*$ and on every hat line $K_s$.
\item\label{r:links} The \emph{links}: $A\I K_a$, \ $H_a\I\ell$, and $H_t\I K_{\pi(t)}$ for $t\in T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\subsection{What the new elements are: a crossed doubling of the frame}\label{ssec:doubling}
The hat point $H_s$ is a copy of $X_s$ that does not see $\ell$; the hat line $K_s$ is a copy of $\lam_s$ that does not see $A$; and $(Q,M)$ is a copy of the anti-flag $(A,\ell)$ that does not see the elements $X_t$, $\lam_t$, $t\in T$. This is how \cite{AL20} prove that the diameter is $3$: using their Observation~2 they compare each hat vertex with the vertex it copies. In both constructions one has, more precisely: \emph{$H_s$ is at the same distance from every point and line of $\Pi$ as $X_s$ is in $\Pi$, except from $X_s$ itself and, if $s\neq a$, from $\ell$; dually $K_s$ is at the same distance from every element of $\Pi$ as $\lam_s$, except from $\lam_s$ and, if $s\ne a$, from $A$.} Indeed, once Theorem~\ref{thm:hats} below is proved, any two distinct points are at distance $2$ and a point and a line not through it at distance $3$, in $\Pi$ and in $\cS$ alike; and by rules~\ref{r:hat} and~\ref{r:links} the lines of $\Pi$ through $H_s$ are those through $X_s$ other than $\ell$, together with $\ell$ itself if $s=a$. The cleanest way to say all this is as a doubling of the frame, in the sense of Delorme \cite{D85a}, with one twist.

Let $\cF$ be the set of frame elements $A$, $\ell$, $X_s$, $\lam_s$ ($s\in S$), and give each $x\in\cF$ a copy $x'$ of the same kind:
\[
A'=Q,\qquad \ell'=M,\qquad X_s'=H_s,\qquad \lam_s'=K_s .
\]
The \emph{crossed doubling} $\cD$ of the frame has the points and lines of $\Pi$ and these copies, and the following incidences: all incidences of $\Pi$; for $x\in\cF$ and $y\notin\cF$ with $x\I y$, the incidence $x'\I y$ (a copy sees the same elements outside the frame as its original); for $x,y\in\cF$ with $x\I y$, the incidence $x'\I y'$ (copies see copies), except that each flag $(X_s,\lam_s)$ is \emph{crossed}: instead of $X_s'\I\lam_s'$ we put $X_s'\I\lam_s$ and $X_s\I\lam_s'$.

Reading off the neighbours of the copies: $H_s$ lies on $M$ (as $X_s\I\ell$) and on the lines through $X_s$ other than $\ell$ (those other than $\lam_s$ are outside $\cF$; $\lam_s$ by the crossing); $K_s$ contains $Q$ and the points of $\lam_s$ other than $A$; $M$ contains the hat points and the unmarked points of $\ell$; $Q$ lies on the hat lines and the lines through $A$ other than the $\lam_s$. Comparing with Definition~\ref{def:hats}:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:doubling}
$\cS$ is obtained from the crossed doubling $\cD$ of the frame by
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=2.5em]
\item adding the \emph{patches} $X_a\I M$, $Q\I\lam_a$, $A\I K_a$, $H_a\I\ell$ and $H_t\I K_{\pi(t)}$ \textup{(}$t\in T$\textup{)};
\item deleting the flags $(X_s,\lam_s)$, $s\in S$, and adding the flags $(X_t,\lam_{\rho(t)})$, $t\in T$ \textup{(}for the three hats: deleting $(X_a,\lam_a)$\textup{)}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

So the question ``what happens to the combinatorics when these points and lines are added'' splits into two: what the doubling does (Lemma~\ref{lem:dbl}), and what the patches, the deletions and the rotation do (\S\ref{ssec:patches}).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dbl}
In the crossed doubling $\cD$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $X_s$ and $\lam_s$ have degree $q+2$; $A$, $\ell$, $Q$, $M$, $H_s$, $K_s$ have degree $q+1$; an element outside $\cF$ has degree $q+2$ if it is on $\ell$ or on some $\lam_s$ \textup{(}a point\textup{)}, or passes through $A$ or some $X_s$ \textup{(}a line\textup{)}, and degree $q+1$ otherwise.
\item The pairs of points that are not collinear are exactly
\[
\{H_s,X_t\}\ (s\ne t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{H_s,Q\},
\]
and also $\{Q,A\}$ if every point of $\ell$ is marked \textup{(}the four hats with $q=3$\textup{)}. Dually, the pairs of lines that are not concurrent are exactly $\{K_s,\lam_t\}$ \textup{(}$s\ne t$\textup{)} and $\{K_s,M\}$, and also $\{M,\ell\}$ if every point of $\ell$ is marked.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) $X_s$ gains $K_s$ (crossing) and $\lam_s$ gains $H_s$; $A$ and $\ell$ gain nothing, since their frame neighbours are copied together with them. $Q$ lies on $|S|$ hat lines and $q+1-|S|$ lines through $A$; $H_s$ on $M$ and $q$ lines; dually for $M$, $K_s$. A point outside $\cF$ gains $M$ if it is on $\ell$ and $K_s$ if it is on $\lam_s$ (at most one of these, as it is not $A$ or an $X_s$).

(b) Two points of $\Pi$ keep their common line. $Q$ and a point $u$ of $\Pi$: if $u=A$, use a line through $A$ outside $\cF$ (there is one unless every point of $\ell$ is marked; then $Q$ and $A$ have no common line, as the hat lines miss $A$); if $Au$ is not a $\lam_s$, use $Au$; if $u\neq A$ is on $\lam_s$, use $K_s$. $H_s$ and a point $u\notin\ell$: use $uX_s$, which is not $\ell$. $H_s$ and $X_s$: a line through $X_s$ outside $\cF$. $H_s$ and an unmarked point of $\ell$: $M$. Two hat points: $M$. This leaves $H_s$ and $X_t$ ($t\ne s$), whose only common line in $\Pi$ is $\ell$, which $H_s$ is not on, while $M\not\ni X_t$ and the other lines of $H_s$ meet $\ell$ only in $X_s$; and $H_s$ and $Q$, which have no common line (the lines through $H_s$ are $M$, which misses $Q$, and lines of $\Pi$ through $X_s$; the only line of $\Pi$ through $X_s$ and $A$ is $\lam_s$, and $Q\notin\lam_s$). The statement for lines is the same argument with points and lines exchanged; $\cD$ is built from $\Pi$ and the frame by rules that are symmetric under this exchange.
\end{proof}

So the copies cost nothing among the old points: no two points of $\Pi$ become non-collinear and no two lines of $\Pi$ stop meeting. The failures all involve a copy, and they come from paths of length $2$ inside the frame: $X_s-\ell-X_t$ (the copy $H_s$ of $X_s$ does not see $\ell$, and the original $X_t$ does not see the copy $M$ of $\ell$) and $X_s-\lam_s-A$ (the crossing removes the copy $H_s-K_s-Q$ of this path); the extra pair $\{Q,A\}$ occurs only when every line through $A$ is in the frame, so that $A$ and its copy have no neighbour in common. For Delorme's doubling without crossing, the hat lemma (\path{POLISHED.md}, Lemma~1.3) says that a set of elements of a projective plane can be doubled without changing the diameter only if each of its connected components is a single element or a flag; the frame contains the paths $\ell,X_s,\lam_s,A$, so some patching is unavoidable. Lemma~\ref{lem:dbl}(a) also says that the doubling leaves exactly one free incidence at each of the $2|S|+4$ elements $A,\ell,Q,M,H_s,K_s$, and none at $X_s$, $\lam_s$.

\subsection{The patches, the deletions and the rotation}\label{ssec:patches}
The patches of Proposition~\ref{prop:doubling}(i) spend the free incidences of $A,\ell,Q,M,H_s,K_s$, one each. The far pairs of Lemma~\ref{lem:dbl}(b) are repaired as follows (a pair $\{H_s,X_t\}$ always has $s\neq t$):

\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
far pair in $\cD$ & common line in $\cS$ & because of\\
\midrule
$\{H_s,X_a\}$ & $M$ & patch $X_a\I M$\\
$\{H_a,X_t\}$ & $\ell$ & patch $H_a\I\ell$\\
$\{H_t,X_{\pi(t)}\}$, $t\in T$ & $K_{\pi(t)}$ & link $H_t\I K_{\pi(t)}$ (and $X_{\pi(t)}\I K_{\pi(t)}$)\\
$\{H_t,X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}\}$, $t\in T$ (four hats) & $\lam_t$ & rotation $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}\I\lam_t$ (and $H_t\I\lam_t$)\\
$\{H_a,Q\}$ & $\lam_a$ & patch $Q\I\lam_a$ (and $H_a\I\lam_a$)\\
$\{H_t,Q\}$, $t\in T$ & $K_{\pi(t)}$ & link $H_t\I K_{\pi(t)}$\\
$\{Q,A\}$ (only if every point of $\ell$ is marked) & $\lam_a$ & patch $Q\I\lam_a$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent The far pairs of lines are repaired dually: $\{K_s,\lam_a\}$ by $Q$, $\{K_a,\lam_t\}$ by $A$, $\{K_{\pi(t)},\lam_t\}$ by $H_t$, $\{K_t,\lam_{\sigma(t)}\}$ by $X_t$ (four hats), $\{K_a,M\}$ by $X_a$, $\{K_{\pi(t)},M\}$ by $H_t$, and $\{M,\ell\}$ (only if every point of $\ell$ is marked) by $X_a$.

\emph{Why the three hats need no rotation and the four hats do.} The pairs $\{H_s,X_t\}$ with $s=a$ or $t=a$ are repaired by $\ell$ and $M$. What remains is $\{H_t,X_u\}$ for $t\ne u$ in $T$: each hat point $H_t$ must reach the $|T|-1$ points $X_u$, $u\in T\setminus\{t\}$, and it has one free incidence, which goes to a link. For the three hats $|T|-1=1$ and the link $H_t\I K_{\pi(t)}$ suffices. For the four hats $|T|-1=2$: the link reaches $X_{\sigma(t)}$, and $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$ is reached through $\lam_t$, which already carries $H_t$, after the rotation has moved $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$ onto $\lam_t$. Hence $\rho=\pi=\sigma$: $\rho^{-1}(t)$ must be the element of $T\setminus\{t\}$ that $\pi$ does not reach. (Dually each hat line $K_t$ meets $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$ in $X_t$ and $\lam_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$ in $H_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$.) With $|T|=4$ the same count gives a hat point two ways to reach three points, which is one heuristic reason why the pattern stops at four hats; a SAT search confirms that no fifth hat can be added inside this family for $q=4,5,7,8,9,11,13$ (\path{research/plus2/REPORT.md}).

\emph{Why the flags $(X_s,\lam_s)$ are deleted.} The patches $X_a\I M$ and $Q\I\lam_a$ raise the degrees of $X_a$ and $\lam_a$, which were already $q+2$, to $q+3$; deleting the flag $(X_a,\lam_a)$ brings both back to $q+2$. The rotation is degree-neutral: $X_t$ trades $\lam_t$ for $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$, and $\lam_t$ trades $X_t$ for $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$. Deleting a flag $(X,\lam)$ from a projective plane makes exactly $q$ pairs of points non-collinear (the pairs $\{X,Y\}$, $Y$ a point of $\lam$ other than $X$) and $q$ pairs of lines non-concurrent (the pairs $\{\lam,N\}$, $N$ a line through $X$ other than $\lam$). After the deletions and the rotation, the pairs of points of $\Pi$ that have no common line of $\Pi$ any more are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item three hats: $\{X_a,Y\}$ for the $q$ points $Y\ne X_a$ of $\lam_a$, and dually $\{\lam_a,N\}$ for the $q$ lines $N\neq\lam_a$ through $X_a$;
\item four hats: $\{X_s,Y\}$ for $s\in S$ and the $q-1$ points $Y$ of $\lam_s$ other than $A$ and $X_s$, and $\{X_a,A\}$; dually $\{\lam_s,N\}$ for the $q-1$ lines $N$ through $X_s$ other than $\ell$ and $\lam_s$, and $\{\lam_a,\ell\}$. That is $4(q-1)+1$ pairs of each kind. The pairs $\{X_t,A\}$ and $\{\lam_t,\ell\}$, $t\in T$, are not broken, because $X_t$ and $A$ are both on $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$, and $\lam_t$ and $\ell$ both contain $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$.
\end{itemize}
Each broken pair is repaired by the crossing and one patch: $X_s$ and the points of $\lam_s$ other than $A$ are all on $K_s$ (rule~\ref{r:hatline}), and $A\I K_a$ repairs $\{X_a,A\}$; dually $\lam_s$ and the lines through $X_s$ other than $\ell$ all pass through $H_s$, and $H_a\I\ell$ repairs $\{\lam_a,\ell\}$. This is why the flags may be deleted at all: the crossing has already given every point of $\lam_s$ a second line through $X_s$ (namely $K_s$), and every line through $X_s$ a second point on $\lam_s$ (namely $H_s$).

\subsection{Degrees and counts}\label{ssec:degrees}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:deg}
In $\cS$ every element has degree $q+1$ or $q+2$. The points of degree $q+1$ \textup{(}the \emph{deficient} points\textup{)} are the points of $\Pi$ off $\ell$, other than $A$, on the lines joining $A$ to the unmarked points of $\ell$; dually, the lines of degree $q+1$ are the lines of $\Pi$ not through $A$, other than $\ell$, through the unmarked points of $\ell$. There are $(q-1)(q+1-|S|)$ of each: $(q-1)(q-2)$ for the three hats and $(q-1)(q-3)$ for the four hats. Each deficient point lies on exactly $q-|S|$ deficient lines, and each deficient line has exactly $q-|S|$ deficient points.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Count the lines through each point: those of $\Pi$, minus deleted flags, plus added ones. $A$: $q+1$ lines and $K_a$. An unmarked point of $\ell$: $q+1$ lines and $M$. $X_a$: loses $\lam_a$, gains $M$ and $K_a$. $X_t$, $t\in T$: four hats, loses $\lam_t$ and gains $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$ and $K_t$; three hats, gains $K_t$. A point $p\notin\ell$, $p\neq A$: it is on exactly one line through $A$, the line $Ap$; it loses nothing, and gains $K_s$ exactly when $Ap=\lam_s$. $Q$: $|\Lambda^*|+|S|=(q+1-|T|)+|S|=q+2$. $H_s$: $q$ lines by~\ref{r:hat}, $M$, and one link line. The lines are the dual count (Lemma~\ref{lem:dual} below). A deficient point $p$ lies on $Ap$ and on $q$ lines meeting $\ell$ in the $q$ points other than $Ap\cap\ell$; among these, $q-|S|$ are unmarked.
\end{proof}

So $\cS$ has $q^2+q+1+|S|+1$ points and as many lines: $2(q^2+q+5)$ elements for the three hats and $2(q^2+q+6)$ for the four hats. To make it regular, join each deficient point to a deficient line not through it, by a perfect matching. Such a matching exists when there are deficient elements ($|S|\le q$; for the four hats with $q=3$ the structure is already regular): the bipartite graph of non-incident pairs between deficient points and deficient lines is regular of degree $(q-1)(q+1-|S|)-(q-|S|)>0$, and a regular bipartite graph of positive degree has a perfect matching (K\"onig). Adding flags cannot increase distances. (In $\mathrm{PG}(2,q)$ explicit matchings are given in coordinates in \path{coords.tex}, and in \cite{AL20} up to the index set discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:checks}; any matching of non-incident pairs works.)

\subsection{The diameter}\label{ssec:diam}

\begin{lemma}[Points]\label{lem:points}
Any two distinct points of $\cS$ are collinear in $\cS$; Table~\ref{tab:cases} gives a common line for every pair.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We check that Table~\ref{tab:cases} covers every pair and that each named line contains both points. The only flags of $\Pi$ missing from $\cS$ are among the $(X_s,\lam_s)$; so a flag $(u,L)$ of $\Pi$ survives whenever $u\notin\ell$ or $L$ does not pass through $A$.

\emph{Two points of $\Pi$.} If the line $uv$ of $\Pi$ lost an incidence, then (up to order) $u=X_s$, $uv=\lam_s$ with the flag deleted, and $v$ is $A$ or another point of $\lam_s$. If $v\neq A$, both lie on $K_s$ by~\ref{r:hatline}. If $v=A$ and $s=a$: $X_a\I K_a$ by~\ref{r:hatline} and $A\I K_a$ by~\ref{r:links}. If $v=A$ and $s=t\in T$ (four hats): the rotation puts $X_t$ on $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$, which passes through $A$.

\emph{$Q$ and a point $u$ of $\Pi$.} $\lam_a\in\Lambda^*$ passes through $A$ and carries $Q$. $K_s$ contains $X_s$ and $Q$. An unmarked point $u$ of $\ell$: $Au\in\Lambda^*$, and the flag $(u,Au)$ was not touched. A point $u\notin\ell$, $u\ne A$: the flag $(u,Au)$ survives; if $Au\in\Lambda^*$ it carries $Q$; otherwise $Au=\lam_t$, $t\in T$, and $u$ is a point of $\lam_t$ other than $A$, so $u\I K_t\ni Q$.

\emph{$H_s$ and a point $u$ of $\Pi$.} If $u\notin\ell$, the line $uX_s$ of $\Pi$ is not $\ell$, so it carries $H_s$, and it contains $u$ in $\cS$. \emph{Two new points.} $M$ carries all hat points. $\lam_a$ carries $Q$ (as $\lam_a\in\Lambda^*$) and $H_a$ (as a line through $X_a$ other than $\ell$). $K_{\pi(t)}$ carries $Q$ and, by the link, $H_t$.
\end{proof}

For lines we use duality. In the dual plane $\Pi^D$ the line $\ell$ is a point and $A$ a line not through it, the lines $\lam_s$ are points on the line $A$, and the line joining the points $\ell$ and $\lam_s$ is the point $X_s$.

\begin{lemma}[Duality]\label{lem:dual}
The dual of $\cS(\Pi;A,\ell,(X_s),\pi,\rho)$ is $\cS(\Pi^D;\ell,A,(\lam_s),\pi^{-1},\rho^{-1})$, by the identity on the elements of $\Pi$ and $H_s\leftrightarrow K_s$, $Q\leftrightarrow M$. Consequently any two distinct lines of $\cS$ are concurrent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Read the six rules in $\Pi^D$ with the frame $(\ell,A,(\lam_s))$. Rule~\ref{r:plane} deletes the flags $(\lam_s,X_s)$ and adds $\lam_t\I X_{\rho^{-1}(t)}$, which is the rotation $X_u\I\lam_{\rho(u)}$ of $\cS$. Rule~\ref{r:hat} gives a new element incident with every element incident with $\lam_s$ other than $A$: this is $K_s$; rule~\ref{r:hatline} gives one incident with every element incident with $X_s$ other than $\ell$: this is $H_s$. Rule~\ref{r:M} gives a new element incident with the lines through $A$ other than the $\lam_t$, $t\in T$, and with all $K_s$: this is $Q$; likewise~\ref{r:Q} gives $M$. Rule~\ref{r:links} gives $\ell\I H_a$, $K_a\I A$ and $K_t\I H_{\pi^{-1}(t)}$, which are the links of $\cS$. $\Pi^D$ is a projective plane of order $q$. For the three hats $\pi^{-1}=\pi$ and $\rho^{-1}=\rho=\mathrm{id}$. For the four hats $\pi^{-1}=\rho^{-1}=\sigma^{-1}$ is again a $3$-cycle on $T$; the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:points} uses only that $\pi=\rho$ is a $3$-cycle on $T$ (renaming the labels $1$ and $c$ turns $\sigma^{-1}$ into $\sigma$). So Lemma~\ref{lem:points}, applied to the dual structure, says that any two lines of $\cS$ are concurrent.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hats}
Let $\Pi$ be a projective plane of order $q\ge 3$. The three-hat structure of \cite{AL20} and the four-hat structure are incidence structures with all degrees $q+1$ or $q+2$ and diameter $3$, with $2(q^2+q+5)$ and $2(q^2+q+6)$ elements. Adding a perfect matching of non-incident pairs between the deficient points and lines makes them $(q+2)$-regular. Hence $n_b(q+2,3)\ge 2(q^2+q+6)$ for every prime power $q\ge 3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Degrees and counts are Lemma~\ref{lem:deg}. Every element is incident with something. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:points} and~\ref{lem:dual} any two points are collinear and any two lines concurrent, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(a) the diameter is at most $3$; it is $3$ because $A\nI\ell$.
\end{proof}

Only the axioms of a projective plane are used, so the theorem holds in non-Desarguesian planes too (we checked the two nearfield planes of order $9$). In the range of the tables, $q=9,11,13$ give $192$, $276$, $376$ at $\Delta=11,13,15$, two more than \cite{AL20}.

\subsection{From three hats to four, step by step}\label{ssec:steps}
Start from the three hats with labels $0,1,a$ and choose a fourth point $X_c$ of $\ell$, different from $X_0,X_1,X_a$. The four-hat structure arises by exactly these changes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=2.5em]
\item add the point $H_c$, on the $q$ lines through $X_c$ other than $\ell$, on $M$ and on $K_1$; add the line $K_c$, through the $q$ points of $\lam_c$ other than $A$, through $Q$ and through $H_0$;
\item delete the flags $(X_0,\lam_0)$, $(X_c,\lam_c)$, $(X_1,\lam_1)$, and add $(X_0,\lam_c)$, $(X_c,\lam_1)$, $(X_1,\lam_0)$;
\item delete $X_c\I M$ and $Q\I\lam_c$ (now $X_c$ is a $T$-point and $\lam_c$ a $T$-line);
\item delete the link $H_0\I K_1$; it is replaced by $H_0\I K_c$ and $H_c\I K_1$ from (1), while $H_1\I K_0$ is kept.
\end{enumerate}
In terms of the links, the cycle $0\to1\to0$ of $\pi=(0\ 1)$ becomes the cycle $0\to c\to 1\to 0$ of $\sigma$. The four-hat structure has $2q+1$ more flags than the three-hat structure: $2(q+2)$ at $H_c$ and $K_c$, three from the rotation, minus six. (Checked by computer against the coordinate versions for $q=4,5,7$ and every choice of $a,c$.)

\section{Diameter 4: clones and shadow lines in generalized quadrangles}\label{sec:d4}

A \emph{generalized quadrangle} of order $(s,t)$ is an incidence structure in which every line has $s+1$ points, every point is on $t+1$ lines, and
\begin{center}
for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ there is \emph{exactly one} point of $L$ collinear with $p$.
\end{center}
It has $(s+1)(st+1)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)$ lines, and its incidence graph has diameter $4$ and girth $8$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(b), an incidence structure has diameter at most $4$ as soon as for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ there is \emph{at least one} point of $L$ collinear with $p$. All constructions in this section only add points, lines and incidences to a quadrangle; nothing is deleted or moved, so no pair of points stops being collinear.

This is the first difference with diameter $3$. In a projective plane two cloned points (new points on all lines through an old point) would overload the line joining them, and the hats of Section~\ref{sec:d3} are clones of the points $X_s$ that avoid the common line $\ell$. In a quadrangle, a clone of a point inherits the covering property from its point at once; what has to be arranged is only that every line receives the same number of clones, and this is what an $m$-ovoid is.

\subsection{Clones and shadow lines}\label{ssec:clones}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized quadrangle of order $(s,t)$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item A \emph{clone} of a point $p$ is a new point incident with exactly the lines of $\cS$ through $p$.
\item A \emph{shadow line} over a line $L$ is a new line that contains, for each point $p$ of $L$, exactly one point \emph{over} $p$: either $p$ itself or one of the clones of $p$, called the \emph{stand-in} for $p$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Clones and shadow lines]\label{def:clones}
Let $w$ assign to every point $p$ a number $w(p)\ge 0$, and let $\cM$ be a finite list of lines of $\cS$ (repetitions allowed). The structure $\cS[w,\cM]$ has the points and lines of $\cS$, $w(p)$ clones of each point $p$, and one shadow line over each entry of $\cM$, with a chosen stand-in for each of its points. Its incidences are those of $\cS$, those of the clones, and those of the shadow lines with their points (Figure~\ref{fig:clones}).
\end{definition}

\emph{What happens to the combinatorics.} No incidence is deleted, so every pair of old points that was collinear stays collinear. A point $p$ and its clones are pairwise collinear, on $t+1$ common lines; two lines through $p$ now meet in $1+w(p)$ points; a shadow line whose stand-ins are all original has all its $s+1$ points on its line (and the same points, if that line carries no clones). So the structure is no longer a partial linear space, but these coincidences are harmless. Two old points that were not collinear stay non-collinear: they have no common old line, a shadow line over $L$ contains only points over points of $L$, and an old point is over itself only.

\emph{Why the diameter is $4$.} Write $\bar x$ for the old point under a point $x$ ($\bar x=x$ for an old point, $\bar x=p$ for a clone of $p$) and $\bar Y$ for the old line under a line $Y$. Every point $x$ is on every old line through $\bar x$. Let $(x,Y)$ be an anti-flag.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $Y$ old. Then $\bar x\nI Y$; let $r$ be the point of $Y$ collinear with $\bar x$, on the line $N$. Then $x\I N\I r\I Y$.
\item $Y$ a shadow line, $\bar x\nI\bar Y$. Let $r$ be the point of $\bar Y$ collinear with $\bar x$, on the line $N$, and $r^*$ the stand-in for $r$ on $Y$. Then $x\I N\I r^*\I Y$, as $r^*$ is on every old line through $r$.
\item $Y$ a shadow line, $\bar x\I\bar Y$. Take a point $r\ne\bar x$ of $\bar Y$ and its stand-in $r^*$: then $x\I\bar Y\I r^*\I Y$.
\end{itemize}
So in every case some point of $Y$ is collinear with $x$, and the diameter is at most $4$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(b)); it is $4$ because non-collinear old points remain.

\emph{Degrees.} An old line $L$ has $s+1+\sum_{p\I L}w(p)$ points. A shadow line has $s+1$ points. An old point or a clone is on $t+1$ old lines plus the shadow lines for which it is the stand-in. This proves:

\begin{theorem}[Theorem B]\label{thm:B}
For every generalized quadrangle $\cS$ of order $(s,t)$, every $w$, $\cM$ and choice of stand-ins, $\cS[w,\cM]$ has diameter $4$, $(s+1)(st+1)+\sum_pw(p)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)+|\cM|$ lines. Its maximum degree is at most $\Delta$ if and only if $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$, every line of $\cS$ carries clones of total number at most $\Delta-s-1$, and every point \textup{(}old or clone\textup{)} is the stand-in for at most $\Delta-t-1$ entries of $\cM$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Exact weights: \texorpdfstring{$m$}{m}-ovoids and covers}\label{ssec:weights}
A \emph{weighted $m$-ovoid} is a weight $w$ on the points such that every line carries total weight exactly $m$; equivalently, a multiset of points meeting every line in exactly $m$ points, counted with multiplicity. It is an \emph{$m$-ovoid} if it is a set, and an \emph{ovoid} if moreover $m=1$ (a set of points meeting every line once). Dually, a \emph{weighted $m$-cover} is a multiset of lines with exactly $m$ members through every point, and a \emph{spread} is a set of lines partitioning the points.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:exact}
Let $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$, let $w$ be a weighted $(\Delta-s-1)$-ovoid and $\cM$ a weighted $(\Delta-t-1)$-cover, with every stand-in original. Then every old element of $\cS[w,\cM]$ has degree exactly $\Delta$, every clone $t+1$ and every shadow line $s+1$, and there are $2\Delta(st+1)$ elements in all. Joining the clones to the shadow lines by a suitable set of anti-flags makes the structure $\Delta$-regular, with diameter still $4$.
\end{corollary}

(Counting flags gives $\sum_pw(p)=(\Delta-s-1)(st+1)$ and $|\cM|=(\Delta-t-1)(st+1)$. No clone is on a shadow line, so all clone--shadow pairs are anti-flags, and the missing incidences can be added in any balanced way, for instance cyclically; \path{POLISHED.md}, Corollary~3.3.)

So for diameter $4$ the whole question is: \emph{which quadrangles carry weighted $m$-ovoids and covers for the right $m$?} Three answers give the lab's values.

\subsection{Theorem A: two elliptic quadrics in the Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle}\label{ssec:A}
Let $q$ be odd and $\Delta=q+2$. The quadrangle is $\mathrm{AS}(q)$, of order $(q-1,q+1)$; then $\Delta-s-1=2$ and $\Delta-t-1=0$, so we need a \emph{weighted $2$-ovoid of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ and no shadow lines}. Every point of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ is already on $t+1=q+2$ lines, so the clones have full degree and the result is $(q+2)$-regular.

\emph{The quadrangle.} $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ is Payne's derived quadrangle of the symplectic quadrangle $W(q)$ at a point $x$ \cite{P71,PT}, and its definition is combinatorial. For points $u,v$ write $\{u,v\}^\perp$ for the set of points collinear with both, and $\{u,v\}^{\perp\perp}$ for the set of points collinear with every point of $\{u,v\}^\perp$; in $W(q)$, for $u,v$ not collinear, $\{u,v\}^{\perp\perp}$ has $q+1$ points (every point of $W(q)$ is regular). Then:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item the points of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ are the $q^3$ points of $W(q)$ not collinear with $x$ (and different from $x$);
\item its lines are (i) the lines of $W(q)$ not through $x$, each without its unique point collinear with $x$, and (ii) the sets $\{x,y\}^{\perp\perp}\setminus\{x\}$, $y$ not collinear with $x$ (the \emph{hyperbolic lines}).
\end{itemize}
Every line has $q$ points, every point is on $q+2$ lines, and the $q^2$ hyperbolic lines form a spread of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$.

\emph{The configuration used.} Two sets $\cE_1,\cE_2$ of $q^2$ points of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(E\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item each hyperbolic line meets $\cE_1$ in exactly one point and $\cE_2$ in exactly one point;
\item every other line of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ meets $\cE_1$ and $\cE_2$ in exactly two points in total \textup{(}a point of $\cE_1\cap\cE_2$ counted twice\textup{)}.
\end{enumerate}
Then $w=\mathbf 1_{\cE_1}+\mathbf 1_{\cE_2}$ is a weighted $2$-ovoid. Such a pair exists for every odd $q$: two elliptic quadrics of $\mathrm{PG}(3,q)$ through $x$, both tangent to the plane of points collinear with $x$, in a suitable relative position; they meet in $x$ and in one point $e$ of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ (\path{POLISHED.md}, Theorem~4.1; the proof is a discriminant computation, and a line of type (i) meets the two quadrics in $1+\chi$ and $1-\chi$ points for some $\chi\in\{-1,0,1\}$). So:

\begin{center}
\emph{Clone every point of $\cE_1$ and every point of $\cE_2$ once} (so $e$ gets two clones).
\end{center}
Each line of $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ gains exactly two clones, each clone is on the $q+2$ lines of its point, nothing else changes, and Theorem~\ref{thm:B} gives a $(q+2)$-regular structure of diameter $4$ with $q^3+q^2(q+2)+2q^2=2q^2(q+2)$ elements. At $q=9,11,13$: $1782$, $3146$, $5070$. Weights are needed: $\mathrm{AS}(3)$ has no $2$-ovoid and $\mathrm{AS}(5)$ no $m$-ovoid for $m\le4$ (integer programs, \path{POLISHED.md} \S4). And nothing more can be added at $\Delta=q+2$: points are full, and every line has room for exactly two clones.

\subsection{Proposition C: a \texorpdfstring{$2$}{2}-ovoid and a spread of \texorpdfstring{$W(q)$}{W(q)}}\label{ssec:C}
Let $q$ be odd and $\Delta=q+3$ in $W(q)$, of order $(q,q)$; now $\Delta-s-1=\Delta-t-1=2$. The configuration used is a \emph{$2$-ovoid} $\cO$ of $W(q)$ (a set of $2(q^2+1)$ points meeting every line in exactly two points) and a \emph{spread} $\Sigma$ ($q^2+1$ lines partitioning the points). The construction:
\begin{center}
\emph{Clone every point of $\cO$ once, and put two shadow lines over every line of $\Sigma$.}
\end{center}
Every old line gains two clones, every old point is on two shadow lines (those over its spread line), clones and shadow lines have degree $q+1$, and Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} completes it to a $(q+3)$-regular structure of diameter $4$ with $2(q^2+1)(q+3)$ elements; one explicit completion joins the $i$-th clone to the shadow lines $2i$ and $2i+1$ (indices modulo $2(q^2+1)$, in any numbering). At $q=13$ this is $5440$ at $\Delta=16$. $2$-ovoids of $W(q)$, $q$ odd, exist: the union of two suitable classes of Ebert's partition of $\mathrm{PG}(3,q)$ into elliptic quadrics (orbits of a Singer subgroup) is one (\path{POLISHED.md}, Proposition~5.1); see also \cite{BLP09}. Spreads of $W(q)$ exist for every $q$.

\subsection{Even \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}, and all degrees}\label{ssec:even4}
For even $q$, $W(q)$ has both an ovoid $\cO$ and a spread $\Sigma$ \cite{PT}. With $j$ clones of every point of $\cO$ and $j$ shadow lines over every line of $\Sigma$, Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} gives a $(q+1+j)$-regular structure of diameter $4$ with $2(q+1+j)(q^2+1)$ elements. Combining the three cases (ovoid and spread for even $q$; $j/2$ copies of a $2$-ovoid and $j$ copies of a spread for odd $q$ and even $j$; $\mathrm{AS}(q)$ with $(\Delta-q)/2$ copies of the weighted $2$-ovoid of Theorem~A and $\Delta-q-2$ copies of the hyperbolic spread for odd $q$ and odd $j$) gives $n_b(\Delta,4)\ge 2q^2\Delta$ for every prime power $q\le\Delta-1$ (\path{POLISHED.md}, Corollary~5.3).

\subsection{For comparison: Delorme's doubling, and stand-ins}\label{ssec:delorme4}
\emph{Delorme's construction} behind the 2013 values \cite{D85a} takes a spread $\Sigma$ of $W(q)$ and a line $v\in\Sigma$. Each line of $\Sigma$ other than $v$ gets one copy with the same points (a shadow line with all stand-ins original); each point of $v$ gets one copy, which is on the $q$ lines through the point other than $v$; and $v$ gets three copies, one through the original points of $v$ and two through the copies. All degrees are at most $q+2$, the diameter is $4$, and there are $2(q+1)(q^2+1)+q^2+q+4$ elements. It is a doubling in Delorme's sense: the copied set is the spread with the points of one of its lines, at two levels. Theorem~\ref{thm:B} is not a doubling: since a clone is on \emph{every} old line through its point, no condition on the shape of the copied set is needed, and whole $m$-ovoids can be cloned.

\emph{Stand-ins: the $(7,4)$ example.} In the quadrangle $Q(4,5)$ (order $(5,5)$) take an ovoid $\cO$ and clone each of its $26$ points once: every line gains one clone and has degree $7$, every old point has degree $6$. Shadow lines can now be added as long as every point serves at most one of them: a point off $\cO$ has no clone and can be on at most one shadow line, while a point of $\cO$ can be on two, standing in for itself on one and letting its clone stand in on the other. An integer program finds $21$ such shadow lines (four points of $\cO$ lie on the underlying lines of two of them, and stand in for themselves on one and through their clone on the other), giving $2\cdot6\cdot26+26+21=359$ elements, maximum degree $7$ and diameter $4$ (\path{POLISHED.md}, Appendix~A). In this family at most $q^2-q+2$ shadow lines are possible (\path{POLISHED.md}, Remark~3.5).

\section{Diameter 6: a spread and whiskers in a generalized hexagon}\label{sec:d6}

Let $\Gamma$ be a generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$: every line has $q+1$ points, every point is on $q+1$ lines, any two elements are at distance at most $6$ in the incidence graph, and there are no circuits of length less than $12$. It has $N=(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)$ points and $N$ lines. Elements at distance $6$ are \emph{opposite}; two elements at distance less than $6$ are joined by a unique shortest path. A point and a line are at distance $1$, $3$ or $5$. In the constructions, $\Gamma$ is the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$ \cite{T59,VM98}, but only in the choice of the special configurations (\S\ref{ssec:herm}).

By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(c) the diameter stays at most $6$ if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point of $L$ is within distance $4$ of $p$. Here the new elements come from a doubling, and the argument is by ``sheets''.

\subsection{Doubling with levels}\label{ssec:levels}
This is Delorme's operation \cite[\S7]{D85a}, in incidence language. Choose sets of elements (points and lines mixed)
\[
\cA_0\supseteq\cA_1\supseteq\cA_2\supseteq\cdots,
\]
and call $\max\{k: x\in\cA_k\}$ the \emph{level} of $x$ (level $-1$ if $x\notin\cA_0$). Fix $m\ge2$.

\begin{definition}[Doubling with levels]\label{def:levels}
Replace every element $x$ of level $k$ by $m^{k+1}$ \emph{versions} $x^w$, one for each word $w$ of length $k+1$ over $\{1,\dots,m\}$; an element of level $-1$ keeps one version, itself. Declare $x^w\I y^u$ if and only if $x\I y$ in $\Gamma$ and one of the words $w,u$ is a prefix of the other.
\end{definition}

For $m=2$ and a single level this says: every element $x$ of $\cA_0$ gets a twin $x'$; inside $\cA_0$ originals see originals and twins see twins; an element outside $\cA_0$ sees both $x$ and $x'$ whenever it saw $x$. So an outside element gains one incidence for each element of $\cA_0$ it is incident with, and an element of $\cA_0$ gains nothing. With more levels, a version $x^w$ of level $k$ sees one version of each neighbour of level at most $k$ (the one whose word is the corresponding prefix of $w$) and all $m$ versions of a neighbour of level $k+1$ extending $w$.

\emph{Sheets.} For an infinite word $\omega$, the versions whose words are prefixes of $\omega$ form a copy of $\Gamma$ (every element has exactly one version in it, and any two of these versions have comparable words). Two sheets share exactly the versions indexed by their common prefixes: all elements of level $-1$, then the versions of level-$0$ elements if the words begin with the same letter, and so on (Figure~\ref{fig:sheets}). So if $r$ is the highest level, the doubled structure is $m^{r+1}$ copies of $\Gamma$ glued along a tree.

Two conditions control the degrees and one the diameter:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(L\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em,start=0]
\item every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_0$;
\item for every $i\ge0$, every element incident with an element of $\cA_{i+1}$ lies in $\cA_i$, and every element of $\cA_i\setminus\cA_{i+1}$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_{i+1}$;
\end{enumerate}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(C)},leftmargin=3em]
\item any two elements $x,y$ in one connected component of $\cA_0$ (possibly $x=y$) admit an element $z\notin\cA_0$ with $d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le 6$.
\end{enumerate}
Under (L0) and (L1) every version has degree at most $q+1+(m-1)$: it sees one version of each neighbour, except of the one neighbour (if any) one level up, of which it sees $m$. Under (C) the diameter stays at most $6$: two versions in a common sheet are within $6$; two versions in no common sheet have incomparable words, so both elements are in $\cA_0$; if they lie in different components of $\cA_0$, a shortest path between them in $\Gamma$ passes through an element of level $-1$, which belongs to every sheet, and the two halves of the path can be lifted into the two sheets; if they lie in the same component, (C) gives such an element. In a generalized hexagon, (C) holds if and only if every connected component of $\cA_0$ is a tree of diameter at most $4$ (the hat lemma, \path{POLISHED.md} Lemma~1.3: a path of length $5$ inside $\cA_0$ is the unique shortest path between its ends, so no detour through an outside element is short enough). Finally there are $(m-1)\sum_k m^k|\cA_k|$ new elements.

Counting flags between $\cA_0$ and its complement gives a ceiling: if $\Gamma$ has order $(q,q)$, (L0) holds, every component of $\cA_0$ is a tree and $c$ is the number of components, then $q|\cA_0|+2c\le 2N$, with equality exactly when every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_0$ (\path{geometry.tex}, Proposition~3.6).

\subsection{The configuration: a spread, its distinguished line, and whiskers}\label{ssec:herm}
\emph{A spread} of $\Gamma$ is a set $\Sigma$ of $q^3+1$ pairwise opposite lines. We use two properties: the lines of $\Sigma$ have disjoint point sets, so every point of the set $\PS$ of points on lines of $\Sigma$ is on exactly one line of $\Sigma$ ($|\PS|=(q+1)(q^3+1)$); and every line not in $\Sigma$ meets exactly one line of $\Sigma$ (a line meeting two lines of $\Sigma$ would put them at distance $4$, and counting gives all $N$ lines). $H(q)$ has spreads for every $q$: the \emph{Hermitian spreads}, the lines of $H(q)$ in a hyperplane that meets the quadric of $H(q)$ in an elliptic quadric \cite{T80}.

Fix a line $v\in\Sigma$. The points split by their distance from $v$: the $q+1$ points of $v$ (distance $1$), the $q^2(q+1)$ further points on the $q(q+1)$ lines meeting $v$ (distance $3$), and the rest (distance $5$). A point of $\PS$ not on $v$ is at distance $5$ from $v$, because its line in $\Sigma$ is opposite $v$. Call a point \emph{free} if it is not in $\PS$ and is at distance $5$ from $v$; there are
\[
N-(q+1)-q^2(q+1)-q^3(q+1)=q^3(q^2-1)
\]
free points.

\emph{Whiskers.} Let $h$ be a line opposite $v$ and not in $\Sigma$. It meets exactly one line $n$ of $\Sigma$, with $n\ne v$, in one point; its other $q$ points are free (they are not in $\PS$, since $h$ meets only one line of $\Sigma$ and only once, and every point of a line opposite $v$ is at distance $5$ from $v$). Such a line is a \emph{whisker}, hanging from the point $h\cap n$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:whiskers}
A \emph{whisker set} is a set $W$ of whiskers such that no free point is on two lines of $W$. It is \emph{exact} if every free point is on exactly one line of $W$; then $|W|=q^3(q^2-1)/q=q^2(q^2-1)$, the largest possible.
\end{definition}

Exact whisker sets exist in $H(q)$ for every $q$: for odd $q$ an explicit one is cut out by $q^2-1$ further Hermitian spreads through $v$ (with the spread itself, $q^2$ spreads pairwise meeting only in $v$), with one whisker per free point chosen by an affine condition (\path{POLISHED.md}, Theorem~6.5); for even $q\ge4$ a characteristic-$2$ variant (\path{POLISHED.md}, Theorem~6.14; checked here for $q=4$); for $q=2$ one found by computer.

\subsection{The levels, and what each element becomes}\label{ssec:thmD}
Put
\[
\cA_2=\{v\},\qquad \cA_1=\{v\}\cup\{\text{points of }v\}\cup\Sigma,\qquad \cA_0=\cA_1\cup\PS\cup\{\text{lines meeting }v\}\cup W .
\]
In words: $v$ is at level $2$; the other spread lines and the points of $v$ at level $1$; the other points of $\PS$, the $q(q+1)$ lines meeting $v$ and the whiskers at level $0$; everything else at level $-1$.

\emph{The conditions.} (L1): the elements incident with $v$ are its points, which are at level $1$, and each sees only $v$ at level $2$; the elements incident with a level-$1$ element are the points of $\PS$ and the lines meeting $v$, all in $\cA_0$, and each of them sees exactly one level-$1$ element (its spread line, or its point on $v$), while a whisker sees none. (L0) holds, and with $W$ exact it holds with equality: a point at distance $3$ from $v$ is on exactly one line meeting $v$ (two would close a circuit of length at most $6$) and on no whisker; a free point is on exactly one whisker; a line outside $\cA_0$ meets exactly one line of $\Sigma$, hence contains exactly one point of $\PS$, and no other element of $\cA_0$. (C): the connected components of $\cA_0$ are
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item the \emph{ball} of radius $2$ about $v$: the line $v$, its $q+1$ points and the $q(q+1)$ lines through them;
\item for each of the $q^3$ lines $n\in \Sigma\setminus\{v\}$, the \emph{broom} of $n$: the line $n$, its $q+1$ points, and the whiskers hanging from them.
\end{itemize}
Both are trees of diameter $4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:components}). Since $W$ is exact, the ceiling $q|\cA_0|+2(q^3+1)=2N$ is attained: no family of this kind can double more.

\subsection{Theorem R: a regular version}\label{ssec:thmR}
The doubled structure has more lines than points, $(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$ more, and its deficient elements are the versions of spread lines and whiskers. Two observations make it regular.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Repeated lines.} For $n\in \Sigma\setminus\{v\}$ and a letter $i$, the $m$ versions $n^{i1},\dots,n^{im}$ have the same points (the $p^i$, $p$ on $n$); likewise the versions $v^{ij1},\dots,v^{ijm}$. Deleting a line that has a twin with the same points changes no distance between the remaining elements (a shortest path through it may use the twin). Delete $(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$ such lines, at most $m-1$ from each class of $m$, so that points and lines are equal in number.
\item \emph{Completion.} Now every element has degree between $q+1$ and $q+m$, and the deficits on the two sides have the same sum; this sum is large compared with $q+m$, and a swapping argument (\path{POLISHED.md}, Lemma~7.2) gives a set of anti-flags, one for each missing incidence, whose addition makes the structure $(q+m)$-regular. Adding flags does not increase distances.
\end{itemize}
The result has $2(q+1)\bigl(q^4+q^2+1+(m-1)(q^3+m+1)\bigr)$ elements: $9348$, $147500$, $386328$, $866068$ at $q=5,9,11,13$, $m=2$, and $927724$ at $q=13$, $m=3$. The completing anti-flags are not given by a rule; only their existence is proved.

\section{Diameter 7: copies of a hexagon joined by matchings, freed sets and hubs}\label{sec:d7}

(Theorems A, B, C of this section are those of \path{D7_HEXAGON.md}, not those of Section~\ref{sec:d4}.)

Let $\Gamma$ be a generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$ with $N$ points and $N$ lines, as in Section~\ref{sec:d6}, and $\Gamma^D$ its dual (points and lines exchanged). Two facts are used repeatedly: a point and a line of $\Gamma$ are at distance at most $5$, two elements of the same kind at most $6$; and for any two elements $x,y$ there is an element $z$ at distance $2$ from $x$ (a point collinear with $x$ if $x$ is a point, a line concurrent with $x$ if $x$ is a line) with $d(z,y)\le 3$ if $x,y$ are of different kinds and $d(z,y)\le 4$ if they are of the same kind (walk two steps from $x$ along a shortest path to $y$; if $x$ and $y$ are incident, take for $z$ another element incident with $y$; if $x=y$, any $z$).

\subsection{Level structures}\label{ssec:levelgraph}
Fix $r\ge1$ and take $2r+1$ copies $\Gamma_0,\dots,\Gamma_{2r}$ of $\Gamma$, indexed modulo $2r+1$. For each copy $j$ and each $k=1,\dots,r$ (a \emph{transition} from copy $j$ to copy $j+k$) choose a bijection $M_{j,k}$ from the points of $\Gamma_j$ to the lines of $\Gamma_{j+k}$.

\begin{definition}[Level structure]\label{def:levelgraph}
Its points are the points of all copies, its lines the lines of all copies, and its incidences are those inside each copy together with
\[
p\I M_{j,k}(p)\qquad\text{for every point $p$ of $\Gamma_j$ and every $k=1,\dots,r$.}
\]
\end{definition}

So every point gains $r$ lines, one in each of the next $r$ copies, and every line gains $r$ points, one from each of the previous $r$ copies: the structure is $(q+1+r)$-regular with $(2r+1)\cdot 2N$ elements. (For each $\Delta$, the largest order $(2r+1)\cdot 2N$ obtainable this way with $\Delta=q+1+r$ is the 2013 table value at diameter $7$, which the CombinatoricsWiki table credits to Delorme, G\'omez and Quisquater.) A copy may also be a copy of the dual $\Gamma^D$; its ``points'' are then lines of $\Gamma$. A transition from a copy of $\Gamma$ to a copy of $\Gamma^D$ is called \emph{friendly}: it may join each point $p$ of $\Gamma$ to $p$ itself, now a line of the dual copy.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem A of \path{D7_HEXAGON.md}]\label{thm:levelA}
For any choice of the bijections, the level structure has diameter at most $7$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Two elements of one copy are within $6$. Any two copies differ by $\pm k$ with $1\le k\le r$, since there are $2r+1$ of them; so take $u$ in copy $j$, $v$ in copy $j+k$, and $M=M_{j,k}$. If $u$ is a point: $u$, then $M(u)$, then $v$ inside copy $j+k$: at most $1+6$. If $u$ and $v$ are lines: $u$ to the point $M^{-1}(v)$ inside copy $j$, then $v$: at most $5+1$. If $u$ is a line and $v$ a point: choose a line $b$ through $v$; $u$ to the point $M^{-1}(b)$, then $b$, then $v$: at most $5+1+1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Freeing incidences and adding hubs}\label{ssec:hubs}
To add elements, incidences must be freed. For each transition $t=(j,k)$ choose a set $R_t$ of points of copy $j$, the \emph{freed} points, and:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=2.5em]
\item delete the incidences $p\I M_t(p)$, $p\in R_t$; each deletion leaves a free incidence (a \emph{slot}) at the point $p$ of copy $j$ and one at the line $M_t(p)$ of copy $j+k$;
\item add new lines, the \emph{line-hubs}, each incident with one freed point in \emph{every} copy (taking a slot there), and new points, the \emph{point-hubs}, each incident with one freed line in every copy;
\item add incidences between line-hubs and point-hubs (a \emph{hub graph}), at most $q-r$ at each hub.
\end{enumerate}
All degrees stay at most $q+1+r$ (a hub has $2r+1$ incidences with the copies), and there are $(2r+1)\cdot2N$ elements plus the hubs. Figure~\ref{fig:hubs} shows the case $r=1$.

\emph{What the hubs need.} A line-hub is incident with a point in every copy, which is within $6$ of everything in its copy: so a hub is within $7$ of every element of every copy. A line-hub and a point-hub are within $7$ through copy $0$ (a point and a line of one copy are within $5$). Two hubs of the same kind must be within $6$, through the hub graph or through the copies. \emph{What the deletion breaks.} Only pairs in two copies joined by a transition can be affected, and only through that transition. Call a transition \emph{repaired} if after the deletion every element of copy $j$ is still within $7$ of every element of copy $j+k$ using only these two copies and the remaining incidences of the transition. Then:

\begin{theorem}[Assembly, \path{D7_HEXAGON.md} Theorem 3.4]\label{thm:assembly}
If every transition is repaired and any two hubs of the same kind are within distance $6$, the structure has diameter at most $7$.
\end{theorem}

So everything reduces to a property of one matching and one freed set, and to finding a hub graph. Two answers to the first question:

\subsection{Matchings that extend to an isomorphism: segment sets}\label{ssec:segment}
Suppose the transition's bijection is the restriction of an isomorphism $\psi$ from copy $j$ to copy $j+k$ that sends points to lines: the identity of a friendly transition, or a \emph{duality} of $\Gamma$ (an incidence-preserving bijection exchanging points and lines; $H(q)$ has one exactly when $q$ is a power of $3$).

\begin{definition}\label{def:segment}
A set $F$ of points is a \emph{segment set} if for every $p\in F$ the other points of $F$ collinear with $p$ all lie on one line through $p$; equivalently, there are no $p,p',p''\in F$ with $p,p'$ collinear, $p',p''$ collinear, and $p,p''$ not collinear (a path of two collinearities that \emph{turns} at $p'$).
\end{definition}

Non-collinear sets are segment sets, and so is any subset of the point set of a set of pairwise opposite lines. A segment set has at most $(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points, with equality exactly for the point set of a spread (\path{D7_HEXAGON.md}, Proposition~1.4: count the lines meeting $F$ in exactly one point).

\begin{theorem}[Theorem B of \path{D7_HEXAGON.md}]\label{thm:segment}
Such a transition is repaired if and only if its freed set is a segment set.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Why.} The key fact is that for any two elements $u,v$ of $\Gamma$ there is a point $p\notin F$ with $d(u,p)+d(p,v)\le 6$. If some shortest path from $u$ to $v$ has a point outside $F$, take it. Otherwise all points on all shortest paths lie in $F$; a shortest path with three consecutive points would turn, so the remaining cases are short ($u$ and $v$ equal, collinear, incident, or at distance $3$ or $4$), and each is settled by stepping off the line that carries the other points of $F$ (\path{D7_HEXAGON.md}, Lemma~4.1, seven cases). Now let $u$ be in copy $j$ and $v$ in copy $j+k$, and apply this to $u$ and $\psi^{-1}(v)$ with $F=R_t$: the route $u$, then $p$, then the line $\psi(p)$ in copy $j+k$, then $v$ avoids the deleted incidences and has length $d(u,p)+1+d(\psi(p),v)=d(u,p)+1+d(p,\psi^{-1}(v))\le 7$, since $\psi$ preserves distances. Conversely, if $p,p',p''\in F$ turn at $p'$ and $u\ne m'$ is a line through $p''$ ($m'$ the line through $p'$ and $p''$), the only routes of length at most $7$ from $u$ in copy $j$ to $\psi(p)$ in copy $j+k$ would cross at $p$, $p'$ or $p''$, all freed.

So with a duality ($q=3^h$) the whole point set of a spread can be freed on every transition, and no larger set.

\subsection{Arbitrary matchings: local dualities}\label{ssec:localdual}
For a transition whose bijection $M$ (points of copy $j$ to lines of copy $j+k$) is not an isomorphism, it is enough that $M$ looks like a duality near the freed points.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem C of \path{D7_HEXAGON.md}]\label{thm:localdual}
The transition is repaired if the freed set $R$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(H\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item no two points of $R$ are collinear;
\item no two lines of $M(R)$ are concurrent;
\item for every $x\in R$, $M$ maps the points collinear with $x$ onto the lines concurrent with $M(x)$ \textup{(}$M$ is a \emph{local duality} at $x$\textup{)}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $u$ be in copy $j$ and $v$ in copy $j+k$; distances are taken inside each copy.
(1) $u$ a point not in $R$: $u$, $M(u)$, then $v$: at most $1+6$.
(2) $u$ a line, $v$ a point: the $q+1$ lines through $v$ are pairwise concurrent, so by (H2) at most one is in $M(R)$; take another, $b$, and $a=M^{-1}(b)\notin R$: $u$ to $a$, then $b$, then $v$: at most $5+1+1$.
(3) $u$ a line, $v$ a line not in $M(R)$: $u$ to $M^{-1}(v)$, then $v$: at most $5+1$.
(4) $u$ a line, $v=M(x)$ with $x\in R$: there is a point $z$ collinear with $x$ with $d(z,u)\le 3$; $z\notin R$ by (H1), and $M(z)$ is concurrent with $v$ by (H3): $v$ to $M(z)$, then $z$, then $u$: at most $2+1+3$.
(5) $u=x\in R$, $v$ a point: there is a line $w$ concurrent with $M(x)$ with $d(w,v)\le3$; by (H3) $w=M(z)$ with $z$ collinear with $x$, and $z\notin R$: $x$ to $z$, then $w$, then $v$: at most $2+1+3$.
(6) $u=x\in R$, $v$ a line: the same, with $d(w,v)\le 4$: at most $2+1+4$.
\end{proof}

Such matchings are easy to build when $R$ is a set of pairwise opposite points (a \emph{partial ovoid}) and $M(R)$ is to be a set of pairwise opposite lines (a \emph{partial spread}) of the same size: then the sets of points collinear with different points of $R$ are disjoint, and so are the sets of lines concurrent with different lines of $M(R)$; send each $x\in R$ to a chosen line, the $q(q+1)$ points collinear with $x$ bijectively onto the $q(q+1)$ lines concurrent with it, and the remaining points arbitrarily onto the remaining lines (\path{D7_HEXAGON.md}, Proposition~5.2 and Corollary~5.3(a)). More generally $R$ may contain pairs at distance $4$ if $M(R)$ has the same pattern of pairs at distance $4$ (``mirrored sets''). For example the $q^2+1$ points of an elliptic quadric section $Q^-(3,q)$ of the quadric of $H(q)$ are pairwise opposite, for every $q$, and the Hermitian spread supplies pairwise opposite lines.

With classical ingredients only (the $q^2+1$ points of an elliptic quadric section, $q^2+1$ lines of a Hermitian spread, and an explicit hub graph built from a projective plane with pendant trees) the same theorems give $n_b(q+2,7)\ge 6N+2(q^2+1)$ for every $q\ge5$ (\path{D7_HEXAGON.md}, Corollary~9.1).

\section{Diameter 5: level structures of generalized quadrangles, freed sets and hubs}\label{sec:d5}

(Theorems A, B, C, E and the other numbered statements cited in this section are those of \path{D5_QUADRANGLE.md}.)

This section follows the plan of Section~\ref{sec:d7} one step lower: copies of a generalized quadrangle joined by bijections have diameter $5$, and freeing some of the joining incidences makes room for new points and lines. The difference is that at diameter $5$ there is an exact criterion for which incidences can be freed, and it is much more restrictive than at diameter~$7$.

Let $\cQ$ be a generalized quadrangle of order $(q,q)$ (Section~\ref{sec:d4}): every line has $q+1$ points, every point is on $q+1$ lines, and for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ exactly one point of $L$ is collinear with $p$. It has $N=(q+1)(q^2+1)$ points and $N$ lines, and its dual $\cQ^D$ (points and lines exchanged) is again a quadrangle of order $(q,q)$. Three facts are used throughout:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(Q\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item two distinct points are collinear or have a common collinear point, so they are at distance $2$ or $4$; a point and a line are at distance $1$ or $3$; dually for lines;
\item a line not through $p$ has exactly one point collinear with $p$, and a line through $p$ has $q$ of them; dually, a point not on $L$ is on exactly one line concurrent with $L$;
\item for every point $p$ and every line $L$, some point collinear with $p$ lies on $L$ (by (Q2), or another point of $L$ if $p\I L$); dually.
\end{enumerate}
The quadrangles used are $W(q)$ and $Q(4,q)$ \cite{PT}, and $Q(4,q)$ is isomorphic to the dual of $W(q)$ \cite[3.2.1]{PT}. As in Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}, distances in a structure of diameter $5$ can be read off from pairs of the same kind:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:diam5}
An incidence structure in which every element is incident with something has diameter at most $5$ if and only if any two points, and any two lines, are at distance at most $4$; that is, any two points are collinear or both collinear with a third point, and dually.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Same-kind distances are even and point--line distances odd. If $p$ is a point and $L$ a line, and $p'$ is a point of $L$, then $d(p,L)\le d(p,p')+1\le 5$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Level structures}\label{ssec:levels5}
Fix $r\ge1$ and take $2r+1$ copies $\cQ_0,\dots,\cQ_{2r}$, indexed modulo $2r+1$, each a copy of $\cQ$ (\emph{type} $H$) or of $\cQ^D$ (\emph{type} $D$). For an element $x$ of $\cQ$ write $x_j$ for its copy in $\cQ_j$; if $\cQ_j$ has type $D$, a line $\ell$ of $\cQ$ gives a point $\ell_j$ and a point $p$ gives a line $p_j$. The \emph{transitions} are the pairs $j\to j+k$, $1\le k\le r$; every two copies are joined by exactly one transition. (Any orientation of the pairs in which every copy has $r$ outgoing transitions would do; all layouts below use this one.) For each transition $t=(j\to j')$ choose a bijection $M_t$ from the points of $\cQ_j$ to the lines of $\cQ_{j'}$.

\begin{definition}[Level structure]\label{def:levels5}
Its points are the points of all copies, its lines the lines of all copies, and its incidences are those inside each copy together with $p\I M_t(p)$ for every transition $t=(j\to j')$ and every point $p$ of $\cQ_j$.
\end{definition}

Every point gains $r$ lines and every line $r$ points, so the structure is $(q+1+r)$-regular with $(2r+1)\cdot 2N$ elements. A transition is \emph{friendly} if the two copies have different types. Then a point $p_j$ of $\cQ_j$ and the line $p_{j'}$ of $\cQ_{j'}$ come from the same element $p$ of $\cQ$, and $M_t$ may be the \emph{identity} $p_j\mapsto p_{j'}$. For $q=7,8,9,9,9,11$ and $2r+1=5,5,5,7,9,7$ the orders $(2r+1)\cdot2N$ are the values of the 2013 table in the cells $(10,5)$--$(15,5)$, which the CombinatoricsWiki \cite{LPP} credits to Delorme, G\'omez and Quisquater \cite{DGQ}. We could not find that paper; the cell $(16,5)$, credited to them as well, is not of this form, so we can say only that the orders coincide.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem A]\label{thm:levelA5}
For any types and bijections, the level structure has diameter at most $5$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam5} it suffices to join two points, and two lines, by a path of length at most $4$. Inside one copy this is (Q1). Otherwise take $u$ in $\cQ_j$ and $v$ in $\cQ_{j'}$ with a transition $M$ from $j$ to $j'$ (one of the two directions is a transition). Two points: $u$, the line $M(u)$, then $v$ inside $\cQ_{j'}$, at most $1+3$. Two lines: $u$ to the point $M^{-1}(v)$ inside $\cQ_j$, then $v$, at most $3+1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Freeing incidences and adding hubs}\label{ssec:hubs5}
For each transition $t=(j\to j')$ choose a set $R_t$ of points of $\cQ_j$, the \emph{freed} points, and:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=2.5em]
\item delete the incidences $p\I M_t(p)$, $p\in R_t$; each deletion leaves a free incidence (a \emph{slot}) at the point $p$ of $\cQ_j$ and one at the line $M_t(p)$ of $\cQ_{j'}$;
\item add $K$ new lines, the \emph{line-hubs}, each incident with one freed point in \emph{every} copy (taking a slot there), and $K'$ new points, the \emph{point-hubs}, each incident with one freed line in every copy; every slot is taken at most once;
\item add incidences between line-hubs and point-hubs (the \emph{hub graph} $Y$), at most $q-r$ at each hub.
\end{enumerate}
Copy $j$ has $\alpha_j=\sum_{t\text{ out of }j}|R_t|$ free point slots and $\beta_j=\sum_{t\text{ into }j}|R_t|$ free line slots, so $K\le\min_j\alpha_j$ and $K'\le\min_j\beta_j$. All degrees stay at most $q+1+r$ (a hub has $2r+1$ incidences with the copies), and there are $(2r+1)\cdot 2N+K+K'$ elements. This is the operation of \S\ref{ssec:hubs} with quadrangles in place of hexagons.

Call the transition $t$ \emph{repaired} if after the deletion every point of $\cQ_j$ is within distance $4$ of every point of $\cQ_{j'}$, and every line of $\cQ_j$ of every line of $\cQ_{j'}$, using only these two copies and the remaining incidences of $t$. A shortest such path uses exactly one incidence of $t$: it uses an odd number of them, and with three it would contain a path between two distinct lines of $\cQ_{j'}$ and one between two distinct points of $\cQ_j$, each of length at least $2$, so its length would be at least $3+2+2=7$. So $t$ is repaired if and only if for all $u$ in $\cQ_j$ and $v$ in $\cQ_{j'}$ of the same kind there is a point $a\notin R_t$ of $\cQ_j$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repair5}
d(u,a)+1+d(M_t(a),v)\le 4 .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Assembly; Theorems 3.5, 3.6]\label{thm:assembly5}
If every transition is repaired, any two line-hubs and any two point-hubs are within distance $4$, and every hub is on at most $q-r$ hub incidences, the structure has diameter at most $5$ and maximum degree at most $q+1+r$; so $n_b(q+1+r,5)\ge(2r+1)\cdot 2N+K+K'$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam5}. Two old points or two old lines: inside a copy by (Q1), in different copies through their transition. A line-hub $h$ and an old line $v$ of $\cQ_j$: $h$ contains a point of $\cQ_j$, which is within $3$ of $v$; dually for point-hubs and old points. Two hubs of the same kind: by hypothesis.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis on the hubs holds, for instance, if $Y$ alone has all same-kind distances at most $4$ (\S\ref{ssec:hubgraphs}). The hypothesis on the transitions is a property of one bijection and one freed set, and the next theorem decides it exactly.

\subsection{The exact criterion}\label{ssec:thmE}

\begin{theorem}[Theorem E]\label{thm:E}
Let $t=(j\to j')$ be a transition with bijection $M$ and freed set $R$. Then $t$ is repaired if and only if for every $x\in R$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(E\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em,start=2]
\item every point of $\cQ_{j'}$ lies on $M(a)$ for some point $a\notin R$ collinear with $x$;
\item every line of $\cQ_j$ contains a point $a\notin R$ such that $M(a)$ and $M(x)$ are concurrent.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We check \eqref{eq:repair5}. Distances between points of one copy are even, between a point and a line odd. \emph{Two points $u\in\cQ_j$, $v\in\cQ_{j'}$}: we need $d(u,a)+d(M(a),v)\le3$, with $d(u,a)\in\{0,2,4\}$ and $d(M(a),v)\in\{1,3\}$. If $u\notin R$, take $a=u$: $d(M(u),v)\le 3$ by (Q1). If $u=x\in R$, then $a\ne x$, so we need $d(x,a)=2$ and $d(M(a),v)=1$: a point $a\notin R$ collinear with $x$ with $v$ on $M(a)$. For all $v$ this is (E2). \emph{Two lines $u\in\cQ_j$, $v\in\cQ_{j'}$}: now $d(u,a)\in\{1,3\}$ and $d(M(a),v)\in\{0,2,4\}$, so the possibilities are $(1,0)$, $(1,2)$, $(3,0)$. If $v=M(b)$ with $b\notin R$, take $a=b$: $d(u,b)\le3$. If $v=M(x)$ with $x\in R$, then $M(a)=v$ is impossible, so we need $a\I u$, $a\notin R$, and $M(a)$ concurrent with $M(x)$. For all $u$ this is (E3).
\end{proof}

(The note's proof also goes through point--line pairs and a condition (E1), which (E2) implies; with Lemma~\ref{lem:diam5} these are not needed. The program \path{check_d5.py} compares (E2), (E3) with a direct computation of distances on $336$ transitions in $W(q)$ and $Q(4,q)$, $q=2,3$.)

\subsection{Transitions given by an isomorphism: partial ovoids}\label{ssec:thmB5}
Call a transition \emph{automorphic} if $M$ is the restriction of an isomorphism from $\cQ_j$ to the dual of $\cQ_{j'}$: the identity of a friendly transition, or a duality of $\cQ$ (an incidence-preserving bijection exchanging points and lines; $W(q)$ has one exactly when $q$ is even \cite[3.2.1]{PT}).

\begin{theorem}[Theorem B]\label{thm:B5}
An automorphic transition is repaired if and only if its freed points are pairwise non-collinear (a \emph{partial ovoid} of $\cQ_j$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An isomorphism preserves incidence and collinearity, so (E2) and (E3) for $x$ both say: every line of $\cQ_j$ contains a point $a\notin R$ collinear with $x$. A line through $x$ has $q$ points collinear with $x$, a line $\ell$ not through $x$ exactly one, $p_\ell$ (Q2). If no freed point is collinear with $x$, every line is covered. If a freed point $y$ is collinear with $x$, every line $\ell\ne xy$ through $y$ has $p_\ell=y$ and is not covered (Figure~\ref{fig:collinearpair}).
\end{proof}

At diameter $7$ the corresponding sets were the segment sets (Theorem~\ref{thm:segment}), which may contain collinear pairs; at diameter $5$ a single collinear pair breaks the transition. In $W(q)$ and $Q(4,q)$ the largest partial ovoids are the ovoids, $q^2+1$ points meeting every line once; $Q(4,q)$ has ovoids for every $q$, $W(q)$ only for $q$ even \cite[3.4.1, 3.4.2]{PT}. Dually, freed lines on a friendly transition from a copy of $\cQ^D$ must be pairwise non-concurrent lines of $\cQ$ (a \emph{partial spread}).

\subsection{Local dualities, and why they stop at two points for odd \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}\label{ssec:thmC5}
For a transition between two copies of the same type, $M$ goes from the points to the lines of $\cQ$ itself, and for $Q(4,q)$ with $q$ odd there is no duality. As at diameter~$7$ (\S\ref{ssec:localdual}), it is enough that $M$ looks like a duality near the freed points.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem C]\label{thm:C5}
The transition is repaired if
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(H\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item no two freed points are collinear, and
\end{enumerate}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(H\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em,start=3,topsep=0pt]
\item for every $x\in R$, $M$ maps the points collinear with $x$ onto the lines concurrent with $M(x)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(E2): by (H1) no point collinear with $x$ is freed, so by (H3) the lines $M(a)$, $a\notin R$ collinear with $x$, are all the lines concurrent with $M(x)$, and every point lies on one of them (Q3). (E3): the points $a$ with $M(a)$ concurrent with $M(x)$ are exactly the points collinear with $x$, none of them freed, and every line contains one (Q3).
\end{proof}

The diameter-$7$ condition (H2), that the lines $M(R)$ be pairwise non-concurrent, follows here: for $x\ne y$ in $R$, $M$ maps the $q+1$ points collinear with both onto the lines concurrent with both $M(x)$ and $M(y)$, and two concurrent lines have only $q-1$ such lines (those through their common point).

\emph{Two points always work} (Proposition~6.4). Let $x,y$ be non-collinear points and $\ell,m$ non-concurrent lines. The sets $\{x,y\}$, $\{x,y\}^\perp$ (the $q+1$ points collinear with both), the $q^2-1$ other points collinear with $x$, and the $q^2-1$ other points collinear with $y$ are disjoint, and the same holds for $\{\ell,m\}$ and the lines concurrent with them, with the same sizes. A bijection sending $x\mapsto\ell$, $y\mapsto m$ and each piece onto the corresponding piece, extended arbitrarily, satisfies (H1) and (H3) for $R=\{x,y\}$.

\emph{Not three} (Corollary~6.2). Three pairwise non-collinear points form a \emph{triad}, and its \emph{centres} are the points collinear with all three; dually for lines. Under (H1) and (H3), $M$ maps the centres of a triad $\{x,y,z\}\subseteq R$ onto the lines concurrent with all of $M(x),M(y),M(z)$, which are pairwise non-concurrent by the remark above; so a triad and its image have the same number of centres. For $q$ odd the points of $Q(4,q)$ are \emph{antiregular} (every triad has $0$ or $2$ centres) and the points of $W(q)$ \emph{regular} (every triad has $1$ or $q+1$ centres) \cite[1.3.6, 3.3.1]{PT}. A triad of lines of $Q(4,q)$ is a triad of points of $W(q)$, so the two counts never agree, and a local duality of $Q(4,q)$ (or of $W(q)$) has at most two freed points. At diameter $7$ local dualities carried $81$ to $768$ freed points per transition; here they carry two.

\subsection{Freeing concurrent lines in pairs, and the hub rule}\label{ssec:relaxed}
On a friendly transition the freed set must be a partial ovoid or a partial spread (Theorem~\ref{thm:B5}), and in the layouts of \S\ref{ssec:layouts} the number of freed lines on the transitions from type $D$ to type $H$ limits everything. Some concurrent pairs can be freed if the hubs are placed with care.

Let $\cQ=Q(4,q)$, let $\cQ_j$ have type $D$ and $\cQ_{j'}$ type $H$, and let $M$ be the identity: the point $\ell_j$ of $\cQ_j$ ($\ell$ a line of $\cQ$) is matched with the line $\ell_{j'}$. Free a set $T$ of lines of $\cQ$ in which every line meets at most one other line of $T$, its \emph{partner}.

\begin{proposition}[Proposition 7.1]\label{prop:badpairs}
After the deletion, the pairs of the same kind in $\cQ_j\cup\cQ_{j'}$ at distance more than $4$ are exactly the following, for every partner pair $\ell,m$ with common point $z$ and every point $p\ne z$ of $m$: the points $\ell_j$ and $p_{j'}$; the lines $p_j$ and $\ell_{j'}$; and the same with $\ell$ and $m$ exchanged. That is $4q$ pairs for each partner pair.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a line $\ell\in T$ without partner, (E2) and (E3) hold as in Theorem~\ref{thm:B5}. Let $\ell$ have partner $m$. (E2) for $\ell$ asks that every point $v$ of $\cQ$ lie on a line $a\notin T$ meeting $\ell$, $a\ne\ell$. A point $v$ on $\ell$ is on $q$ such lines, at most one of them in $T$. A point $v$ off $\ell$ is on exactly one line meeting $\ell$ (Q2), and this line is in $T$ only if it is $m$, that is, $v\in m\setminus\{z\}$. (E3) for $\ell$ asks the same of every point $u$ of $\cQ$, read as the line $u_j$ of $\cQ_j$. Following the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:E}, the failures are exactly the stated pairs.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Proposition 7.2: the hub rule]\label{prop:hubrule}
Suppose that, for every line $\ell\in T$ with partner $m$, the slot at the line $\ell_{j'}$ is taken by a point-hub $g_\ell$ whose line in $\cQ_j$ is $o_j$ for some point $o$ of $m$. Then all the pairs of Proposition~\ref{prop:badpairs} are within distance $4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $p\in m\setminus\{z\}$: $\ell_{j'}$, $g_\ell$, $o_j$, $m_j$, $p_j$ is a path, since $o$ and $p$ are points of $m$, so that the lines $o_j$ and $p_j$ of $\cQ_j$ pass through its point $m_j$. And $\ell_j$, $o'_j$, $g_m$, $m_{j'}$, $p_{j'}$ is a path, where $o'$ is the point of $\ell$ at which the hub $g_m$ of the partner sits in $\cQ_j$.
\end{proof}

\emph{The hubs exist} (Lemma~7.3). In the seven-copy layout of \S\ref{ssec:layouts} the transitions from type $H$ to type $D$ free an ovoid $O$ of $Q(4,q)$, which meets every line exactly once; put $g_\ell$, in the two copies of type $D$, at the point $o=O\cap m$ of the partner. A point of $O$ has three free slots in one of these copies and two in the other, and at most two lines of $T$ pass through a point, so at most two hubs ask for it. The requests can be met whatever the common points of the partners are, even when a partner pair meets at a point of $O$ (then both hubs sit there). One point-hub per line of $T$ suffices, because in this layout the transitions out of the two copies of type $D$ end in different copies.

How large can $T$ be? Read in $W(q)$, $T$ is a set of points each collinear with at most one other. A single point uses its $q+1$ lines, a pair $2q+1$ lines, and no line is shared, so $|T|\le 2(q+1)(q^2+1)/(2q+1)$, which is $86$ at $q=9$; for partial ovoids of $W(q)$, $q$ odd, Tallini's bound is $q^2-q+1$ \cite{Ta91}. Computer searches found $66$ at $q=9$ and $89$ at $q=11$ (\path{D5_QUADRANGLE.md}, \S7).

\subsection{Layouts and slot counts}\label{ssec:layouts}
Over $\cQ=Q(4,q)$, $q$ odd, let every transition from type $H$ to type $D$ be the identity and free $a$ points, every transition from $D$ to $H$ the identity and free $b$ lines, and every transition between copies of the same type free $c$ points with a local duality ($c=2$). Counting the transitions out of and into each copy (Propositions~8.1--8.3):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item five copies $HHDHD$ ($r=2$): $\min_j\alpha_j=\min_j\beta_j=b+c$;
\item seven copies $HHHDHHD$ ($r=3$): $\alpha=(a+2c,a+2c,a+2c,2b+c,a+2c,a+2c,3b)$, $\beta=(b+2c,b+2c,b+2c,3a,b+2c,b+2c,2a+c)$;
\item nine copies $HHHHDHHHD$ ($r=4$): $\min_j\alpha_j=\min(a+3c,3b+c)$, $\min_j\beta_j=b+3c$.
\end{itemize}
(Over all regular tournaments on seven vertices and all typings, the best value of $\min\alpha+\min\beta$ is $\min(a+b+4c,3b+3c)$, \path{D5_QUADRANGLE.md}, \S8.) For $q$ even all copies have type $H$ over $W(q)$, every transition is a duality restricted to the points, and every transition frees an ovoid of $W(q)$, so $\alpha_j=\beta_j=r(q^2+1)$.

\subsection{Hub graphs}\label{ssec:hubgraphs}
The hub incidences form a bipartite graph $Y$ whose two sides are the line-hubs and the point-hubs. It needs all degrees at most $\delta=q-r$ and any two vertices of the same side within distance $4$ (Lemma~9.1). Either side may play either role below. Explicit ones:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(Y\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em,start=0]
\item $K,K'\le\delta$: the complete bipartite graph;
\item the incidence graph of a projective plane of order $s$, $s+1\le\delta$, with up to $\delta-s-1$ new vertices of degree one attached to every vertex: $s^2+s+1\le K,K'\le(s^2+s+1)(\delta-s)$. Two old vertices of one side are at distance $2$. A new vertex on $c$ and an old vertex $x$ of its side are within $1+3$, since $c$ and $x$ are on different sides; two new vertices of one side hang on vertices at distance at most $2$;
\item the incidence graph of a quadrangle of order $(s,s)$, $s+1\le\delta$, with up to $\delta-s-1$ new vertices of degree one attached to each of the $s+1$ lines through a fixed point and to each of the $s+1$ points of a fixed line: $N(s)\le K,K'\le N(s)+(s+1)(\delta-s-1)$;
\item the incidence graph of the level structure of Theorem~\ref{thm:levelA5} built on a quadrangle of order $(s,s)$ with $2r'+1$ copies, $s+1+r'\le\delta$: $K=K'=(2r'+1)N(s)$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Diameter 8: G\'omez's construction on generalized hexagons, and a labelling for each copy}\label{sec:d8}

(Theorems 1--4 and the other numbered statements cited in this section are those of \path{OMEGA_NOTE.md}.)

Six entries of the 2013 table at diameter $8$, the cells $(10,8)$, $(11,8)$, $(12,8)$, $(14,8)$, $(15,8)$ and $(16,8)$, are the orders of the compound graphs $\Omega H_q$ of J.~G\'omez \cite[\S2.9.3]{G86} for $q=7,8,9,11,11,13$; the table credits them to Delorme, G\'omez and Quisquater \cite{DGQ}. \emph{The construction is G\'omez's}: the copies of the hexagon, the wiring of the new elements (his Figures 2.21a,b), the order, the degree, and the claim that the diameter is at most $D_1+2=8$, which the thesis states without proof. \emph{What follows is ours}: the construction in incidence terms; a proof that, as defined, it has diameter at least $9$ (\S\ref{ssec:omegafail}); and the attempt to \emph{repair} it by giving every copy its own labelling (\S\ref{ssec:relabel}). The repair works at $q=2$ and $q=3$, with labellings found by computer; for every $q\ge7$ it provably fails for all labellings that respect the geometry; for other labellings it is open. So, unlike Sections~\ref{sec:d3}--\ref{sec:d5}, this section describes a construction that does not reach the diameter claimed for it, and why.

Let $\Gamma$ be a generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$, with $N=(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)$ points and $N$ lines (Section~\ref{sec:d6}); for the table, $\Gamma$ is the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$ \cite{T59,VM98}. Recall: any two elements are at distance at most $6$, there are no circuits of length less than $12$, and elements at distance $6$ are \emph{opposite}. By the argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}, an incidence structure has diameter at most $8$ if and only if every point is within distance $7$ of every line. Two counts in $\Gamma$ are used.

\begin{lemma}[Lemmas 1.4 and 1.6]\label{lem:hexcounts}
Let $t$ be an element of $\Gamma$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item For $1\le i\le5$ there are $(q+1)q^{i-1}$ elements at distance $i$ from $t$, and $q^5$ elements opposite $t$.
\item Let $z$ be an element of the same kind as $t$. Among the elements opposite $t$, exactly $q^4$ are within distance $4$ of $z$ if $z\ne t$, and none if $z=t$; exactly $q^2$ are within distance $2$ of $z$ if $d(t,z)\in\{4,6\}$, and none if $d(t,z)\le 2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) A walk that never steps straight back is a shortest path as long as its length is at most $6$, and it is the only shortest path between its ends when its length is at most $5$; otherwise two of them would close a circuit shorter than $12$. Such walks have $q+1$ choices for the first step and $q$ for each later one. (b) If $d(t,u)=k$ with $1\le k\le5$, exactly one element incident with $u$ is at distance $k-1$ from $t$ and the other $q$ at distance $k+1$; if $k=6$, all $q+1$ are at distance $5$. Follow the distance to $t$ along the walks of length at most $4$ from $z$ that never step straight back. For example, if $d(t,z)=2$, a walk of length $4$ ends opposite $t$ exactly when every step goes away from $t$, which happens for $q\cdot q^3$ walks, and the walks of length $2$ or $0$ end at distance at most $4$ from $t$. The cases $d(t,z)=4$ and $6$ are the same bookkeeping (\path{OMEGA_NOTE.md}, Lemma~1.6).
\end{proof}

\subsection{G\'omez's construction}\label{ssec:gomez}
Put $\alpha=\lfloor(q+3)/2\rfloor$, so that $2\alpha$ is $q+3$ or $q+2$. Take $5\alpha$ copies $\Gamma_c$ of $\Gamma$, numbered $c=5B+i$ with a \emph{block} $B\in\{0,\dots,\alpha-1\}$ and a \emph{position} $i\in\mathbb{Z}_5$; each block has a \emph{type}, $a$ or $b$. The thesis gives the points of $\Gamma$ a set of $N$ labels, and the lines the same set of labels, and uses \emph{the same labels in every copy}. We write $x_c$ for the copy in $\Gamma_c$ of an element $x$ of $\Gamma$.

The new elements come in ten \emph{classes}: an \emph{unbarred} class $k$ and a \emph{barred} class $\bar k$ for each $k\in\mathbb{Z}_5$. Each class has one new element for each label, and each class \emph{meets} $2\alpha$ of the copies, two in every block:

In every copy it meets, the new element of a class with label $t$ is incident with exactly one element of that copy: the point labelled $t$ or the line labelled $t$. Which of the two is fixed per class and copy by the figures of the thesis, which draw each copy with an outer and an inner dot and do not say which dot is the points; the natural reading (\emph{reading~2}) is that the unbarred classes sit on points and the barred classes on lines, in every copy. \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item the points and lines of all $5\alpha$ copies, with their incidences;
\item for every point $p$ of $\Gamma$ and every $k\in\mathbb{Z}_5$, a new line $p^{(k)}$ through the copies $p_c$ of $p$ in the $2\alpha$ copies $\Gamma_c$ met by $k$;
\item for every line $L$ of $\Gamma$ and every $k\in\mathbb{Z}_5$, a new point $L^{(\bar k)}$ on the copies $L_c$ of $L$ in the $2\alpha$ copies met by $\bar k$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What happens: the diameter is at least \texorpdfstring{$9$}{9}}\label{ssec:omegafail}
A new element is incident only with copies of \emph{one} element of $\Gamma$. So passing through it lets a path change copy, but not move in $\Gamma$. This is the whole difficulty.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 1 and Corollary 3.1]\label{thm:projection}
In reading~$2$, for every new element $x$ with label $t$ \textup{(}the point $p$ for $p^{(k)}$, the line $L$ for $L^{(\bar k)}$\textup{)}, every copy $\Gamma_{c'}$ that $x$ misses and every element $v$ of $\Gamma$ opposite $t$, the distance from $x$ to $v_{c'}$ is at least $9$. So the structure has diameter at least $9$, for every $q$ and every choice of block types.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Send every element $y_c$ of a copy to $y$, and every new element to its label. Along a path starting at $x$, an incidence inside a copy is sent to an incidence of $\Gamma$, and a passage copy, new element, copy uses two incidences and is sent to a single element (the new element is incident only with copies of its label). A path from $x$ to $v_{c'}$ starts with an incidence from $x$ to a copy of $t$ in a copy that $x$ meets; to reach $\Gamma_{c'}$ it must pass through at least one more new element. Removing the passages, its image is a walk in $\Gamma$ from $t$ to $v$, of length at least $6$. So the path has length at least $1+6+2=9$ (Figure~\ref{fig:projection}).
\end{proof}

The argument uses only that every new element meets copies of a single element and misses some copy; it holds for any bipartite graph in place of the hexagon, and it is formalised in Lean (\path{OMEGA_NOTE.md}, \S3). In reading~1 a new element meets the point labelled $t$ in some copies and the line labelled $t$ in others, so its image may jump between the two; a count of the possible images still leaves elements far away:

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 2]\label{thm:twolabels}
Let $q\ge7$. In every reading, for every block types and every pairing of point labels with line labels, every new element has, in every copy it misses, at least $q^5-5q^4-8q^3-12q^2-16q-10>0$ elements at distance at least $9$.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Why.} Let $x$ have label $t$, and let $z$ be the element labelled $t$ of the kind of the elements of $\Gamma_{c'}$ that $x$ must reach within $7$. A path of length at most $7$ from $x$ into $\Gamma_{c'}$ passes through $1$, $2$ or $3$ new elements and uses at most $4$ incidences inside copies; each passage keeps the image or switches it between the point and the line with the same label. Counting the possible images with Lemma~\ref{lem:hexcounts}(a): at most $A+B+1$ elements opposite $z$ are reached, where $A=\sum_{e_1+e_2\in\{0,2,4\}}s_{e_1}s_{e_2}=5q^4+8q^3+6q^2+4q+2$ and $B=\sum_{e_1+e_2+e_3\le2}s_{e_1}s_{e_2}s_{e_3}=6q^2+12q+7$, with $s_0=1$, $s_i=(q+1)q^{i-1}$. For $q\ge7$ this is less than $q^5$ (for example $15{,}459<16{,}807$ at $q=7$).

By computer, the diameter is exactly $9$ for $q=2,3,5,7,9$ with both block types (for the patterns and readings computed), the eccentricity of a new element is $9$ at the orders of all six table entries, and with a single block type the diameter is $10$ (\path{OMEGA_NOTE.md}, C2, C3; checked again here for $q=2,3$).

\subsection{The repair: a labelling for each copy}\label{ssec:relabel}
The thesis uses one labelling for all copies. The obvious repair keeps the wiring and gives every copy $c$ its own labelling: bijections $P_c$ and $L_c$ from the labels to the points and lines of $\Gamma$, with the new element of label $t$ incident, in copy $c$, with $P_c(t)$ or $L_c(t)$. Degrees and counts do not change. A passage from copy $c$ to copy $d$ through a new element of class $Y$ now moves the image in $\Gamma$ by the \emph{transfer} $P_dP_c^{-1}$ (or $L_dL_c^{-1}$, $L_dP_c^{-1}$, $P_dL_c^{-1}$, according to what $Y$ sits on in $c$ and $d$), and Theorem~\ref{thm:projection} no longer applies. What is known:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{At $q=2$ and $q=3$ the repair works} (C7). Labellings found by annealing in the lab's table audit give diameter $8$: at $q=2$, block types $ab$, $1{,}890$ elements, old elements of degree $5$ and new ones of degree $4$; at $q=3$, block types $aba$, reading~$1$, $14{,}560$ elements, $6$-regular. We rebuilt the $q=3$ structure from the audit's files and checked its diameter (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). These are far below the table at $(5,8)$ and $(6,8)$; they only show that a suitable labelling can reach $D_1+2$. We have no description of these labellings.
\item \emph{Relabelling a copy by an automorphism of $\Gamma$ changes nothing} (Lemma~5.2): if $P_c$ and $L_c$ are $P_0$ and $L_0$ followed by one collineation $g_c$ of $\Gamma$, then $y_c\mapsto (g_c^{-1}(y))_c$ is an isomorphism onto G\'omez's structure.
\item \emph{Geometric labellings cannot work for $q\ge7$} (Theorems 3, 4). Call a labelling \emph{geometric} if every transfer is induced by an automorphism of $\Gamma$: a collineation, or a duality, which exists for the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$ only when $q$ is a power of $3$ \cite{VM98}. For $q\ge7$, every geometric labelling, every reading, every choice of which side each class sits on, and every choice of block types, the diameter is at least $9$.
\item \emph{Open}: labellings that are not geometric, for every $q\ge4$ (searches at $q=4,5$ found none of diameter $8$); labellings chosen separately for each pair (class, copy); other wirings.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Why geometric labellings fail} (combinatorially, for $q\ge8$). After relabelling each copy by an automorphism (which changes nothing), the transfers of one bar, the \emph{point bar}, are the identity and those of the other, the \emph{line bar}, are collineations (Lemma~5.3; for $q$ not a power of $3$ every class sits on one kind in all copies it meets). Collineations preserve distances. A path of length at most $7$ from a new element $x$ of the point bar with label $t$ into a copy $\Gamma_{c'}$ it misses passes through one, two or three new elements; a passage through the point bar keeps the image, a passage through the line bar applies a collineation $\lambda$ that depends only on the two copies. So every point of $\Gamma_{c'}$ within distance $7$ of $x$ lies in $B(t,4)$, in a ball $B(\lambda(t),4)$ for each copy $c_1$ met by $x$ that shares a class of the line bar with $c'$ (one passage through the line bar), or in one of at most $m_2$ balls of radius $2$ (two passages; Lemma~5.4). Call $(x,c')$ \emph{starved} if only $\alpha$ of the $2\alpha$ copies met by $x$ share a class of the other bar with $c'$; for every choice of block types starved pairs exist (Lemma~5.6, a property of the wiring alone). By Lemma~\ref{lem:hexcounts}(b) the balls then contain at most $\alpha q^4+m_2q^2$ of the $q^5$ elements opposite $t$, and a count of the two-passage words gives $m_2\le 8\alpha^2-1$ in general and $m_2\le140$, $201$ for $\alpha=5,6$. So for $q\ge8$ every new element of a starved pair has at least $q^5-\alpha q^4-m_2q^2$ elements at distance at least $9$ in the missed copy: at least $3{,}328$, $3{,}402$, $11{,}253$, $56{,}446$ for $q=8,9,11,13$ (by hand for $q\ge10$; for $q=8,9$ the bounds on $m_2$ are counts done by computer, which we repeated). At $q=7$ the count is negative ($16{,}807-5\cdot2{,}401-140\cdot49<0$), and the proof uses the embedding of $H(7)$ in the quadric $Q(6,7)$: the overlaps of the balls, and a character-sum bound on how many points a collineation without fixed points moves to points perpendicular to them (\path{OMEGA_NOTE.md}, \S5.5). We do not reproduce it here.

\subsection{What can be claimed}\label{ssec:omegaclaim}
The six entries $(10,8)$, $(11,8)$, $(12,8)$, $(14,8)$, $(15,8)$, $(16,8)$ are the orders of G\'omez's structures $\Omega H_q$, $q=7,8,9,11,11,13$. As defined in the thesis (every copy of the hexagon carries the same labels, and a new element with label $t$ is incident only with elements labelled $t$), they have diameter at least $9$ for each of these $q$, under every reading of the figures and every pairing of point labels with line labels (Theorems~\ref{thm:projection} and~\ref{thm:twolabels}, and computation). The paper the table cites was never published, as far as we could find, so these cells have no checkable structure of diameter $8$. Giving each copy its own labelling repairs the construction at $q=2,3$; for $q\ge7$ no geometric labelling does. (For $q=7,8,11,13$, reading~$1$ with both block types has no geometric labelling at all, since a transfer from points to lines would be a duality; there Theorem~\ref{thm:twolabels} covers the thesis labelling.) We do not claim that no structure with these parameters exists, nor that no labelling works.
