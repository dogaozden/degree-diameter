\title[Doubling in generalized polygons]{Doubling in generalized polygons:\\ large incidence structures of diameter $3$, $4$ and $6$}
\author{Draft for discussion --- authors to be settled}
\date{20 October 2026; revised 25 October 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Let $n_b(\Delta,D)$ be the largest number of elements (points plus lines) of an incidence structure in which every point lies on at most $\Delta$ lines, every line has at most $\Delta$ points, and any two elements are at distance at most $D$ in the incidence graph. For $D\in\{3,4,6\}$ and $\Delta-1$ a prime power the classical generalized $D$-gons of order $(\Delta-1,\Delta-1)$ attain the Moore bound. For the remaining degrees we adjoin new points and new lines to a classical generalized polygon. The main tool is Delorme's \emph{doubling} of a set of elements, with levels, which we recast as an operation on incidence structures. We show that in a generalized $n$-gon, doubling a set of elements keeps the diameter at most $n$ if and only if every connected component of that set is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$.
For $D=3$ we add four \emph{hats} to any projective plane of order $q\ge 3$ and obtain $2(q^2+q+6)$ elements at $\Delta=q+2$; in $\PG(2,q)$ a correlation completes it to a regular structure.
For $D=4$ we show that two elliptic quadrics through a point $x$ of $\Wq(q)$ form a weighted $2$-ovoid of $\AS(q)=P(\Wq(q),x)$, $q$ odd, which gives $(q+2)$-regular structures with $2q^2(q+2)$ elements; we also treat copies of lines (``shadows'') and give a short proof that two orbits of a Singer subgroup form a $2$-ovoid of $\Wq(q)$. For $q$ even the quadrangle $\Qf(4,q)$ has an ovoid and a spread, and doubling them gives $(q+2)$-regular structures with $2(q^2+1)(q+2)$ elements.
For $D=6$ we extend Delorme's doubling of the split Cayley hexagon $\Hq(q)$ along a Hermitian spread by an explicit set of $q^2(q^2-1)$ lines that meets the counting bound, for every odd $q$ and every even $q\ge4$ (for $q=2$ by a set found by computer), and obtain $2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+2q^4+4q^3+4q+10$ elements at $\Delta=q+2$, together with regular versions. The even case needs different hyperplanes and a different formula; it gives no new record in the range $\Delta\le16$. The main theorems are also formalised in Lean~4 (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $\cS=(\cP,\cL,\I)$ be a finite incidence structure, and let $\Gamma(\cS)$ be its incidence graph. Every bipartite graph with a chosen bipartition is the incidence graph of an incidence structure. So the \emph{bipartite degree/diameter problem} asks for the largest number of elements of an incidence structure with given maximum degree and given diameter. Write
\[
n_b(\Delta,D)=\max\bigl\{\,|\cP|+|\cL| \;:\; \Delta(\cS)\le\Delta,\ \diam\cS\le D\,\bigr\},
\]
where $\Delta(\cS)$ is the largest number of lines on a point or points on a line, and $\diam\cS$ is the diameter of $\Gamma(\cS)$.
Counting the elements at distance $0,1,\dots,D-1$ from a flag gives the bipartite Moore bound
\[
n_b(\Delta,D)\le M_b(\Delta,D)=2\bigl(1+(\Delta-1)+\dots+(\Delta-1)^{D-1}\bigr).
\]
Equality holds exactly for the incidence graphs of generalized $D$-gons of order $(\Delta-1,\Delta-1)$. By the theorem of Feit and Higman \cite{FH64} this forces $D\in\{3,4,6\}$ when $\Delta\ge 3$ (an octagon of order $(s,s)$ would need $2s^2$ to be a square). Projective planes, generalized quadrangles and generalized hexagons of order $(q,q)$ are known when $q$ is a prime power, and for no other $q$. So the open cases with $D\in\{3,4,6\}$ are the degrees $\Delta$ for which $\Delta-1$ is not a prime power. In the usual range $\Delta\le16$ these are $\Delta\in\{7,11,13,15,16\}$.

Our constructions all have the same shape. We start from a classical generalized polygon $\cS$ of order $(q,q)$ or $(q-1,q+1)$, with $q+1<\Delta$, and adjoin new points and new lines with prescribed incidences. The incidence graph appears only at the end, as the object whose order and diameter we count.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Results.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Doubling} (Section~\ref{sec:doubling}). We describe Delorme's doubling with levels \cite{D85a,D85b} as an operation $\cS\mapsto\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ on incidence structures, prove Delorme's diameter theorem and its converse (Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme}), and show (Lemma~\ref{lem:hat} and Corollary~\ref{cor:hatiff}) that in a generalized $n$-gon the doubling has diameter at most $n$ if and only if each component of the doubled set is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$. This explains why diameter~$3$ needs extra ``patch'' incidences and why diameters $4$ and $6$ do not.
\item \emph{Diameter $3$} (Section~\ref{sec:d3}). A fourth hat in any projective plane of order $q\ge3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fourhat}), completing the construction of Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20}: $n_b(q+2,3)\ge 2(q^2+q+6)$, with a regular version in $\PG(2,q)$.
\item \emph{Diameter $4$} (Section~\ref{sec:d4}). A weighted $2$-ovoid of $\AS(q)$ formed by two elliptic quadrics (Theorem~\ref{thm:A}); clones and shadow lines in any generalized quadrangle (Theorem~\ref{thm:B}); a $2$-ovoid of $\Wq(q)$ from a Singer subgroup (Proposition~\ref{prop:C}); regular structures of every degree $\Delta\ge q+1$ and every prime power $q$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:B2}; for even $q$ it uses an ovoid and a spread of $\Qf(4,q)$, see Section~\ref{sec:even4}).
\item \emph{Diameter $6$} (Section~\ref{sec:d6}). Delorme's doubling of $\Hq(q)$ along a Hermitian spread, extended by an explicit exact set of $q^2(q^2-1)$ ``whisker'' lines for every odd $q$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}), and regular versions (Theorem~\ref{thm:R}).
\item \emph{Even $q$} (Section~\ref{sec:even}). In characteristic $2$ the hyperplanes of Section~\ref{sec:d6} must avoid the nucleus of $\Qf(6,q)$ and the whisker rule changes (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2}); it gives an exact whisker set for every even $q\ge4$, so that the bound of Corollary~\ref{cor:d6} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} hold for all such $q$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:even6}). For $q=2$ the rule fails and we use an exact set found by computer, so the case $q=2$ is computer-assisted. For $D=4$ nothing new is needed.
\item \emph{Orders} (Section~\ref{sec:orders}). Doubling gains at most about $2q^{D-2}$ elements at $\Delta=q+2$; the constructions reach this order of magnitude for $D=4$ and attain the counting bound for $D=6$.
\end{itemize}

Theorems~\ref{thm:A} and~\ref{thm:exact}, Corollaries~\ref{cor:A1} and~\ref{cor:d6}, Proposition~\ref{prop:C} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} are stated and proved for odd $q$ in Sections~\ref{sec:d4} and~\ref{sec:d6}. For $D=6$, Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2} (even $q\ge4$) and Corollary~\ref{cor:even6} (Corollary~\ref{cor:d6} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} for every prime power $q$, with $q=2$ computer-assisted) are in Section~\ref{sec:even}. \subsection{Incidence structures and distance}
An \emph{incidence structure} $\cS=(\cP,\cL,\I)$ consists of disjoint sets $\cP$ of \emph{points} and $\cL$ of \emph{lines} and a symmetric relation $\I\subseteq(\cP\times\cL)\cup(\cL\times\cP)$. Points and lines are \emph{elements}. We do not assume that two points lie on at most one line: some of our structures have repeated lines. For a set $\cA$ of elements, the \emph{substructure induced on $\cA$} is $(\cA\cap\cP,\cA\cap\cL,\I)$; its connected components are the components of the subgraph of $\Gamma(\cS)$ induced on $\cA$. We say that $\cA$ is a \emph{tree} if this subgraph is a tree.

All distances are taken in $\Gamma(\cS)$. Two points are at even distance, a point and a line at odd distance. Two distinct points are at distance $2$ exactly when they are collinear, and a point $p$ and a line $L$ are at distance $3$ exactly when $p\nI L$ and some point of $L$ is collinear with $p$. An element is \emph{isolated} if it is incident with nothing.

\begin{lemma}[Diameter in geometric terms]\label{lem:diam}
Let $\cS$ be an incidence structure without isolated elements and let $D\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $D$ is even, then $\diam\cS\le D$ if and only if $\dd(p,L)\le D-1$ for every anti-flag $(p,L)$.
\item If $D$ is odd, then $\diam\cS\le D$ if and only if any two points are at distance at most $D-1$ and any two lines are at distance at most $D-1$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $\diam\cS\le3$ if and only if any two points are collinear and any two lines are concurrent;
\item $\diam\cS\le4$ if and only if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point of $L$ is collinear with $p$;
\item $\diam\cS\le6$ if and only if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point $x\I L$ and some point $y$ satisfy $p\in y^\perp$ and $x\in y^\perp$.
\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $p\ne p'$ be points. Choose a line $L\I p'$. If $p\I L$ then $\dd(p,p')=2$; otherwise $\dd(p,p')\le\dd(p,L)+1\le D$. Two lines are handled in the same way, choosing a point on one of them. A flag is at distance $1$. (ii) Let $(p,L)$ be an anti-flag and choose $p'\I L$. Then $p'\ne p$ and $\dd(p,L)\le\dd(p,p')+1\le D$. The converse directions of (i) and (ii) hold by parity. The three special cases are the translations of $\dd\le2$, $\dd\le3$ and $\dd\le5$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Generalized polygons}
Following Tits and \cite{VM98}, a \emph{generalized $n$-gon} ($n\ge3$) is an incidence structure whose incidence graph has diameter $n$ and girth $2n$. It has \emph{order $(s,t)$} if every line has $s+1$ points and every point is on $t+1$ lines; it is \emph{thick} if $s,t\ge2$. Elements at distance $n$ are \emph{opposite}. We use two consequences of the girth.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(G\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item\label{g:unique} If $\dd(x,y)<n$, there is a unique path of length $\dd(x,y)$ from $x$ to $y$.
\item\label{g:count} A generalized quadrangle ($n=4$) of order $(s,t)$ has $(s+1)(st+1)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)$ lines; for an anti-flag $(p,L)$ there is a unique point of $L$ collinear with $p$. A generalized hexagon of order $(s,t)$ has $(s+1)(s^2t^2+st+1)$ points and $(t+1)(s^2t^2+st+1)$ lines.
\end{enumerate}
A generalized $n$-gon of order $(q,q)$ has $2(1+q+\dots+q^{n-1})=M_b(q+1,n)$ elements. By Feit and Higman \cite{FH64}, finite thick generalized $n$-gons exist only for $n\in\{3,4,6,8\}$, and for $n=8$ the number $2st$ is a square.

\subsection{The classical examples}
Let $q$ be a prime power and $\F=\GF(q)$.

\emph{The plane $\PG(2,q)$} \cite{H98}. Points $\pnt xyz$ and lines $\lin uvw$ are nonzero vectors of $\F^3$ up to scalars, and $\pnt xyz\I\lin uvw$ if and only if $ux+vy+wz=0$. It is a generalized $3$-gon (projective plane) of order $(q,q)$. Non-Desarguesian projective planes also exist for many $q$; Section~\ref{sec:d3} works in any projective plane.

\emph{The quadrangles $\Wq(q)$ and $\Qf(4,q)$} \cite[3.1.1, 3.2.1]{PT}. $\Wq(q)=\Wq(3,q)$ has as points all points of $\PG(3,q)$ and as lines the totally isotropic lines of a symplectic polarity; $\Qf(4,q)$ has as points and lines those of a non-singular quadric of $\PG(4,q)$. Both have order $(q,q)$, and $\Qf(4,q)$ is isomorphic to the dual of $\Wq(q)$. An \emph{ovoid} of a generalized quadrangle is a set of points meeting every line in exactly one point; a \emph{spread} is the dual notion. A \emph{partial ovoid} meets every line in at most one point. $\Qf(4,q)$ has ovoids for every $q$ (the elliptic quadric sections $\Qf^-(3,q)$); $\Wq(q)$ has ovoids if and only if $q$ is even, so $\Qf(4,q)$ has spreads if and only if $q$ is even \cite[3.4.1]{PT}. $\Wq(q)$ always has spreads, for instance regular spreads of $\PG(3,q)$.

\emph{$m$-ovoids and weights.} An \emph{$m$-ovoid} is a set of points meeting every line in exactly $m$ points \cite{BLP09}. A \emph{weighted $m$-ovoid} is a function $w\colon\cP\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ with $\sum_{p\I L}w(p)=m$ for every line $L$; equivalently a multiset of points meeting every line in $m$ points counted with multiplicity. Dually, a \emph{(weighted) $m$-cover} is a set (multiset) of lines with exactly $m$ members through every point. By duality, a weighted $m$-ovoid of $\Wq(q)$ is a weighted $m$-cover of $\Qf(4,q)$.

\emph{The quadrangle $\AS(q)$.} Every point $x$ of $\Wq(q)$ is regular. Payne's derivation $P(\Wq(q),x)$ \cite{P71}, \cite[3.1.4]{PT} has as points the points of $\PG(3,q)$ not in $x^\perp$, and as lines (i) the totally isotropic lines not through $x$, each with its point in $x^\perp$ removed, and (ii) the lines of $\PG(3,q)$ through $x$ not contained in $x^\perp$ (the hyperbolic lines $\{x,y\}^{\perp\perp}$), each with $x$ removed. It is a generalized quadrangle of order $(q-1,q+1)$, and for $q$ odd it is isomorphic to the Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle $\AS(q)$ \cite{AS69}, \cite[3.1.5, 3.2.6]{PT}. The lines of type (ii) form a spread of $\AS(q)$.

\emph{The hexagon $\Hq(q)$} \cite{T59}, \cite[\S2.4]{VM98}. Let $\Qf(6,q)$ be the quadric $X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6=X_3^2$ of $\PG(6,q)$. The points of $\Hq(q)$ are all points of $\Qf(6,q)$; the lines are the lines of $\Qf(6,q)$ whose Grassmann coordinates $p_{ij}=x_iy_j-x_jy_i$ satisfy Tits' equations
\[
p_{12}=p_{34},\quad p_{54}=p_{32},\quad p_{20}=p_{35},\quad p_{65}=p_{30},\quad p_{01}=p_{36},\quad p_{46}=p_{31}.
\]
$\Hq(q)$ is a generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$. For a point $x$, the conditions that a point $y$ lies on a line of $\Hq(q)$ through $x$ are linear in $y$; the $q+1$ lines of $\Hq(q)$ through $x$ are the lines through $x$ of a totally singular plane $\pi_x$ of $\Qf(6,q)$ (the hexagon is \emph{flat}). A \emph{Hermitian spread} of $\Hq(q)$ is the set of lines of $\Hq(q)$ contained in a hyperplane $\Pi$ for which $\Pi\cap\Qf(6,q)$ is an elliptic quadric $\Qf^-(5,q)$ \cite{T80}, \cite{VM98}; Lemma~\ref{lem:herm} proves the properties we use.

\section{Doubling with levels}\label{sec:doubling}

Delorme and Farhi \cite{DF84} observed that a \emph{duplicate} of an element (a new element incident with exactly the same elements) does not increase the diameter. Delorme \cite{D85a,D85b} refined this into a doubling of a whole set of elements, with levels, and used it to build the largest bipartite graphs then known of diameters $4$ and $6$. We recast it as an operation on incidence structures.\footnote{Note for co-authors: the full texts of Delorme's two 1985 papers \cite{D85a,D85b} have not been read by the authors of this draft. Our earlier notes attribute the doubling with levels and the bipartite constructions for $D=4,6$ to \cite{D85a}, Sections~7--8; the abstract of \cite{D85b} also describes bipartite constructions. Please check which paper contains which construction; no section numbers are claimed here.}

\subsection{The operation}
Let $\cS=(\cP,\cL,\I)$ be an incidence structure, $m\ge2$ an integer, and
\[
\cA=(\cA_0\supseteq\cA_1\supseteq\dots\supseteq\cA_r\supseteq\cA_{r+1}=\emptyset)
\]
a filtration of a set $\cA_0$ of elements (points and lines may be mixed). The \emph{level} of an element $x$ is $\lambda(x)=\max\{k: x\in\cA_k\}$, with $\lambda(x)=-1$ if $x\notin\cA_0$. Let $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$ and write $u\preceq w$ if the word $u$ is a prefix of the word $w$; two words are \emph{comparable} if one is a prefix of the other.

\begin{definition}[Doubling]\label{def:doubling}
The \emph{$m$-fold doubling of $\cS$ along $\cA$} is the incidence structure $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ whose elements are the \emph{versions}
\[
x^w,\qquad x\in\cP\cup\cL,\quad w\in[m]^{\lambda(x)+1},
\]
where $x^w$ is a point if $x$ is a point and a line if $x$ is a line, and in which
\[
x^w\I y^u\iff x\I y\ \text{ and $w,u$ are comparable.}
\]
An element of level $-1$ has the single version $x^\varnothing$, which we identify with $x$. For $m=2$ we speak of \emph{doubling}, for $m=3$ of \emph{tripling}.
\end{definition}

For a single level ($r=0$, $\cA=\cA_0$) and $m=2$, write $x=x^1$ and $x'=x^2$ for $x\in\cA$. Then $\Dbl{\cS}{2}$ is obtained from $\cS$ by adjoining, for each $x\in\cA$, a new element $x'$ of the same type, incident with
\[
y'\ \text{for every } y\in\cA \text{ with } y\I x,\qquad\text{and}\qquad y\ \text{for every } y\notin\cA \text{ with } y\I x.
\]
If $\cA$ contains no flag, $x'$ is a duplicate of $x$ in the sense of \cite{DF84}. In general $x'$ is \emph{not} a duplicate: inside $\cA$ the copies are incident with copies, outside $\cA$ with originals.

The map $x^w\mapsto x$ preserves incidence, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proj}
\dd(x^w,y^u)\ge\dd(x,y)\qquad\text{for all versions.}
\end{equation}
In particular two opposite elements of level $-1$ stay at distance $n$.

\subsection{Delorme's theorem}
Consider the following conditions on $\cA$, where $D\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(L\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em,start=0]
\item\label{L0} Every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_0$.
\item\label{L1} For every $i\ge0$: every element incident with an element of $\cA_{i+1}$ lies in $\cA_i$, and every element of $\cA_i\setminus\cA_{i+1}$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_{i+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(C$_D$)},leftmargin=3em]
\item\label{CD} For any two elements $x,y$ (possibly $x=y$) in the same connected component of the substructure induced on $\cA_0$, there is an element $z\notin\cA_0$ with $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le D$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}[Delorme {\cite{D85a,D85b}}]\label{thm:delorme}
Let $\cS$ be an incidence structure of diameter at most $D$, $D\ge2$, and let $m\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $\cA$ satisfies \ref{CD}, then $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ has diameter at most $D$.
\item If $\cA$ satisfies \ref{L0} and \ref{L1}, then every version $x^w$ has $\deg x^w\le\deg x+m-1$, with equality only if $x$ is incident with an element of level $\lambda(x)+1$. In particular $\Delta(\Dbl{\cS}{m})\le\Delta(\cS)+m-1$.
\item The numbers of points and of lines of $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ are
\[
|\cP|+(m-1)\sum_{k\ge0}m^k|\cA_k\cap\cP|\qquad\text{and}\qquad |\cL|+(m-1)\sum_{k\ge0}m^k|\cA_k\cap\cL|.
\]
\item Conversely, if $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ has diameter at most $D$, then \ref{CD} holds; indeed its conclusion holds for every pair $x,y\in\cA_0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

We include a proof.

\begin{proof}
(c) An element of level $k$ has $m^{k+1}$ versions, and $m^{k+1}-1=(m-1)(1+m+\dots+m^k)$. Summing over the levels gives the formula.

(b) Let $\lambda(x)=k$ and $|w|=k+1$. A neighbor $y$ of $x$ with $\lambda(y)=j\le k$ has exactly one version incident with $x^w$ (the one indexed by the prefix of $w$ of length $j+1$), and a neighbor with $\lambda(y)=j>k$ has $m^{j-k}$ such versions. If $k=-1$, \ref{L0} says that $x$ has at most one neighbor in $\cA_0$, and that neighbor has level $0$, since a neighbor in $\cA_1$ would put $x$ in $\cA_0$ by \ref{L1}. If $k\ge0$, \ref{L1} says that $x$ has at most one neighbor in $\cA_{k+1}$, and none in $\cA_{k+2}$, since that would put $x$ in $\cA_{k+1}$. So $\deg x^w\le\deg x-1+m$, with equality only when the higher neighbor exists.

(a) For an infinite word $\omega$ over $[m]$, the \emph{sheet} $\Sigma_\omega$ is the set of versions $x^w$ with $w\preceq\omega$. Every element has exactly one version in $\Sigma_\omega$, and any two versions in $\Sigma_\omega$ have comparable words, so $x^w\mapsto x$ is an isomorphism from the substructure on $\Sigma_\omega$ onto $\cS$. Two versions lie in a common sheet if and only if their words are comparable, and then they are at distance at most $D$.

Let $x^w,y^u$ have incomparable words. Then both words are non-empty, so $x,y\in\cA_0$. If $x$ and $y$ lie in different components of the substructure on $\cA_0$, a shortest path $x=z_0,z_1,\dots,z_k=y$ in $\Gamma(\cS)$, $k\le D$, contains some $z_i\notin\cA_0$. The element $z_i$ has a single version, which lies in every sheet. Lift $z_0,\dots,z_i$ into a sheet containing $x^w$ and $z_i,\dots,z_k$ into a sheet containing $y^u$; this is a path of length $k$ from $x^w$ to $y^u$. If $x$ and $y$ lie in the same component (this includes $x=y$), \ref{CD} gives $z\notin\cA_0$ with $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le D$, and the same lifting gives a path of length at most $D$.

(d) Let $x,y\in\cA_0$ (possibly $x=y$). Both have versions with non-empty words, so choose $x^w$ and $y^u$ with $w$ beginning with the letter $1$ and $u$ with the letter $2$. Take a path of length at most $D$ from $x^w$ to $y^u$ in $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$. Consecutive versions on it have comparable words, and two comparable non-empty words begin with the same letter. So some version on the path has the empty word; it is the unique version of an element $z\notin\cA_0$. The map $x^w\mapsto x$ preserves incidence, so the path projects to a walk from $x$ through $z$ to $y$ of length at most $D$, whence $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le D$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The hat lemma}
In a generalized polygon, condition \ref{CD} has a clean geometric form.

\begin{lemma}[Hat lemma]\label{lem:hat}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized $n$-gon of order $(s,t)$ with $s,t\ge1$, and let $\cA_0$ be a set of elements. Then \textup{(C$_n$)} holds if and only if every connected component of the substructure induced on $\cA_0$ is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose some component $B$ contains a path $x=z_0,z_1,\dots,z_{n-1}=y$ of length $n-1$. (It does if $B$ has diameter at least $n-1$; it does if $B$ contains a cycle, because cycles of $\Gamma(\cS)$ have length at least $2n$.) By the girth, this is the unique path of length at most $n-1$ from $x$ to $y$, so $\dd(x,y)=n-1$. If $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le n$, then by parity $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)=n-1$, so $z$ lies on a shortest path from $x$ to $y$, that is $z=z_i\in\cA_0$. So \textup{(C$_n$)} fails.

Conversely, let $B$ be a tree of diameter at most $n-2$, let $x,y\in B$, and let $P$ be the path from $x$ to $y$ in $B$, of length $d$. The component $B$ is not all of $\Gamma(\cS)$, which contains cycles, so some element of $B$ has a neighbor outside $\cA_0$. Let $k$ be the least distance in $B$ from $P$ to such an element $u$, and let $z\notin\cA_0$ be a neighbor of $u$. Then $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le d+2k+2$. If $k=0$ this is at most $n$. If $k\ge1$, every element of $B$ at distance less than $k$ from $P$ has all its neighbors in $B$, and every element has at least two neighbors. Walking $k$ steps away from $P$ from $x$, and $k$ steps away from $P$ from $y$ (along two different neighbors if $x=y$), we obtain a path of length $d+2k$ in the tree $B$. Hence $d+2k\le n-2$, and again $\dd(x,z)+\dd(z,y)\le n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hatiff}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized $n$-gon of order $(s,t)$ with $s,t\ge1$, let $\cA$ be a filtration of a set $\cA_0$ of elements, and let $m\ge2$. Then $\Dbl{\cS}{m}$ has diameter at most $n$ if and only if every connected component of the substructure induced on $\cA_0$ is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme}(a) and (d), the diameter is at most $n$ if and only if \textup{(C$_n$)} holds; apply Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}.
\end{proof}

In geometric terms, the components allowed by Lemma~\ref{lem:hat} are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $n=3$ (projective planes): single elements and flags;
\item $n=4$ (quadrangles): single elements, flags, and \emph{stars}: a point with some of the lines on it, or a line with some of its points;
\item $n=6$ (hexagons): trees of diameter at most $4$, for instance an element $x$, some elements incident with $x$, and some elements incident with those.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two consequences}
\begin{proposition}[Diameter $3$ needs patches]\label{prop:d3patch}
Let $\cS$ be a projective plane and let $\cA$ satisfy \ref{L0}, \ref{L1} and \textup{(C$_3$)}. Then $\cA_0$ consists of at most one point and at most one line, and $\cA_1=\emptyset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x\ne y$ be points of $\cA_0$. If the line $xy$ is not in $\cA_0$, it is incident with two elements of $\cA_0$, against \ref{L0}. If it is in $\cA_0$, then $x,xy,y$ lie in one component, which is then not a single element or a flag, against Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}. The same holds for lines. If $\cA_1$ contained an element, its $q+1\ge3$ neighbors would lie in $\cA_0$ by \ref{L1}.
\end{proof}

So a pure doubling of a projective plane gains at most two elements. The constructions of Section~\ref{sec:d3} double flags and then modify a bounded number of incidences (\emph{patches}).

\begin{proposition}[Counting bound]\label{prop:count}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized $n$-gon of order $(q,q)$ with $N$ points and $N$ lines, and let $\cA_0$ satisfy \ref{L0} and \textup{(C$_n$)}. Let $c$ be the number of components of the substructure on $\cA_0$. Then
\[
q\,|\cA_0|+2c\le 2N,
\]
with equality if and only if every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:hat} the substructure on $\cA_0$ is a forest with $|\cA_0|-c$ flags. So the number of flags with exactly one element in $\cA_0$ is $(q+1)|\cA_0|-2(|\cA_0|-c)$. By \ref{L0} it is at most $2N-|\cA_0|$, with equality as stated.
\end{proof}

Since $N\sim q^{n-1}$, a doubling along $\cA$ at $\Delta=q+2$ has $|\cA_0|<2N/q\sim 2q^{n-2}$; the higher levels contribute lower-order terms. Section~\ref{sec:orders} compares this with the Moore bound.

\section{Diameter 3: four hats in a projective plane}\label{sec:d3}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}, an incidence structure without isolated elements has diameter at most $3$ exactly when any two points are collinear and any two lines are concurrent. Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20} add three \emph{hats} to $\PG(2,q)$ and obtain $n_b(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+5)$. We add a fourth hat.

\subsection{The frame}
Let $\Pi$ be a projective plane of order $q\ge3$. Fix an anti-flag $(A,\ell)$, and four distinct points of $\ell$, labeled $X_s$ with $s$ in a four-element index set $S=\{0,1,a,c\}$. Put
\[
T=S\setminus\{a\}=\{0,1,c\},\qquad \lam_s=AX_s\ (s\in S),
\]
and let $\sigma$ be a $3$-cycle on $T$; for definiteness $\sigma=(0\;c\;1)$, that is $\sigma(0)=c$, $\sigma(c)=1$, $\sigma(1)=0$. Let
\[
\ell^*=\ell\setminus\{X_t:t\in T\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \Lambda^*=\{\text{lines on }A\}\setminus\{\lam_t:t\in T\}.
\]
So $\ell^*$ consists of $q-2$ points of $\ell$, among them $X_a$, and $\Lambda^*$ of $q-2$ lines on $A$, among them $\lam_a$.

\begin{definition}[Four hats]\label{def:fourhat}
The incidence structure $\cS=\cS(\Pi;A,\ell,(X_s)_{s\in S},\sigma)$ has as points the points of $\Pi$ and five new points $Q$, $H_s$ ($s\in S$), and as lines the lines of $\Pi$ and five new lines $M$, $K_s$ ($s\in S$). Its incidences are exactly the following.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(H\arabic*)},ref=\textup{H\arabic*},leftmargin=3em]
\item\label{h:plane} The incidences of $\Pi$, except the four flags $(X_s,\lam_s)$, $s\in S$; and the three \emph{rotated} flags $(X_t,\lam_{\sigma(t)})$, $t\in T$:
\[
X_0\I\lam_c,\qquad X_c\I\lam_1,\qquad X_1\I\lam_0.
\]
\item\label{h:hat} For $s\in S$, the hat point $H_s$ lies on every line of $\Pi$ through $X_s$ other than $\ell$.
\item\label{h:hatline} For $s\in S$, the hat line $K_s$ contains every point of $\lam_s$ other than $A$.
\item\label{h:M} $M$ contains the points of $\ell^*$ and the four hat points $H_s$.
\item\label{h:Q} $Q$ lies on the lines of $\Lambda^*$ and on the four hat lines $K_s$.
\item\label{h:links} The \emph{links}: $A\I K_a$, $H_a\I\ell$, and $H_t\I K_{\sigma(t)}$ for $t\in T$:
\[
H_0\I K_c,\qquad H_c\I K_1,\qquad H_1\I K_0.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The hat pair $(H_s,K_s)$ is a copy of the flag $(X_s,\lam_s)$: $H_s$ lies on the lines through $X_s$ and $K_s$ contains the points of $\lam_s$, with the flag ``crossed'' ($H_s\I\lam_s$ and $X_s\I K_s$). The pair $(Q,M)$ is a copy of the anti-flag $(A,\ell)$; see Remark~\ref{rem:al-doubling}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fourhat}
Let $\Pi$ be a projective plane of order $q\ge3$. The incidence structure $\cS$ of Definition~\ref{def:fourhat} has $q^2+q+6$ points and $q^2+q+6$ lines, every element has degree $q+1$ or $q+2$, and $\cS$ has diameter~$3$. Hence $n_b(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+6)$ for every prime power $q\ge3$.
\end{theorem}

The proof occupies Lemmas~\ref{lem:fh-deg}--\ref{lem:fh-dual}.

\begin{lemma}[Degrees]\label{lem:fh-deg}
In $\cS$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $A$, every point of $\ell$, $Q$ and every $H_s$ are on exactly $q+2$ lines;
\item a point $p\notin\ell$, $p\ne A$, of $\Pi$ is on $q+2$ lines if $Ap=\lam_s$ for some $s\in S$, and on $q+1$ lines otherwise;
\item dually, $\ell$, every line on $A$, $M$ and every $K_s$ have exactly $q+2$ points, and a line $L\ne\ell$ of $\Pi$ not on $A$ has $q+2$ points if $L\cap\ell=X_s$ for some $s\in S$, and $q+1$ points otherwise.
\end{enumerate}
In particular there are $(q-1)(q-3)$ points and $(q-1)(q-3)$ lines of degree $q+1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We count lines on each point: the number in $\Pi$, minus removed flags, plus added flags.
$A$: its flags in $\Pi$ are untouched, and it gains $K_a$ (\ref{h:links}); it is not on any other $K_s$ by~\ref{h:hatline}.
$X\in\ell^*$, $X\ne X_a$: gains $M$.
$X_a$: loses $\lam_a$, gains $M$ and $K_a$ (\ref{h:hatline}, as $X_a\ne A$ is on $\lam_a$).
$X_t$, $t\in T$: loses $\lam_t$, gains $\lam_{\sigma(t)}$ and $K_t$; it is not on $M$.
A point $p\notin\ell$, $p\ne A$: it is on exactly one line on $A$, namely $Ap$; it loses nothing and gains $K_s$ exactly when $Ap=\lam_s$.
$Q$: $|\Lambda^*|+4=q+2$ lines.
$H_s$: $q$ lines of $\Pi$, $M$, and one link (\ref{h:links}): $\ell$ if $s=a$, $K_{\sigma(s)}$ if $s\in T$.
The statement for lines is the dual count (or follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:fh-dual}). Finally, the points of degree $q+1$ are the $q-1$ points other than $A$ and $X$ on each of the $q-3$ lines $AX$ with $X\in\ell\setminus\{X_s:s\in S\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Points]\label{lem:fh-points}
Any two distinct points of $\cS$ are collinear in $\cS$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The only flags of $\Pi$ missing from $\cS$ are $(X_s,\lam_s)$, $s\in S$. Let $u\ne v$ be points of $\cS$.

\emph{(P1) $u,v$ in $\Pi$.} Let $L=uv$ in $\Pi$. If both flags survive, $L$ works. Otherwise, say, $u=X_s$ and $L=\lam_s$. If $v\ne A$, both $u$ and $v$ lie on $K_s$ by~\ref{h:hatline}. If $v=A$ and $s=a$, use $K_a$ (\ref{h:hatline}, \ref{h:links}). If $v=A$ and $s\in T$, use $\lam_{\sigma(s)}$, which contains $A$ and, by the rotation, $X_s$.

\emph{(P2) $Q$ and a point $u$ of $\Pi$.} If $u=A$, use $\lam_a\in\Lambda^*$. If $u\in\ell$ and $u\ne X_s$ for all $s$, use $Au\in\Lambda^*$. If $u=X_s$, use $K_s$. If $u\notin\ell$, $u\ne A$, let $X=Au\cap\ell$: if $Au\in\Lambda^*$ use $Au$; otherwise $X=X_t$ with $t\in T$ and $u$, $Q$ lie on $K_t$.

\emph{(P3) $H_s$ and a point $u$ of $\Pi$.} If $u\notin\ell$ (this includes $u=A$), the line $uX_s$ of $\Pi$ is not $\ell$, contains $u$ in $\cS$ (as $u\notin\ell$) and contains $H_s$ by~\ref{h:hat}. If $u=X_s$, use any line through $X_s$ other than $\ell$ and $\lam_s$. If $u\in\ell^*$, $u\ne X_s$, use $M$. Otherwise $u=X_t$ with $t\in T\setminus\{s\}$. If $s=a$, use $\ell$ (\ref{h:links}). If $s\in T$, then $t\in\{\sigma(s),\sigma^{-1}(s)\}$ because $\sigma$ is a $3$-cycle. If $t=\sigma(s)$, use $K_{\sigma(s)}$, which contains $X_{\sigma(s)}$ (\ref{h:hatline}) and $H_s$ (\ref{h:links}). If $t=\sigma^{-1}(s)$, use $\lam_s$, which contains $X_{\sigma^{-1}(s)}$ (rotation) and $H_s$ (\ref{h:hat}).

\emph{(P4) Two new points.} $H_s$ and $H_{s'}$: $M$. $Q$ and $H_a$: $\lam_a$ ($\lam_a\in\Lambda^*$, and $\lam_a\ne\ell$ is a line through $X_a$). $Q$ and $H_t$, $t\in T$: $K_{\sigma(t)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Duality]\label{lem:fh-dual}
Let $\Pi^D$ be the dual plane. The dual of $\cS(\Pi;A,\ell,(X_s),\sigma)$ is isomorphic to $\cS(\Pi^D;\ell,A,(\lam_s),\sigma^{-1})$, by the identity on $\Pi$ and
\[
H_s\leftrightarrow K_s,\qquad Q\leftrightarrow M .
\]
Consequently any two distinct lines of $\cS$ are concurrent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In $\Pi^D$ the point $\ell$ is off the line $A$, the points $\lam_s$ lie on the line $A$, and the line joining the points $\ell$ and $\lam_s$ is $X_s$. Read the six rules of Definition~\ref{def:fourhat} in $\Pi^D$ with these data and with $\sigma^{-1}$. Rule~\ref{h:plane} removes the flags $(\lam_s,X_s)$ and adds $\lam_t\I X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$, which is the rotation $X_u\I\lam_{\sigma(u)}$ of $\cS$. Rule~\ref{h:hat} gives a new element incident with every element of $\Pi$ incident with $\lam_s$ other than $A$: this is $K_s$. Rule~\ref{h:hatline} gives a new element incident with every element incident with $X_s$ other than $\ell$: this is $H_s$. Rule~\ref{h:M} gives a new element incident with the lines on $A$ other than $\lam_t$ ($t\in T$) and with all $K_s$: this is $Q$; likewise~\ref{h:Q} gives $M$. Rule~\ref{h:links} gives $\ell\I H_a$, $K_a\I A$ and $K_t\I H_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$, which are the links of $\cS$. Since $\Pi^D$ is a projective plane of order $q$ and $\sigma^{-1}$ is a $3$-cycle on $T$, Lemma~\ref{lem:fh-points} for $\cS(\Pi^D;\ell,A,(\lam_s),\sigma^{-1})$ says that any two lines of $\cS$ are concurrent.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:fourhat}]
The counts are Lemma~\ref{lem:fh-deg}; no element is isolated. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:fh-points}, \ref{lem:fh-dual} and~\ref{lem:diam}, $\diam\cS\le3$, and $\diam\cS=3$ because $A\nI\ell$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A correlation]\label{lem:corr}
The map $\delta\colon\pnt xyz\mapsto\lin xz{-y}$, $\lin uvw\mapsto\pnt uw{-v}$ is a correlation of $\PG(2,q)$ with
\[
\delta(A)=\ell,\quad \delta(X_t)=\lam_t,\quad \delta(P)=\lam_\infty,
\]
and a point $p$ lies on $\delta(p)$ if and only if $p\in\ell$. It maps the points of degree $q+1$ bijectively onto the lines of degree $q+1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$ux+wz+(-v)(-y)=ux+vy+wz$, so $\delta$ preserves incidence; it is clearly bijective. $\pnt xyz\I\lin xz{-y}$ iff $x^2+yz-zy=x^2=0$. Finally $\delta\pnt xy1=\lin x1{-y}$ with $x\ne0$, $y\notin S$, and $\delta\pnt x10=\lin x0{-1}=\lin10{-1/x}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fourhat-regular}
Let $q\ge4$ be a prime power and $a,c$ distinct elements of $\F\setminus\{0,1\}$. Adjoining to $\cS(\PG(2,q);A,\ell,(X_s),\sigma)$ the $(q-1)(q-3)$ flags $(p,\delta(p))$, $p$ a point of degree $q+1$, gives a $(q+2)$-regular incidence structure with $q^2+q+6$ points, $q^2+q+6$ lines and diameter~$3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:corr} the new flags are not flags of $\cS$ (the points of degree $q+1$ are off $\ell$) and they match the elements of degree $q+1$ in pairs. Adding flags cannot increase distances, and $A\nI\ell$ still.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Arbitrary planes]\label{rem:arbplanes}
In any projective plane of order $q\ge4$ a regular completion also exists. Let $N=(q-1)(q-3)$. A point $p$ of degree $q+1$ is incident with exactly $q-4$ lines of degree $q+1$: the lines on $p$ other than $Ap$ meet $\ell$ in the $q$ points other than $Ap\cap\ell$, and four of these are the $X_s$. Dually, a line of degree $q+1$ has exactly $q-4$ points of degree $q+1$. Since $2(q-4)\le N$, Hall's theorem gives a perfect matching of non-incident pairs: a set of at most $N-(q-4)$ points has at least $N-(q-4)$ non-incident partners, and a larger set has every line as a non-incident partner of one of its points. (This is the argument of the Lean proof, Section~\ref{sec:verify}.)
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The three-hat structure of {\cite{AL20}}]\label{rem:al}
In the same coordinates, the structure of Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez uses the hats $S_{AL}=\{0,1,a\}$, no rotation, and links $A\I K_a$, $H_a\I\ell$, $H_0\I K_1$, $H_1\I K_0$; it removes only the flag $(X_a,\lam_a)$, and $M$ contains $P$ and $X_t$ for $t\notin\{0,1\}$, $Q$ lies on $\lam_\infty$ and $\lam_t$ for $t\notin\{0,1\}$. (In their affine coordinates $(x,y)=\pnt xy1$, $[m,b]=\lin m{-1}b$, $L_c=\lin10{-c}$, $L=\lin001$, $P_m=\pnt1m0$; so $L_0=\ell$, $P_0=A$, $(0,s)=X_s$, $[0,s]=\lam_s$ and $L=\lam_\infty$.) It has $2(q^2+q+5)$ elements and diameter $3$ for $q\ge3$ \cite{AL20}; the proof is a case analysis like that of Lemma~\ref{lem:fh-points}. Its elements of degree $q+1$ are the points $(x,y)$, $x\ne0$, $y\notin S_{AL}$, and $P_i$, $i\ne0$, and the lines $[x,y]$, $x\ne0$, $y\notin S_{AL}$, and $L_i$, $i\ne0$. The completing matching printed in \cite{AL20} is $(x,y)\sim[x,y]$ together with $P_i\sim L_i$ for $i\in\F\setminus\{0,1,a\}$; with this index set the four elements $P_1,P_a,L_1,L_a$ keep degree $q+1$. Taking $i\in\F^*$ instead (the pairs $P_i\notin L_i$ are always non-incident) gives a $(q+2)$-regular structure, as intended. This affects neither the maximum degree nor the diameter.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Hats as doubled flags]\label{rem:al-doubling}
Let $\cA=\{\ell,A\}\cup\{X_s,\lam_s:s\in S_{AL}\}$ in $\PG(2,q)$ and double it ($m=2$), except that each flag $(X_s,\lam_s)$ is \emph{crossed}: instead of $X_s'\I\lam_s'$ put $X_s'\I\lam_s$ and $X_s\I\lam_s'$. With $M=\ell'$, $Q=A'$, $H_s=X_s'$, $K_s=\lam_s'$, one checks from the rules that the structure of \cite{AL20} is this crossed doubling plus the six incidences $H_1\I K_0$, $H_0\I K_1$, $H_a\I\ell$, $A\I K_a$, $X_a\I M$, $Q\I\lam_a$, minus the flag $(X_a,\lam_a)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:d3patch} some patching is unavoidable: the component of $\cA$ contains the path $\ell,X_s,\lam_s,A$ of length $3>n-2=1$. For $q=5$ a direct check shows that in the crossed doubling without patches the pairs at distance greater than $3$ are exactly $(H_s,X_t)$, $(K_s,\lam_t)$ ($s\ne t$), $(H_s,Q)$ and $(K_s,M)$, all of them copies of pairs joined inside $\cA$ by a path of length $2=n-1$ (such as $X_s,\ell,X_t$ or $X_s,\lam_s,A$), as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}; the patches repair exactly these. Heuristically, the fourth hat needs the rotation because $M$ now misses three points of $\ell$, and each hat point $H_t$, $t\in T$, must reach the two of them other than $X_t$ with only two spare incidences.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
All proofs above use only the axioms of a projective plane, so Theorem~\ref{thm:fourhat} holds in non-Desarguesian planes as well. For $\Delta=q+2\le16$ the theorem gives $192$, $276$, $376$ at $\Delta=11,13,15$ ($q=9,11,13$), two more than \cite{AL20}; at $\Delta=7$ ($q=5$) it gives $72$, below the known value $80$.
\end{remark}

\section{Diameter 4: generalized quadrangles}\label{sec:d4}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}, an incidence structure without isolated elements has diameter at most $4$ exactly when, for every anti-flag $(p,L)$, some point of $L$ is collinear with $p$. Every generalized quadrangle has this property, with a unique such point.

\subsection{Doubling in quadrangles}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}, in a generalized quadrangle a set $\cA_0$ satisfies \textup{(C$_4$)} exactly when its components are single elements, flags or stars. The following is Delorme's construction \cite{D85a,D85b} in this language.

\begin{example}[Delorme]\label{ex:delorme4}
Let $\Sigma$ be a spread of $\Wq(q)$, let $v\in\Sigma$, and let $\cP(v)$ be the set of points of $v$. Put
\[
\cA_1=\{v\},\qquad \cA_0=\Sigma\cup\cP(v).
\]
\ref{L1}: the elements incident with $v$ are its points; a point of $v$ is incident with $v$ only, a line of $\Sigma\setminus\{v\}$ with nothing in $\cA_1$. \ref{L0}: a point off $v$ is on exactly one line of $\Sigma$ and on no other line of $\cA_0$; a line not in $\Sigma$ meets $v$ in at most one point and contains no other element of $\cA_0$. The components are the star $\{v\}\cup\cP(v)$ and the single lines of $\Sigma\setminus\{v\}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme} with $m=2$ we get maximum degree $q+2$, diameter $4$ and
\[
2(q+1)(q^2+1)+|\cA_0|+2|\cA_1|=2(q+1)(q^2+1)+q^2+q+4=2q^3+3q^2+3q+6
\]
elements. These are the previous table values $346$, $1734$, $3064$, $4946$ at $\Delta=7,11,13,15$. More generally, the $m$-fold doubling along the same $\cA$ has maximum degree $q+m$ and $2(q+1)(q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl((q^2+1)+(q+1)+m\bigr)$ elements; for $m=3$, $q=13$ this is $5134$, the previous table value at $\Delta=16$. Dually: an ovoid $\cO$ of $\Qf(4,q)$, a point $o\in\cO$, $\cA_1=\{o\}$, $\cA_0=\cO\cup\{\text{lines on }o\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Ovoids and stars]\label{rem:ovoidfamily}
More generally, let $\cO$ be an ovoid of $\Qf(4,q)$ and $\cM$ a set of lines such that every point outside $\cO$ is on at most one line of $\cM$. Then $\cA_0=\cO\cup\cM$ (one level) satisfies \ref{L0} and \textup{(C$_4$)}: its components are stars centered at points of $\cO$. This gives maximum degree $q+2$, diameter~$4$ and $2(q+1)(q^2+1)+q^2+1+|\cM|$ elements. If some $o\in\cO$ is on $k\ge2$ lines of $\cM$, then $|\cM|\le k+(q+1-k)q\le q^2-q+2$. Indeed, a line $L\in\cM$ not on $o$ has a unique point $y$ collinear with $o$; $y\notin\cO$, the line $oy$ is not in $\cM$ (else $y$ would be on two lines of $\cM$), and distinct $L$ give distinct $y$. If every point of $\cO$ is on at most one line of $\cM$, then $\cM$ is a partial spread, so $|\cM|\le q^2-q+1$ for $q$ odd (Tallini \cite{Tal91}; for large partial ovoids see also \cite{CDPS22}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Clones and shadow lines}
The next construction is not a doubling: it adjoins several copies of points and copies of lines that are attached to copies of points. Copies of lines in a generalized quadrangle appear already in Araujo-Pardo, Dalf\'o, Fiol and L\'opez \cite[Sec.~4]{ADFL21}, who double every line to obtain biregular graphs (semi-double graphs); here only some lines are copied, with weights, and a copy may be attached to clones.

\begin{definition}[Clones and shadows]\label{def:clones}
Let $\cS=(\cP,\cL,\I)$ be a generalized quadrangle of order $(s,t)$. Let $w\colon\cP\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ be a weight and $\cM$ a finite multiset of lines. For each entry $\mu$ of $\cM$, with underlying line $L_\mu$, choose for every point $p\I L_\mu$ a \emph{stand-in} $\sigma_\mu(p)\in\{p,p^1,\dots,p^{w(p)}\}$. The structure $\cS[w,\cM,\sigma]$ has
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item points: $\cP$ and the \emph{clones} $p^1,\dots,p^{w(p)}$ of each $p\in\cP$;
\item lines: $\cL$ and one \emph{shadow line} $L^\mu$ for each entry $\mu$ of $\cM$;
\item incidences: those of $\cS$; $p^i\I L$ whenever $p\I L\in\cL$; and $\sigma_\mu(p)\I L^\mu$ for $p\I L_\mu$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

So a clone is incident with exactly the old lines through its point, and a shadow line has exactly one point ``over'' each point of its line. Write $\pi$ for the map sending a clone or a shadow line to the underlying point or line ($\pi$ is the identity on $\cP\cup\cL$).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:B}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized quadrangle of order $(s,t)$, $s,t\ge1$, and let $\cS'=\cS[w,\cM,\sigma]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\cS'$ has diameter $4$.
\item $\Delta(\cS')\le\Delta$ if and only if $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$, $\sum_{p\I L}w(p)\le\Delta-s-1$ for every line $L$ of $\cS$, and every point of $\cS'$ \textup{(}old point or clone\textup{)} is incident with at most $\Delta-t-1$ shadow lines.
\item $\cS'$ has $(s+1)(st+1)+\sum_pw(p)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)+|\cM|$ lines.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(b) and (c) are counts: an old line $L$ has $s+1+\sum_{p\I L}w(p)$ points, a shadow line $s+1$ points, and an old point or clone is on $t+1$ old lines plus the shadow lines it serves.

(a) We use Lemma~\ref{lem:diam}(i). Let $(x,Y)$ be an anti-flag of $\cS'$. Every point $x$ of $\cS'$ is incident with every old line through $\pi(x)$.
If $Y$ is an old line, then $\pi(x)\nI Y$; let $r$ be the point of $Y$ collinear with $\pi(x)$ in $\cS$, on the line $N$. Then $x\I N\I r\I Y$.
If $Y=L^\mu$ and $\pi(x)\nI L_\mu$, let $r\I L_\mu$ be collinear with $\pi(x)$ on the line $N$. Then $x\I N\I\sigma_\mu(r)\I L^\mu$.
If $Y=L^\mu$ and $\pi(x)\I L_\mu$, pick $r\I L_\mu$ with $r\ne\pi(x)$. Then $x\I L_\mu\I\sigma_\mu(r)\I L^\mu$.
In each case some point of $Y$ is collinear with $x$, so $\diam\cS'\le4$. Two non-collinear points $p,p'$ of $\cS$ remain non-collinear: they share no old line, and a shadow line $L^\mu$ contains only points over points of $L_\mu$, and an old point is over itself only. So $\diam\cS'=4$.
\end{proof}

If all stand-ins are the old points, a shadow line is a duplicate of its line except that it misses the clones; this is the setting of the following corollary.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:B1}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized quadrangle of order $(s,t)$ and $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$. Suppose $\cS$ has a weighted $(\Delta-s-1)$-ovoid $w$ and a weighted $(\Delta-t-1)$-cover $\cM$. Then $\cS[w,\cM,\mathrm{id}]$ has diameter $4$, maximum degree $\Delta$ and $2\Delta(st+1)$ elements. Adjoining further flags between clones and shadow lines makes it $\Delta$-regular, with the same number of elements and diameter $4$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Double counting flags gives $\sum_pw(p)=(\Delta-s-1)(st+1)$ and $|\cM|=(\Delta-t-1)(st+1)$, and the total is $(s+t+2)(st+1)+(\Delta-s-1)(st+1)+(\Delta-t-1)(st+1)=2\Delta(st+1)$. Old points and old lines have degree exactly $\Delta$. Each clone has degree $t+1$ and lacks $\Delta-t-1$; each shadow line has degree $s+1$ and lacks $\Delta-s-1$; and $\sum_pw(p)\,(\Delta-t-1)=|\cM|\,(\Delta-s-1)$. Order the clones $c_0,c_1,\dots$ and the shadow lines $y_0,\dots,y_{b-1}$, $b=|\cM|$, and join $c_i$ to $y_j$ for $j\equiv i(\Delta-t-1)+k\pmod b$, $0\le k<\Delta-t-1$. Since $\Delta-t-1\le b$ these are distinct, and every shadow line receives exactly $\Delta-s-1$ of them. Adding flags cannot increase distances, and two non-collinear old points still have no common line.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two elliptic quadrics in \texorpdfstring{$\AS(q)$}{AS(q)}}
Let $q$ be odd. Take $\Wq(q)$ with the alternating form $\beta(u,v)=u_0v_1-u_1v_0+u_2v_3-u_3v_2$ and the point $x=(1{:}0{:}0{:}0)$. Then $x^\perp$ is the plane $X_1=0$, and the points of $\AS(q)=P(\Wq(q),x)$ are the $q^3$ points $(a{:}1{:}w_1{:}w_2)$, which we write $(a,w)$ with $a\in\F$, $w=(w_1,w_2)\in\F^2$. The lines of $\AS(q)$ are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item the \emph{fibers} $\{(a_0+\mu,w_0):\mu\in\F\}$: the hyperbolic lines $xy$, $y\notin x^\perp$, with $x$ removed;
\item the sets $\{(a_0+\lambda\det(w_0,d),\,w_0+\lambda d):\lambda\in\F\}$ with $d\ne0$: the totally isotropic line through $(a_0,w_0)$ and $(\det(w_0,d){:}0{:}d)$, with its point in $x^\perp$ removed.
\end{itemize}
Fix a non-square $\varepsilon\in\F$ and let $\Nm(w)=w_1^2-\varepsilon w_2^2$, an anisotropic form. For $\kappa\in\F^*$ let
\[
\cE_\kappa\colon\ X_0X_1=\kappa\,\Nm(X_2,X_3).
\]
This is an elliptic quadric of $\PG(3,q)$ through $x$, with tangent plane $x^\perp$ at $x$. So $\cE_\kappa\cap x^\perp=\{x\}$ and $\cE_\kappa\setminus\{x\}=\{(\kappa\Nm(w),w):w\in\F^2\}$ is a set of $q^2$ points of $\AS(q)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:A}
Let $q$ be odd, $\varepsilon$ a non-square and $\kappa_1\kappa_2=1/(4\varepsilon)$. Then every line of $\AS(q)$ meets $\cE_{\kappa_1}\setminus\{x\}$ and $\cE_{\kappa_2}\setminus\{x\}$ in exactly two points in total, a point on both quadrics being counted twice. The two quadrics meet only in $x$ and $e=(0,0)$. Hence $w=\mathbf 1_{\cE_{\kappa_1}\setminus x}+\mathbf 1_{\cE_{\kappa_2}\setminus x}$ is a weighted $2$-ovoid of $\AS(q)$ with $w(e)=2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Fibers.} A fiber lies on a line of $\PG(3,q)$ through $x$ not in the tangent plane $x^\perp$, so it meets each $\cE_\kappa$ in one point besides $x$ (in coordinates, at $\mu=\kappa\Nm(w_0)-a_0$).

\emph{The other lines.} Write $\rho=\det(w_0,d)$ and $B=\Nm(w_0+d)-\Nm(w_0)-\Nm(d)$. The point with parameter $\lambda$ lies on $\cE_\kappa$ if and only if
\[
\kappa\Nm(d)\lambda^2+(\kappa B-\rho)\lambda+\kappa\Nm(w_0)-a_0=0 .
\]
As $\Nm(d)\ne0$ this is a quadratic with $1+\chi(\operatorname{disc}_\kappa)$ roots, where $\chi$ is the quadratic character ($\chi(0)=0$) and $\operatorname{disc}_\kappa$ the discriminant. With $w_0=(b_0,c_0)$, $d=(b_1,c_1)$ one has $B=2(b_0b_1-\varepsilon c_0c_1)$ and
\[
B^2-4\Nm(w_0)\Nm(d)=4\varepsilon(b_0c_1-c_0b_1)^2=4\varepsilon\rho^2,
\]
hence
\[
\operatorname{disc}_\kappa=(\kappa B-\rho)^2-4\kappa\Nm(d)(\kappa\Nm(w_0)-a_0)=4\kappa\Nm(d)\,(a_0-a^*_\kappa),\qquad a^*_\kappa=-\frac{4\varepsilon\kappa\rho^2-2B\rho+\rho^2/\kappa}{4\Nm(d)}.
\]
Now $a^*_{\kappa_1}-a^*_{\kappa_2}=-\frac{\rho^2}{4\Nm(d)}(\kappa_1-\kappa_2)\bigl(4\varepsilon-\frac1{\kappa_1\kappa_2}\bigr)=0$; call the common value $a^*$. The line meets the two quadrics in
\[
2+\chi\bigl(\Nm(d)(a_0-a^*)\bigr)\bigl(\chi(\kappa_1)+\chi(\kappa_2)\bigr)=2
\]
points, because $\chi(\kappa_1)\chi(\kappa_2)=\chi(\varepsilon)=-1$. (A double root, $a_0=a^*$, gives one point on each quadric.)

\emph{Intersection.} $\kappa_1\ne\kappa_2$, since $\kappa_1^2=1/(4\varepsilon)$ is impossible. So $\kappa_1\Nm(w)=\kappa_2\Nm(w)$ forces $w=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:A1}
For every odd prime power $q$, $\AS(q)[w,\emptyset]$ with $w$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:A} is $(q+2)$-regular of diameter $4$ with $2q^2(q+2)$ elements. Hence $n_b(q+2,4)\ge2q^2(q+2)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:B} with $(s,t)=(q-1,q+1)$ and $\cM=\emptyset$: every line gains exactly two points, every clone is on the $q+2$ lines of its point, and there are $q^3+(q+2)q^2+2q^2$ elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A normalisation, and the new elements]\label{rem:Acoords}
The choice $\kappa_1=\tfrac12$, $\kappa_2=\tfrac1{2\varepsilon}$ satisfies $\kappa_1\kappa_2=1/(4\varepsilon)$, so the two quadrics can be taken to be $2X_0X_1=X_2^2-\varepsilon X_3^2$ and $2\varepsilon X_0X_1=X_2^2-\varepsilon X_3^2$. In the structure of Corollary~\ref{cor:A1}, the clone of $P=(\kappa_i\Nm(w){:}1{:}w)$ is incident with exactly the $q+2$ lines of $\AS(q)$ on $P$: the fiber $\{(\kappa_i\Nm(w)+\mu,w):\mu\in\F\}$ and, for each $d\in\PG(1,q)$, the line $\{(\kappa_i\Nm(w)+\lambda\det(w,d),\,w+\lambda d):\lambda\in\F\}$. The fiber over $w_0$ gains the clones of $(\kappa_1\Nm(w_0),w_0)$ and $(\kappa_2\Nm(w_0),w_0)$; a line of the second kind gains the clones of its points on the two quadrics, given by the roots of the quadratic in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}. No other incidence changes.
\end{remark}

At $q=9,11,13$ this gives $1782$, $3146$, $5070$. Since the structure is regular, no choice of clones and shadows on $\AS(q)$ does better at $\Delta=q+2$. Weights are needed: for $q=3,5$, $\AS(q)$ has no $2$-ovoid without repeated points.

\subsection{A Singer \texorpdfstring{$2$}{2}-ovoid of \texorpdfstring{$\Wq(q)$}{W(q)}}
Let $q$ be odd, $\F=\GF(q)\subset K=\GF(q^2)\subset E=\GF(q^4)$, and $g$ a primitive element of $E$. Model $\PG(3,q)$ as $E^*/\F^*$; the point $\langle g^i\rangle$ has index $i$ modulo $(q+1)(q^2+1)$. Write $\bar y=y^{q^2}$, $k'=k^q$ for $k\in K$, $\Nm(y)=y\bar y\in K$ and $\Tr=\Tr_{K/\F}$. Let $d=g^{(q^2+1)/2}$, so $\bar d=-d$ and $h=d^2=g^{q^2+1}$ generates $K^*$. The form
\[
b(y,z)=\Tr_{E/\F}(d\,y\bar z)=\Tr\bigl(d(y\bar z-\bar yz)\bigr)
\]
is alternating and non-degenerate; let $\Wq(q)$ be its symplectic quadrangle. Multiplication by $g^{q+1}$ scales $b$ by an element of $\F^*$, so it preserves $\Wq(q)$. Its orbits on points are the classes
\[
C_a=\{\langle g^i\rangle: i\equiv a\pmod{q+1}\},\qquad |C_a|=q^2+1,
\]
and $\langle y\rangle\in C_a$ if and only if $\Nm(y)\in h^a\F^*$. These are Ebert's elliptic quadrics partitioning $\PG(3,q)$ \cite{E85}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:C}
Let $q$ be odd.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item Every line of $\PG(3,q)$ meets each class $C_a$ in at most two points.
\item Every totally isotropic line of $\Wq(q)$ meets $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ in exactly two points.
\end{enumerate}
So the $(q+1)/2$ sets $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$, $0\le a\le(q-1)/2$, are $2$-ovoids of $\Wq(q)$ partitioning its points; dually, they are $2$-covers of $\Qf(4,q)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $L=\langle y,z\rangle_\F$ and $u=sy+tz$. Then $\Nm(u)=A_1s^2+\tau st+A_2t^2$ with $A_1=\Nm(y)$, $A_2=\Nm(z)$, $\tau=y\bar z+\bar yz$, all in $K$. Put $\theta=y\bar z-\bar yz$; as $\bar\theta=-\theta$, $\theta=dk_0$ with $k_0\in K$, and
\[
\mathcal D:=\tau^2-4A_1A_2=\theta^2=hk_0^2,\qquad b(y,z)=\Tr(hk_0).
\]
Fix $\omega\in K^*$ with $\omega'=-\omega$. For $\mu\in K^*$ put $\nu=\omega/\mu$; then $\Tr(\nu k)=0$ if and only if $k\in\mu\F$. So $\langle u\rangle$ lies in the class $\{\langle u\rangle:\Nm(u)\in\mu\F^*\}$ if and only if $(s{:}t)$ is a zero of
\[
f_\nu(s,t)=\Tr(\nu A_1)s^2+\Tr(\nu\tau)st+\Tr(\nu A_2)t^2 .
\]
(a) Suppose $f_\nu\equiv0$. Then $A_1,\tau,A_2\in\mu\F$. If $\mathcal D\ne0$, then $h=\mathcal D/k_0^2\in\mu^2\F^*/k_0^2$; but $\F^*\subseteq(K^*)^2$, as $q+1$ is even, so $h$ would be a square in $K^*$, which it is not. If $\mathcal D=0$, then $\Nm(u)=\mu(\alpha_0s^2+\beta_0st+\gamma_0t^2)$ with $\beta_0^2=4\alpha_0\gamma_0$ and $\alpha_0\ne0$ (as $A_1\ne0$), which vanishes at $(s{:}t)=(-\beta_0:2\alpha_0)$, impossible for $u\ne0$. So $f_\nu\not\equiv0$, and it has $1+\chi(\operatorname{disc}(\nu))\le2$ zeros, $\operatorname{disc}(\nu)=\Tr(\nu\tau)^2-4\Tr(\nu A_1)\Tr(\nu A_2)$.

(b) Expanding, $\operatorname{disc}(\nu)=\Tr(\nu^2\mathcal D)+\nu\nu'P$ with $P=2\tau\tau'-4A_1A_2'-4A_1'A_2\in\F$. If $L$ is totally isotropic then $hk_0\in\omega\F$, so $\eta:=h\mathcal D=(hk_0)^2\in\F$, and
\[
\Tr(h^2\nu'^2\mathcal D)=\eta(h\nu'^2+h'\nu^2)=hh'\,\Tr(\nu^2\mathcal D).
\]
Hence $\operatorname{disc}(h\nu')=\Nm_{K/\F}(h)\,\operatorname{disc}(\nu)$. Since $h$ generates $K^*$, $\Nm_{K/\F}(h)$ generates $\F^*$ and is a non-square, so $\chi(\operatorname{disc}(h\nu'))=-\chi(\operatorname{disc}(\nu))$. The class belonging to $h\nu'$ is $\omega/(h\nu')\equiv\mu'/h\equiv(h\mu)^{-1}$ modulo $\F^*$ (using $\mu^{q+1}\in\F^*$); in indices, $a\mapsto q-a$. Scaling $\nu$ by $\F^*$ multiplies the discriminant by a square. So $L$ meets $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ in $(1+\chi(\operatorname{disc}(\nu)))+(1-\chi(\operatorname{disc}(\nu)))=2$ points. The pairing $a\leftrightarrow q-a$ has no fixed point since $q+1$ is even.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For $q$ odd, $\Wq(q)$ is known to have $m$-ovoids for every even $m$ (in particular $2$-ovoids) and $(q+1)/2$-ovoids \cite{BLP09}; see also \cite{CCEM08} for $m$-ovoids of $\Wq(q)$. We have not checked whether the $2$-ovoids $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$, or the partition they form, coincide with those constructions; the proof above is self-contained.\footnote{Note for co-authors: we have confirmed from the abstract of \cite{BLP09} only the existence of $m$-ovoids of $\Wq(q)$ for even $m$ and of $(q+1)/2$-ovoids, $q$ odd. Whether partitions into $2$-ovoids occur there, and whether \cite{CCEM08} has a Singer-group description, still has to be checked.}
The proof only uses that $d^2$ is a non-square in $K$: replacing $d$ by any $\delta_0$ with $\bar\delta_0=-\delta_0$ gives a symplectic form for which $C_a\cup C_{b}$ is a $2$-ovoid when $h^{a+b}\delta_0^2\in\F^*$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{All degrees}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:B2}
Let $q$ be a prime power and $\Delta\ge q+1$. There is a $\Delta$-regular incidence structure of diameter $4$ with
\[
2\Delta(q^2+1)\ \text{elements if $q$ is even or $\Delta-q-1$ is even},\qquad 2\Delta q^2\ \text{elements otherwise.}
\]
In particular $n_b(\Delta,4)\ge2q^2\Delta$ for every prime power $q\le\Delta-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Corollary~\ref{cor:B1}. Let $j=\Delta-q-1$. If $q$ is even, $\Qf(4,q)$ has an ovoid $\cO$ and a spread $\Sigma$; take $w=j\mathbf 1_\cO$ and $\cM=j\Sigma$. If $q$ is odd and $j$ is even, take $w=j\mathbf 1_\cO$ and $\cM$ equal to $j/2$ copies of a $2$-cover of $\Qf(4,q)$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:C}. If $q$ is odd and $j$ is odd, then $\Delta\ge q+2$; in $\AS(q)$, of order $(q-1,q+1)$, take $(\Delta-q)/2$ copies of the weighted $2$-ovoid of Theorem~\ref{thm:A} and $\Delta-q-2$ copies of the spread of fibers.
\end{proof}

At $q=13$, $\Delta=16$ this gives $n_b(16,4)\ge5440$.

\begin{remark}[The case $\Delta=q+3$ inside $\Wq(q)$]\label{rem:C1W}
For $q$ odd and $j=2$ the construction can be written in $\Wq(q)$ itself, without passing to $\Qf(4,q)$. In the model of Proposition~\ref{prop:C}, the lines $yK$ ($y\in E^*$) are totally isotropic, as $b(yk_1,yk_2)=\Tr\bigl(d\,\Nm(y)(k_1k_2-k_1k_2)\bigr)=0$ for $k_1,k_2\in K$, so they form a spread $\Sigma$ (the regular spread). By Proposition~\ref{prop:C}, $w=\mathbf 1_{C_a\cup C_{q-a}}$ is a $2$-ovoid, and $2\Sigma$ is a $2$-cover. So $\Wq(q)[w,2\Sigma,\mathrm{id}]$ has diameter $4$, maximum degree $q+3$ and $2(q^2+1)(q+3)$ elements: the clone of $\langle y\rangle\in C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ is on the $q+1$ totally isotropic lines through $\langle y\rangle$, and the two shadow lines over $yK$ each contain the $q+1$ points $\langle yk\rangle$, $k\in K^*$. Joining the clone $c_i$ to the shadow lines $y_{2i},y_{2i+1}$ (indices modulo $2(q^2+1)$) makes it $(q+3)$-regular (Corollary~\ref{cor:B1}). This is the point--line dual of the $\Qf(4,q)$ description, and it is the form formalised in Lean (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Limits of the method}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:shadowbound}
Let $q$ be odd, $\cO$ an ovoid of $\Qf(4,q)$, $w=\mathbf 1_\cO$, and let $\cS'=\Qf(4,q)[w,\cM,\sigma]$ have maximum degree $q+2$. Then $|\cM|\le q^2-q+2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:B}(b) every old point and every clone serves at most one shadow line. So a point outside $\cO$ is on at most one entry of $\cM$, and a point $o\in\cO$ on at most two (one served by $o$, one by its clone). In particular $\cM$ has no repeated line. If some $o\in\cO$ is on two lines $L_1,L_2\in\cM$, then every other $L\in\cM$ misses $o$, and its unique point $y$ collinear with $o$ is not in $\cO$ (points of an ovoid are pairwise non-collinear). The line $oy$ is not in $\cM$, since $y$ is on at most one line of $\cM$, so $oy$ is one of the $q-1$ lines on $o$ other than $L_1,L_2$, and $y$ is one of its $q$ points other than $o$. Distinct $L$ give distinct $y$, so $|\cM|\le2+q(q-1)$. Otherwise $\cM$ is a partial spread of $\Qf(4,q)$ and $|\cM|\le q^2-q+1$ (Tallini \cite{Tal91}).
\end{proof}

So these structures have at most $2(q+1)(q^2+1)+(q^2+1)+(q^2-q+2)=2q^2(q+2)+q+5$ elements; to beat Corollary~\ref{cor:A1} one needs $|\cM|\ge q^2-2q-2$. For $q=5$ there is a set $\cM$ of $21$ lines with suitable stand-ins (an explicit example, checked by computer), giving $n_b(7,4)\ge359$; we do not know whether $q^2-2q-2$ lines are possible for $q\ge9$. In particular, $q^2+1$ shadow lines (which would give $2(q^2+1)(q+2)$ elements) are impossible for odd $q$.

\begin{remark}[Weighted $3$-ovoids]
Weighted $3$-ovoids of $\Wq(q)$, that is weighted $3$-covers of $\Qf(4,q)$, exist at least for $q=3,5,7$ (found by computer; data in \path{research/p4/d4/sols/w_q3_j3.json}, \path{research/p4/d4/sols/w_q5_j3.json}, \path{research/p4/d4/sols/w3_q7.json}). For $q=5$ there is even a $3$-ovoid, and the $(q+1)/2$-ovoids of \cite{BLP09} are $3$-ovoids exactly when $q=5$. Together with $2$-covers they would remove the case distinction in Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} for $\Delta\ge q+4$.
\end{remark}

\begin{question}
Does $\Wq(q)$ have a weighted $3$-ovoid for every odd $q$? Does $\AS(q)$ have a weighted $3$-ovoid for every odd $q$ (known for $q=5,7$ by computer search; logs \path{research/gq4x/runs/ilp_q5_m3_exact_mult3.log} and \path{research/gq4x/runs/ilp_q7_m3_exact_mult3.log})?
\end{question}

\section{Diameter 6: the split Cayley hexagon}\label{sec:d6}

Throughout this section $q$ is odd (even $q$ is treated in Section~\ref{sec:even}), $\F=\GF(q)$, and $\Hq(q)$ is the split Cayley hexagon on the quadric $\Qf(6,q)$ of equation $X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6=X_3^2$, as in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}. Distances are taken in the incidence graph of $\Hq(q)$; two lines are opposite when they are at distance $6$, and a point is at distance $1$, $3$ or $5$ from a line.

\subsection{The frame}
Let $v=\langle e_1,e_6\rangle$. It is a line of $\Hq(q)$: all six Tits equations involve no Grassmann coordinate $p_{16}$, and $e_1,e_6$ span a totally singular line. Fix $c\in\F$ with $-c$ a non-square. For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in\F^2$ let
\[
\Pi_z\colon\ X_0+cX_4+\alpha X_2+\beta X_5=0,\qquad \Pi=\Pi_{(0,0)},
\]
and let $S_z$ be the set of lines of $\Hq(q)$ contained in $\Pi_z$, $S=S_{(0,0)}$. All $\Pi_z$ contain $v$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:herm}
For every $z\in\F^2$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\Pi_z\cap\Qf(6,q)$ is an elliptic quadric $\Qf^-(5,q)$.
\item Every point of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi_z$ lies on exactly one line of $S_z$. So $|S_z|=q^3+1$, the lines of $S_z$ are pairwise opposite, and $v\in S_z$. \textup{(}$S_z$ is a Hermitian spread.\textup{)}
\item A line of $\Hq(q)$ not in $S_z$ meets $\Pi_z$ in exactly one point.
\item The points of $\Hq(q)$ at distance at most $3$ from $v$ are exactly the points of $\Qf(6,q)$ with $X_2=X_5=0$. Among them only the points of $v$ lie in $\Pi_z$.
\item $S_z\cap S_{z'}=\{v\}$ for $z\ne z'$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) On $\Pi_z$ substitute $X_0=-cX_4-\alpha X_2-\beta X_5$:
\[
X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6-X_3^2=(X_1-\beta X_4)X_5+X_2(X_6-\alpha X_4)-(X_3^2+cX_4^2).
\]
This is the sum of two hyperbolic planes and the binary form $X_3^2+cX_4^2$, which is anisotropic because $-c$ is a non-square. So the form on $\Pi_z$ is non-degenerate of Witt index $2$.

(b) Let $y\in\Pi_z\cap\Qf(6,q)$. The lines of $\Hq(q)$ through $y$ are the lines through $y$ of the totally singular plane $\pi_y$. By (a), $\Pi_z\cap\Qf(6,q)$ contains no totally singular plane, so $\pi_y\not\subseteq\Pi_z$ and $\pi_y\cap\Pi_z$ is a single line through $y$; it is the only line of $\Hq(q)$ on $y$ inside $\Pi_z$. As $\Qf^-(5,q)$ has $(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points, $|S_z|=q^3+1$. Two lines of $S_z$ are not concurrent by uniqueness. If two lines of $S_z$ were at distance $4$, a line $N$ of $\Hq(q)$ would meet both in distinct points; then $N\subseteq\Pi_z$, so $N\in S_z$ is concurrent with them, a contradiction. So they are opposite.

(c) A line not contained in the hyperplane $\Pi_z$ meets it in one point.

(d) Let $y=ae_1+be_6$ be a point of $v$ and let $u$ be a point with $yu$ a line of $\Hq(q)$. The Tits equations $p_{12}=p_{34}$ and $p_{65}=p_{30}$ and the polarity condition give $au_2=0$, $bu_5=0$ and $au_5+bu_2=0$, hence $u_2=u_5=0$. So the points at distance at most $3$ from $v$ satisfy $X_2=X_5=0$. Conversely, $\Qf(6,q)\cap\{X_2=X_5=0\}$ is $X_0X_4=X_3^2$, a cone with vertex $v$ over a conic; it has $(q+1)+(q+1)q^2$ points, which is the number of points at distance at most $3$ from $v$ ($q+1$ on $v$, and $q$ further points on each of the $q(q+1)$ other lines meeting $v$). If such a point also lies in $\Pi_z$, then $X_0=-cX_4$ and $-cX_4^2=X_3^2$, so $X_0=X_3=X_4=0$ and the point is on $v$.

(e) Let $\ell\in S_z\cap S_{z'}$, $\ell\ne v$. On $\ell$, $(\alpha-\alpha')X_2+(\beta-\beta')X_5=0$, so the linear map $\ell\to\F^2$, $X\mapsto(X_2,X_5)$, has a non-trivial kernel, and $\ell$ has a point with $X_2=X_5=0$. By (d) that point is at distance at most $3$ from $v$. But $\ell$ and $v$ are opposite by (b), so every point of $\ell$ is at distance $5$ from $v$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Delorme's doubling and whiskers}
Let $\cP(v)$ be the set of points of $v$, $\cL_v$ the set of lines $\ne v$ meeting $v$, and $\cP_\Pi$ the set of points of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi$ (the points on lines of $S$). A point is \emph{free} if it is not in $\Pi$ and is at distance $5$ from $v$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(d) the points of $\Pi$ other than those of $v$ are at distance $5$ from $v$, so there are
\[
(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)-(q+1)-q^2(q+1)-q^3(q+1)=q^3(q^2-1)
\]
free points.

\begin{definition}[Whiskers]
A \emph{whisker set} is a set $W$ of lines of $\Hq(q)$, each opposite $v$ and not in $S$, such that no free point is on two lines of $W$. It is \emph{exact} if every free point is on exactly one line of $W$.
\end{definition}

A line $\ell$ opposite $v$ and not in $S$ meets $\Pi$ in one point, which lies on a unique line of $S\setminus\{v\}$, and its other $q$ points are free. So $|W|\le q^3(q^2-1)/q=q^2(q^2-1)$, with equality if and only if $W$ is exact.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:whisk}
Let $W$ be a whisker set and $m\ge2$. Put
\[
\cA_2=\{v\},\qquad \cA_1=\{v\}\cup\cP(v)\cup S,\qquad \cA_0=\cA_1\cup\cP_\Pi\cup\cL_v\cup W .
\]
Then $\cA$ satisfies \ref{L0}, \ref{L1} and \textup{(C$_6$)}. Hence $\Dbl{\Hq(q)}{m}$ has maximum degree $q+m$, diameter $6$, and
\[
2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(q^4+2q^3+q^2+2q+2+|W|+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr)
\]
elements.
\end{theorem}

For $W=\emptyset$ this is Delorme's construction \cite{D85a,D85b}; at $m=2$ it gives $2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+q^4+4q^3+q^2+4q+10$ elements, the previous table values $8992$, $142464$, $374452$, $842048$ at $\Delta=7,11,13,15$, and at $q=13$, $m=3$ the value $884062$ at $\Delta=16$.

\begin{proof}
\ref{L1}, $i=1$: the elements incident with $v$ are its points, which are in $\cA_1$; a point of $v$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_2$, a line of $S\setminus\{v\}$ with none.
\ref{L1}, $i=0$: the elements incident with $\cA_1$ are the points of $v$, the lines of $\cL_v$, and the points on lines of $S$, all in $\cA_0$. A line of $\cL_v$ meets $v$ in one point and contains no other point of $\cA_1$. A point of $\cP_\Pi\setminus\cP(v)$ is on exactly one line of $S$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(b)) and not on $v$. A line of $W$ contains no point of $v$, being opposite $v$.
\ref{L0}: a point outside $\cA_0$ is a point off $\Pi$. If it is at distance $3$ from $v$, it is on exactly one line of $\cL_v$ (two would close a circuit of length at most $6$) and on no line of $W$ (that line would be at distance at most $4$ from $v$). If it is free, it is on no line of $\cL_v$ and on at most one line of $W$. A line outside $\cA_0$ is not in $\Pi$, so it meets $\Pi$ in one point, and that point is its only point in $\cA_0$.
\textup{(C$_6$)}: a line of $\cL_v$ contains no point of $\Pi$ other than its point on $v$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(d)), and a line of $W$ contains exactly one point of $\Pi$, not on $v$. So the components of the substructure on $\cA_0$ are
\[
\{v\}\cup\cP(v)\cup\cL_v\qquad\text{and}\qquad \{n\}\cup\cP(n)\cup\{w\in W: w\text{ meets }n\}\quad(n\in S\setminus\{v\}).
\]
Both are trees of diameter at most $4$: the first is the ball of radius $2$ about $v$, the second consists of $n$, its points, and whiskers hanging from them. Lemma~\ref{lem:hat} gives \textup{(C$_6$)}.
Now Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme} applies. The diameter is exactly $6$ by \eqref{eq:proj}, because there are opposite elements of level $-1$: a free point $p$ (level $-1$) has $q^5$ opposite points, and only $(q+1)(q^3+1)<q^5$ points lie in $\cA_0$. For the count: $\cA_2=\{v\}$; $\cA_1$ consists of $q^3+1$ lines and $q+1$ points, $q^3+q+2$ elements; and $\cA_0$ consists of the $q^3+1$ lines of $S$, the $q^2+q$ lines of $\cL_v$, the $|W|$ whiskers and the $(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points of $\Pi$, which is $q^4+2q^3+q^2+2q+2+|W|$ elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The incidences of every version]\label{rem:versions}
By Definition~\ref{def:doubling}, with $a,b,c\in[m]$, the versions in Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk} are incident as follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $v^{abc}$: the points $p^{ab}$, $p\I v$ (degree $q+1$).
\item $p^{ab}$, $p\I v$: the lines $v^{ab1},\dots,v^{abm}$, and $L^a$ for the $q$ lines $L\in\cL_v$ on $p$ (degree $q+m$).
\item $n^{ab}$, $n\in S\setminus\{v\}$: the points $p^a$, $p\I n$ (degree $q+1$).
\item $p^a$, $p\in\cP_\Pi\setminus\cP(v)$: $n^{a1},\dots,n^{am}$ for the line $n\in S$ on $p$; $w^a$ for every $w\in W$ on $p$; and every other line on $p$ (degree $q+m$).
\item $L^a$, $L\in\cL_v$: $p^{a1},\dots,p^{am}$ for $p=L\cap v$, and the $q$ other points of $L$ (degree $q+m$).
\item $w^a$, $w\in W$: $p^a$ for $p=w\cap\Pi$, and the $q$ free points of $w$ (degree $q+1$).
\item a point $r\notin\Pi$: all $m$ versions of the line of $\cL_v\cup W$ on $r$, if there is one, and every other line on $r$ (degree $q+m$, or $q+1$ if $r$ is free and on no line of $W$).
\item a line $N\notin\cA_0$: all $m$ versions of its point in $\Pi$, which is not on $v$, and its $q$ other points (degree $q+m$).
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{An exact whisker set for every odd \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}\label{sec:exactodd}
For a point $X$ of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi$, not on $v$, put
\[
\nu(X)=\frac{X_2X_3-X_4X_5}{cX_2^2+X_5^2}.
\]
This is well defined: the denominator vanishes only when $X_2=X_5=0$ (as $-c$ is a non-square), and by Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(d) such a point of $\Pi$ lies on $v$. Let
\[
G(a,b)=(c_0a+c_1b)(a^2+cb^2)-b^3,\qquad F(z)=\frac{G(\alpha,\beta)}{\alpha^2+c\beta^2}\quad(z=(\alpha,\beta)\ne0),
\]
where $c_0,c_1\in\F$ are chosen so that $G$ has no zero on $\F^2\setminus\{0\}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:G}
Such $c_0,c_1$ exist for every odd $q$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$G(a,0)=c_0a^3$, so we need $c_0\ne0$. For $b\ne0$ put $\alpha=a/b$: $G(a,b)=0$ if and only if $c_0\alpha+c_1=1/(\alpha^2+c)$ (note $\alpha^2+c\ne0$). For fixed $\alpha$ this is a line in the $(c_0,c_1)$-plane with $q-1$ points having $c_0\ne0$. The lines for $\alpha=0$ and $\alpha=1$ meet at $c_0=1/(1+c)-1/c=-1/(c(1+c))\ne0$ (note $c\ne-1$ as $-c$ is a non-square). So the $q$ lines cover fewer than $q(q-1)$ of the points with $c_0\ne0$, and some $(c_0,c_1)$ is good.
\end{proof}

For instance, the following choices are valid (with $\F_9=\F_3[i]$, $i^2=-1$): $(c;c_0,c_1)=(1;1,2)$ for $q=3$, $(2;1,0)$ for $q=5$, $(1;1,0)$ for $q=7$, $(1+i;i,2i)$ for $q=9$, $(1;1,3)$ for $q=11$, $(2;1,1)$ for $q=13$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:exact}
Let $q$ be odd, $-c$ a non-square, and $c_0,c_1$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:G}. Then
\[
W=\bigl\{\,\ell\in S_z\setminus\{v\}\;:\; z\in\F^2\setminus\{0\},\ \nu(\ell\cap\Pi)=F(z)\,\bigr\}
\]
is an exact whisker set: every free point lies on exactly one line of $W$, and $|W|=q^2(q^2-1)$.
\end{theorem}

Here $\ell\cap\Pi$ is a single point not on $v$: $\ell\subseteq\Pi_z$, $\ell\ne v$, so $\ell\notin S$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(e), and $\ell$ is opposite $v$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(b).

The proof rests on a complex structure on each hyperplane $\Pi_z$ and on an identity that it yields. We use the following notation. A point of $\Pi_z$, $z=(\alpha,\beta)$, is written $x=(x_1,\dots,x_6)$, meaning $(X_0,\dots,X_6)=(-cx_4-\alpha x_2-\beta x_5,\,x_1,\dots,x_6)$. On $\Pi_z$ the quadratic form is
\[
Q_z(x)=x_1x_5+x_2x_6-x_3^2-cx_4^2-\alpha x_2x_4-\beta x_4x_5 ,
\]
with polar form $B_z$. Put $\mathrm{num}(y)=y_2y_3-y_4y_5$ and $\mathrm{den}(y)=cy_2^2+y_5^2$, so that $\nu=\mathrm{num}/\mathrm{den}$ on $\Pi$. Define the linear map $J_z$ of $\Pi_z$ by
\[
J_zx=\bigl(-\beta x_3+\alpha x_4-x_6,\ x_5,\ -cx_4-\beta x_5,\ x_3+\beta x_2,\ -cx_2,\ cx_1+\alpha\beta x_2+\alpha x_3+c\beta x_4+\beta^2x_5\bigr);
\]
for $z=0$ this is $J_0x=(-x_6,x_5,-cx_4,x_3,-cx_2,cx_1)$.

\begin{lemma}[Complex structure]\label{lem:J}
For every $z\in\F^2$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $J_z^2=-c$, $Q_z(J_zx)=cQ_z(x)$ and $B_z(x,J_zx)=0$.
\item The six differences $p_{12}-p_{34}$, $p_{54}-p_{32}$, $p_{20}-p_{35}$, $p_{65}-p_{30}$, $p_{01}-p_{36}$, $p_{46}-p_{31}$ of the Grassmann coordinates of $x\wedge J_zx$ are equal to $Q_z(x)$ times $1,\,0,\,0,\,-c,\,\alpha,\,-\beta$ respectively.
\item For every point $x$ of $\Qf(6,q)\cap\Pi_z$, the line $\langle x,J_zx\rangle$ is the line of $S_z$ through $x$.
\item $\mathrm{num}(J_0x)=c\,\mathrm{num}(x)$, $\mathrm{den}(J_0x)=c\,\mathrm{den}(x)$, and the polar forms of $\mathrm{num}$ and $\mathrm{den}$ vanish on $(x,J_0x)$. Consequently $\nu$ is constant on every line of $S\setminus\{v\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a), (b) are polynomial identities of degree $2$ in $x$, checked by direct expansion (we also checked them by computer algebra). For $z=0$, for instance, $Q_0(J_0x)=cx_2x_6+cx_1x_5-c^2x_4^2-cx_3^2=cQ_0(x)$, and $p_{12}=x_1x_5+x_2x_6$, $p_{34}=x_3^2+cx_4^2$, so $p_{12}-p_{34}=Q_0(x)$.

(c) As $-c$ is a non-square, $J_z$ has no eigenvector, so $x$ and $J_zx$ span a line. If $x$ is on the quadric, then $Q_z(x)=0$, hence by (a) every point of the line is on the quadric, and by (b) its Grassmann coordinates satisfy Tits' equations. So it is a line of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi_z$ through $x$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(b) it is the line of $S_z$ through $x$.

(d) The first assertions are direct: $\mathrm{num}(J_0x)=x_5(-cx_4)-x_3(-cx_2)=c\,\mathrm{num}(x)$, $\mathrm{den}(J_0x)=cx_5^2+c^2x_2^2=c\,\mathrm{den}(x)$, and the polar forms are $x_2y_3+x_3y_2-x_4y_5-x_5y_4$ and $2cx_2y_2+2x_5y_5$, which vanish for $y=J_0x$. By (c) every line $n\in S\setminus\{v\}$ is $\{ax+bJ_0x\}$ for any point $x$ of $n$, and $\mathrm{num}(ax+bJ_0x)=(a^2+cb^2)\mathrm{num}(x)$, $\mathrm{den}(ax+bJ_0x)=(a^2+cb^2)\mathrm{den}(x)$. The factor $a^2+cb^2$ is non-zero because $-c$ is a non-square. So $\nu$ is constant on $n$.
\end{proof}

In particular the condition defining $W$ depends only on the line of $S$ that $\ell$ meets: $\nu$ is a function on the Hermitian spread $S\setminus\{v\}$.

Let $p$ be a free point. By Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(d), $(p_2,p_5)\ne(0,0)$, and since $p\notin\Pi$ we may normalize $p_0+cp_4=1$. Then $p\in\Pi_z$ if and only if $z$ lies on the affine line
\[
A_p=\{(\alpha,\beta):\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=-1\}\subseteq\F^2\setminus\{0\},
\]
which has $q$ points and direction $(p_5,-p_2)$. For $z\in A_p$ let $\ell_p(z)$ be the line of $S_z$ through $p$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:herm}(b)) and $X(z)=\ell_p(z)\cap\Pi$.

\begin{lemma}[Key identity]\label{lem:key}
For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in A_p$,
\[
\Phi_p(z):=\nu(X(z))+\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+c\beta^2}=\frac{p_2p_3-p_4p_5+\beta\,p_2^2}{cp_2^2+p_5^2}.
\]
In particular $\Phi_p$ is the restriction to $A_p$ of an affine function, and its increment along $(p_5,-p_2)$ is $-p_2^3/(cp_2^2+p_5^2)$. Explicitly, if $p_5\ne0$ and $z=(sp_5,\,-1/p_5-sp_2)$, then
\[
\Phi_p(z)=-\frac{p_2^3p_5\,s+p_2^2-p_2p_3p_5+p_4p_5^2}{p_5(cp_2^2+p_5^2)};
\]
if $p_5=0$ and $z=(-1/p_2,\,s)$, then $\Phi_p(z)=(p_2s+p_3)/(cp_2)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $\Theta=cp_2^2+p_5^2\ne0$ and $\gamma=-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2$. Write $p=(p_1,\dots,p_6)$ in the coordinates of $\Pi_z$; then $\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=-1$. For $y\in\Pi_z$ the quantity $\lambda(y)=y_0+cy_4$ equals $-\alpha y_2-\beta y_5$, and $\Pi$ is the set where it vanishes. We have $\lambda(p)=1$ and, by the formula for $J_z$, $\lambda(J_zp)=-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2=\gamma$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:J}(c), $\ell_p(z)=\langle p,J_zp\rangle$, so $X(z)=\gamma p-J_zp$. Its coordinates are
\[
X_2=\gamma p_2-p_5,\quad X_3=\gamma p_3+cp_4+\beta p_5,\quad X_4=\gamma p_4-p_3-\beta p_2,\quad X_5=\gamma p_5+cp_2 .
\]
Direct expansion gives
\[
\mathrm{den}(X)=(\gamma^2+c)\,\Theta,\qquad \mathrm{num}(X)=(\gamma^2+c)(p_2p_3-p_4p_5)+\beta\bigl(2\gamma p_2p_5+cp_2^2-p_5^2\bigr).
\]
Solving $\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=-1$, $-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2=\gamma$ (determinant $\Theta$) gives $\alpha=-(cp_2+\gamma p_5)/\Theta$, $\beta=(\gamma p_2-p_5)/\Theta$, and therefore $\alpha^2+c\beta^2=(\gamma^2+c)/\Theta$. Here $\gamma^2+c\ne0$ since $-c$ is a non-square (this also shows $\mathrm{den}(X)\ne0$). Hence
\[
\nu(X)=\frac{p_2p_3-p_4p_5}{\Theta}+\frac{\beta\,(2\gamma p_2p_5+cp_2^2-p_5^2)}{(\gamma^2+c)\Theta},\qquad
\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+c\beta^2}=\frac{\beta\,(\gamma p_2-p_5)^2}{(\gamma^2+c)\Theta},
\]
using $\beta^2\Theta^2=(\gamma p_2-p_5)^2$. The sum of the two numerators multiplying $\beta/((\gamma^2+c)\Theta)$ is $2\gamma p_2p_5+cp_2^2-p_5^2+\gamma^2p_2^2-2\gamma p_2p_5+p_5^2=(\gamma^2+c)p_2^2$, which gives the stated formula. Along $(p_5,-p_2)$ the coordinate $\beta$ changes by $-p_2$. The two explicit formulas follow by inserting $\beta=-1/p_5-sp_2$ and $\beta=s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The identities in Lemmas~\ref{lem:J} and~\ref{lem:key} are polynomial identities with integer coefficients, so they hold in every odd characteristic. We also verified them symbolically, and, for $q=3,5,7$, the construction of $W$ itself on all free points of $\Hq(q)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}]
Let $p$ be a free point, normalized as above. The lines of $\bigcup_{z\ne0}S_z\setminus\{v\}$ through $p$ are exactly the lines $\ell_p(z)$, $z\in A_p$, and these $q$ lines are distinct: a common line would lie in $S_z\cap S_{z'}=\{v\}$. Since $F(z)=c_0\alpha+c_1\beta-\beta^3/(\alpha^2+c\beta^2)$, we have $\ell_p(z)\in W$ if and only if
\[
\Phi_p(z)-(c_0\alpha+c_1\beta)=0 .
\]
The left side is affine on $A_p$ with increment
\[
-\frac{p_2^3}{cp_2^2+p_5^2}-(c_0p_5-c_1p_2)=-\frac{G(p_5,-p_2)}{cp_2^2+p_5^2}\ne0
\]
along $(p_5,-p_2)$, so it vanishes at exactly one of the $q$ points of $A_p$. Hence $p$ is on exactly one line of $W$. The lines of $W$ are opposite $v$ and not in $S$, so $W$ is an exact whisker set.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:d6}
For every odd prime power $q$ and every $m\ge2$ \textup{(}even $q$: see Corollary~\ref{cor:even6}\textup{)},
\[
n_b(q+m,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(2q^4+2q^3+2q+2+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr).
\]
In particular $n_b(q+2,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+2q^4+4q^3+4q+10$ and $n_b(q+3,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+4q^4+10q^3+10q+34$.
\end{corollary}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:count} an exact whisker set is the most this family can give: with $W$ exact, every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_0$, and equality $q|\cA_0|+2k=2N$ holds, with $k=q^3+1$ components.

\subsection{Regular versions}
The structure of Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk} with $W$ exact has equally many points and lines in $\cA_0$, but more lines than points in $\cA_1$ and $\cA_2$; it has $(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$ more lines than points. We remove repeated lines and then add flags.

\begin{lemma}[Repeated lines]\label{lem:twin}
If two lines of an incidence structure have the same set of points, deleting one of them changes no distance between the remaining elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A shortest path through the deleted line $L$ can pass through its twin $L'$ instead; it does not already contain $L'$, since then it could be shortened.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Completion]\label{lem:complete}
Let $\cS$ be a finite incidence structure with $\Delta(\cS)\le\Delta$, such that every element has degree at least $\Delta-r$, and $\sum_{p}(\Delta-\deg p)=\sum_{L}(\Delta-\deg L)=:T$. If $T>2r\Delta$, there is a set $J$ of anti-flags such that $(\cP,\cL,\I\cup J)$ is $\Delta$-regular.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose a multiset $J$ of point--line pairs in which each element $x$ occurs $\Delta-\deg x$ times (possible as the sums agree). Call an entry \emph{bad} if it is a flag of $\cS$ or a repeated pair, and choose $J$ with the fewest bad entries. Suppose $(p,L)$ is bad. In $\I\cup J$, the line $L$ is joined to at most $\Delta$ points and each of them occurs in at most $r$ entries of $J$; likewise for $p$. So at most $2r\Delta$ entries $(p',L')$ of $J$ have $p'$ joined to $L$ or $L'$ joined to $p$, and some entry $(p',L')$ has neither. Replacing $(p,L),(p',L')$ by $(p,L'),(p',L)$ removes a bad entry and creates none. So $J$ has no bad entries.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:R}
For every odd prime power $q$ and every $m\ge2$ \textup{(}even $q$: see Corollary~\ref{cor:even6}\textup{)} there is a $(q+m)$-regular incidence structure of diameter at most $6$ with
\[
2(q+1)\bigl(q^4+q^2+1+(m-1)(q^3+m+1)\bigr)
\]
elements; for $m=2$ this is $2(q+1)(q^4+q^3+q^2+4)$, and for $m=3$ it is $2(q+1)(q^4+2q^3+q^2+9)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start from $\Dbl{\Hq(q)}{m}$ with $W$ exact. For $n\in S\setminus\{v\}$ (level $1$) and $a\in[m]$, the $m$ versions $n^{a1},\dots,n^{am}$ all have the point set $\{p^a:p\I n\}$; for $a,b\in[m]$ the versions $v^{ab1},\dots,v^{abm}$ all have the point set $\{p^{ab}:p\I v\}$. So there are $mq^3+m^2$ classes of $m$ repeated lines. Fix a set $R$ of $q$ lines of $S\setminus\{v\}$ and delete the lines $v^{abc}$ with $c\ge2$ and the lines $n^{ab}$ with $b\ge2$ and $n\notin R$: these are $(m-1)m^2+(m-1)m(q^3-q)=(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$ lines, at most $m-1$ from each class. By Lemma~\ref{lem:twin} no distance increases, and points and lines are now equal in number. Every point of $\Pi$ is on exactly one line of $S$, so each point loses at most $m-1$ lines. In $\Dbl{\Hq(q)}{m}$ every version has degree between $q+1$ and $q+m$; after the deletion this still holds, so every deficit is at most $r=m-1$. The deficit sum $T$ is at least that of the $m|W|=mq^2(q^2-1)$ whisker versions, each of degree $q+1$, so $T\ge(m-1)mq^2(q^2-1)>2(m-1)(q+m)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:complete} completes the structure; adding flags cannot increase distances. The number of points is $(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl((q+1)(q^3+1)+m(q+1)\bigr)$, which gives the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is explicit]
After the deletion every incidence is explicit (Remark~\ref{rem:versions}). The flags added by Lemma~\ref{lem:complete} are not given by a formula: the lemma proves that they exist, at the elements of degree $q+1$ ($m-1$ at each), and the program \path{research/p4/regular/regularize.py} finds them by a seeded swap procedure. Neither the maximum degree nor the diameter depends on that choice.
\end{remark}

At $q=5,9,11,13$ and $m=2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:R} gives $9348$, $147500$, $386328$, $866068$ elements, and at $q=13$, $m=3$ it gives $927724$. For these parameters the number of elements exceeds $M_b(q+m,5)$, so the diameter is exactly $6$.

\section{Even \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}\label{sec:even}

Theorems~\ref{thm:A}, \ref{thm:exact} and~\ref{thm:R} and Corollaries~\ref{cor:A1} and~\ref{cor:d6} were stated for odd $q$. Here $q=2^e$. What is proved for even $q$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{$D=4$, every even $q$.} Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} already covers all prime powers: for even $q$ it uses an ovoid and a spread of $\Qf(4,q)$ and gives $2\Delta(q^2+1)$ elements for every $\Delta\ge q+1$ (Section~\ref{sec:even4}). Theorem~\ref{thm:A} and Corollary~\ref{cor:A1} (the quadrangle $\AS(q)$) are for odd $q$ only and are not needed.
\item \emph{$D=6$, even $q\ge4$.} An explicit exact whisker set (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2}); hence Corollary~\ref{cor:d6} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} hold for these $q$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:even6}).
\item \emph{$D=6$, $q=2$.} The formula for the whisker set fails (Lemma~\ref{lem:G2}). We use an exact whisker set of $12$ lines found by computer (an exact cover of the free points by an integer program). So for $q=2$ Corollary~\ref{cor:d6} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} are computer-assisted; the resulting graphs were also built and checked directly (Table~\ref{tab:even}).
\end{itemize}
Throughout, $\F=\GF(q)$, $\Tr$ is the absolute trace of $\F$, and we fix $\delta\in\F$ with $\Tr(\delta)=1$, so that $t^2+t+\delta$ is irreducible over $\F$ and the binary form $a^2+ab+\delta b^2$ is anisotropic.

\subsection{Diameter 4}\label{sec:even4}
For $q$ even nothing new is needed: $\Qf(4,q)$ has both ovoids and spreads \cite[Ch.~3]{PT}, and Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} already gives a $\Delta$-regular structure with $2\Delta(q^2+1)$ elements for every $\Delta\ge q+1$. In the language of doubling (Section~\ref{sec:doubling}): if $\cO$ is an ovoid and $\Sigma$ a spread, every line of $\Sigma$ contains exactly one point of $\cO$, so $\cA_0=\cO\cup\Sigma$ (one level) has $q^2+1$ components, each a flag, and satisfies \ref{L0} and \textup{(C$_4$)}. Doubling gives maximum degree $q+2$, diameter $4$ and $2(q+1)(q^2+1)+2(q^2+1)=2(q^2+1)(q+2)$ elements; the elements of $\cA_0$ and their copies have degree $q+1$, and adding the anti-flags $(p,L')$ and $(p',L)$ for each flag $(p,L)$ of $\cA_0$ makes the structure $(q+2)$-regular. The structure of Corollary~\ref{cor:B1} with $w=\mathbf 1_\cO$, $\cM=\Sigma$ has the same order. Every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_0$, so the counting bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:count} holds with equality for $q^2+1$ components.

At $\Delta=q+2$ this beats the value $2q^2(q+2)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:A} by $2(q+2)$, so for even $q$ the quadrangle $\AS(q)$ is not needed. For $q=2,4,8,16$ these graphs (and the ones with $j=2$ copies of $\cO$ and of $\Sigma$) were built and checked; see Table~\ref{tab:even}. They do not improve Table~\ref{tab:values}: when $q\in\{2,4,8,16\}$, $q+1$ is a prime power, so $\Delta=q+2$ is a Moore degree, and at $\Delta=q+3$ the odd prime power $q+1$ gives more. The first degrees at which an even $q$ is the best choice in Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} are $\Delta=34,35,36,37$ ($q=32$; for example $n_b(34,4)\ge 2\cdot1025\cdot34=69700$, against $65416$ from $q=31$), outside the range of the table.

\subsection{Diameter 6: the frame in characteristic 2}\label{sec:even6}
The split Cayley hexagon $\Hq(q)$ is defined for all $q$ by the same quadric and the same six Tits equations; in characteristic $2$ the signs disappear and the quadric $\Qf(6,q)\colon X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6+X_3^2=0$ has a nucleus, the point $e_3$. The hyperplanes $X_0+cX_4+\dots=0$ of Section~\ref{sec:d6} contain $e_3$ and so meet $\Qf(6,q)$ in a cone; instead we take, for $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in\F^2$,
\[
\Pi_z\colon\ X_3=X_0+\delta X_4+\alpha X_2+\beta X_5,\qquad \Pi=\Pi_{(0,0)},
\]
and $S_z$ the set of lines of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi_z$. Again $v=\langle e_1,e_6\rangle\subseteq\Pi_z$ for all $z$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:herm2}
Lemma~\ref{lem:herm} holds verbatim for even $q$ with these $\Pi_z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) On $\Pi_z$,
\[
X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6+X_3^2=\bigl(X_0^2+X_0X_4+\delta^2X_4^2\bigr)+(X_1+\beta^2X_5)X_5+X_2(X_6+\alpha^2X_2).
\]
The first term is anisotropic since $\Tr(\delta^2)=\Tr(\delta)=1$, and $\Pi_z$ misses the nucleus $e_3$, so $\Pi_z\cap\Qf(6,q)$ is a non-degenerate quadric of Witt index $2$, an elliptic quadric $\Qf^-(5,q)$. (b), (c) and (e) are proved as before. (d) The Tits equations give $X_2=X_5=0$ exactly as before (only signs change); the cone $X_0X_4=X_3^2$ meets $\Pi_z$ where $X_0^2+X_0X_4+\delta^2X_4^2=0$, that is $X_0=X_4=0$, hence $X_3=0$, on $v$.
\end{proof}

So Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk} (Delorme's doubling with whiskers) holds for even $q$ without change. Free points are defined as before, there are $q^3(q^2-1)$ of them, and an exact whisker set has $q^2(q^2-1)$ lines.

\subsection{An exact whisker set for even \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}
For a point $X$ of $\Hq(q)$ in $\Pi$, not on $v$, put
\[
\nu(X)=\frac{X_2X_3+X_2X_4+X_4X_5}{\delta X_2^2+X_2X_5+X_5^2}.
\]
The denominator is anisotropic, and vanishes only if $X_2=X_5=0$, that is (Lemma~\ref{lem:herm2}) only on $v$. For $c_0,c_1\in\F$ let
\[
G(a,b)=(c_0a+c_1b)(a^2+ab+\delta b^2)+b^3,\qquad F(z)=\frac{G(\alpha,\beta)}{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2}\quad(z=(\alpha,\beta)\ne0).
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:G2}
There are $c_0,c_1\in\F$ such that $G$ has no zero on $\F^2\setminus\{0\}$ if and only if $q\ge4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$G(a,0)=c_0a^3$, so $c_0\ne0$ is needed. For $b\ne0$ and $t=a/b$, $G(a,b)=0$ if and only if $c_0t+c_1=r(t):=1/(t^2+t+\delta)$. For fixed $t$ this is a line in the $(c_0,c_1)$-plane with $q-1$ points having $c_0\ne0$. If $q\ge4$, take $\omega\notin\{0,1\}$; then $r(\omega)\ne r(0)$, so the lines for $t=0$ and $t=\omega$ meet at $c_0=(r(0)+r(\omega))/\omega\ne0$, and the $q$ lines cover fewer than $q(q-1)$ points with $c_0\ne0$. If $q=2$, then $r(0)=r(1)=1$ and the lines $c_1=1$, $c_0+c_1=1$ contain both points with $c_0=1$.
\end{proof}

We use coordinates $x=(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_4,x_5,x_6)$ on $\Pi_z$, with $X_3=x_0+\delta x_4+\alpha x_2+\beta x_5$, and define the linear map $J_z$ of $\Pi_z$ by
\[
\begin{aligned}
J_zx=\bigl(\,&(\delta+1)x_0+\delta\alpha x_2+\delta^2x_4+(\alpha+\delta\beta)x_5,\ \ \beta x_0+x_1+\alpha\beta x_2+(\alpha+\delta\beta)x_4+\beta^2x_5+x_6,\\
&x_2+x_5,\ \ x_0+(\alpha+\beta)x_2+\delta x_4+\beta x_5,\ \ \delta x_2,\ \ (\alpha+\beta)x_0+\delta x_1+\alpha^2x_2+\delta\alpha x_4+\alpha\beta x_5\,\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{lemma}[Complex structure, characteristic 2]\label{lem:J2}
For every $z\in\F^2$, with $Q$ the quadratic form and $B$ its polar form:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $J_z^2+J_z+\delta=0$, $Q(J_zx)=\delta Q(x)$ and $B(x,J_zx)=Q(x)$.
\item The six Tits differences for $x\wedge J_zx$ are $Q(x)$ times $1,0,0,\delta,\alpha,\beta$.
\item For every point $x$ of $\Qf(6,q)\cap\Pi_z$ the line $\langle x,J_zx\rangle$ is the line of $S_z$ on $x$.
\item With $\mathrm{num}=X_2X_3+X_2X_4+X_4X_5$ and $\mathrm{den}=\delta X_2^2+X_2X_5+X_5^2$ on $\Pi$,
\[
\mathrm{num}(sx+tJ_0x)=(s^2+st+\delta t^2)\,\mathrm{num}(x),
\]
and likewise for $\mathrm{den}$. So $\nu$ is constant on every line of $S\setminus\{v\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a), (b), (d) are polynomial identities over $\mathbb{F}_2[\delta,\alpha,\beta,x]$, checked by direct expansion (and by computer algebra, \path{research/evenq/proof_J.py}). (c) follows from (a), (b) and Lemma~\ref{lem:herm2}(b) as in Lemma~\ref{lem:J}: $J_z$ has no eigenvector because $t^2+t+\delta$ is irreducible, and on a singular $x$ the line $\langle x,J_zx\rangle$ is totally singular with Grassmann coordinates satisfying Tits' equations. In (d), $s^2+st+\delta t^2\ne0$ for $(s,t)\ne0$.
\end{proof}

Let $p$ be a free point. Then $(p_2,p_5)\ne(0,0)$, and we normalize $p_0+p_3+\delta p_4=1$. Now $p\in\Pi_z$ if and only if $z$ lies on the affine line $A_p\colon\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=1$, which has direction $(p_5,p_2)$. For $z\in A_p$ let $X(z)$ be the point of $\Pi$ on the line of $S_z$ through $p$.

\begin{lemma}[Key identity, characteristic 2]\label{lem:key2}
For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in A_p$,
\[
\Phi_p(z):=\nu(X(z))+\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2}=\frac{p_2p_3+p_2p_4+p_4p_5+\beta p_2^2}{\delta p_2^2+p_2p_5+p_5^2}.
\]
So $\Phi_p$ is affine on $A_p$, with increment $p_2^3/(\delta p_2^2+p_2p_5+p_5^2)$ along $(p_5,p_2)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $\Theta=\delta p_2^2+p_2p_5+p_5^2\ne0$ and $\lambda(y)=y_0+y_3+\delta y_4$, so that $\Pi=\{\lambda=0\}$ and $\lambda=\alpha y_2+\beta y_5$ on $\Pi_z$. Then $\lambda(p)=1$ and $\gamma:=\lambda(J_zp)=\alpha(p_2+p_5)+\delta\beta p_2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:J2}(c) the line of $S_z$ on $p$ is $\langle p,J_zp\rangle$, so $X(z)=\gamma p+J_zp$ (then $\lambda(X)=2\gamma=0$). In particular $X_2=(\gamma+1)p_2+p_5$ and $X_5=\delta p_2+\gamma p_5$. Direct expansion gives
\[
\mathrm{den}(X)=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\,\Theta,\qquad
\mathrm{num}(X)=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)(p_2p_3+p_2p_4+p_4p_5)+\beta\bigl((\gamma+\delta+1)p_2^2+p_5^2\bigr),
\]
and $\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta\ne0$. Solving $\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=1$, $\alpha(p_2+p_5)+\delta\beta p_2=\gamma$ (determinant $\Theta$) gives $\alpha=(\delta p_2+\gamma p_5)/\Theta$, $\beta=((\gamma+1)p_2+p_5)/\Theta$, hence $\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)/\Theta$ and
\[
\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2}=\frac{\beta\bigl((\gamma+1)p_2+p_5\bigr)^2}{(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\Theta}.
\]
The $\beta$-terms add up to $\beta\bigl((\gamma+\delta+1)+(\gamma+1)^2\bigr)p_2^2/((\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\Theta)=\beta p_2^2/\Theta$. Along $(p_5,p_2)$, $\beta$ changes by $p_2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:exact2}
Let $q\ge4$ be even and $c_0,c_1$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:G2}. Then
\[
W=\bigl\{\,\ell\in S_z\setminus\{v\}\;:\;z\in\F^2\setminus\{0\},\ \nu(\ell\cap\Pi)=F(z)\,\bigr\}
\]
is an exact whisker set, so $|W|=q^2(q^2-1)$. For $q=2$ the formula gives none, since no $(c_0,c_1)$ is good (Lemma~\ref{lem:G2}); but an exact whisker set of $12=q^2(q^2-1)$ lines exists, found by computer (data: \path{research/evenq/whiskers_raw_q2.json}, program \path{research/evenq/explore.py}, mode \texttt{raw}). So Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk} has an exact $W$ for every even $q$, with a computer-found $W$ when $q=2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
As in Theorem~\ref{thm:exact}: the lines of $\bigcup_{z\ne0}S_z\setminus\{v\}$ on a free point $p$ are the $q$ distinct lines $\ell_p(z)$, $z\in A_p$, and $\ell_p(z)\in W$ if and only if $\Phi_p(z)+c_0\alpha+c_1\beta=0$, because $F(z)=c_0\alpha+c_1\beta+\beta^3/(\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:key2} the left side is affine on $A_p$ with increment
\[
\frac{p_2^3}{\Theta}+c_0p_5+c_1p_2=\frac{G(p_5,p_2)}{\Theta}\ne0
\]
along $(p_5,p_2)$, so it vanishes at exactly one point of $A_p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:even6}
Corollary~\ref{cor:d6} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} hold for every even $q\ge4$, and for $q=2$ with the computer-found whisker set of Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2}. Hence they hold for every prime power $q$ \textup{(}for $q=2$ computer-assisted\textup{)}: for $m\ge2$,
\[
n_b(q+m,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(2q^4+2q^3+2q+2+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr),
\]
and there is a $(q+m)$-regular incidence structure of diameter at most $6$ with $2(q+1)\bigl(q^4+q^2+1+(m-1)(q^3+m+1)\bigr)$ elements.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk} with Lemma~\ref{lem:herm2} and an exact $W$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2}). The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:R} uses, besides Theorem~\ref{thm:whisk}, only the facts that $W$ is exact, that each point of $\Pi$ is on exactly one line of $S$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:herm2}) and the inequality $T\ge(m-1)mq^2(q^2-1)>2(m-1)(q+m)$, which holds for $q=2$ as well ($12m>2m+4$).
\end{proof}

As for $D=4$, this does not change Table~\ref{tab:values}: for $q=2,4,8,16$ the degree $q+2$ is a Moore degree ($\Hq(q+1)$ exists), and at $\Delta=q+3$ the odd $q+1$ gives more. Even $q$ is the best choice first at $\Delta=34,\dots,37$ ($q=32$; e.g.\ $n_b(34,6)\ge 71502028$ against $63159250$ from $q=31$, $m=3$).

\begin{remark}[What changes in characteristic $2$]
The anisotropic form $a^2+cb^2$ ($-c$ a non-square) becomes $a^2+ab+\delta b^2$ ($\Tr\delta=1$); $J^2=-c$ becomes $J^2+J+\delta=0$; the hyperplanes $\Pi_z$ must avoid the nucleus; $\nu$ gains the term $X_2X_4$; and Lemma~\ref{lem:G} needs $q\ge4$. Equivalently, with $K=\F[\omega]$, $\omega^2=\omega+\delta$: $\nu(X)=\Tr_{K/\F}\bigl(\omega\,(X_3+X_4\omega)/(\delta X_2+X_5\omega)\bigr)$.
\end{remark}

\section{Orders}\label{sec:orders}

At $\Delta=q+2$ the gap between the Moore bound and a generalized $D$-gon of order $(q,q)$ is
\[
M_b(q+2,D)-M_b(q+1,D)=2\sum_{i=1}^{D-1}\bigl((q+1)^i-q^i\bigr)=2(D-1)q^{D-2}+O(q^{D-3});
\]
it is $4q+4$ for $D=3$ and $6q^2+10q+6$ for $D=4$. Doubling cannot close this gap. By Proposition~\ref{prop:count}, a doubled set satisfies $|\cA_0|<2N/q$, where $N=q^{D-1}+O(q^{D-2})$ is the number of points; the higher levels are smaller still, since every element of $\cA_1$ has all its $q+1$ neighbors in $\cA_0$. So doubling gains at most $2q^{D-2}+O(q^{D-3})$ elements, roughly a fraction $1/(D-1)$ of the gap.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item $D=3$: the gain is a constant. A pure $m$-fold doubling gains at most $2(m-1)$, that is $2$ at $\Delta=q+2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:d3patch}); with patches, three hats gain $8$ \cite{AL20} and four hats gain $10$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fourhat}).
\item $D=4$: the gain is of order $q^2$. Delorme's doubling gains $q^2+q+4$. For even $q$, doubling an ovoid and a spread of $\Qf(4,q)$ gains $2(q^2+1)$ and meets the counting bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:count} with equality (Section~\ref{sec:even4}). For odd $q$, Theorem~\ref{thm:A} uses a different quadrangle, $\AS(q)$, and has $2q^2(q+2)=2(q+1)(q^2+1)+2q^2-2q-2$ elements, which has the order of magnitude of the counting bound $2q^2+O(q)$; Proposition~\ref{prop:shadowbound} limits the shadow-line variant on $\Qf(4,q)$ to at most $q+5$ more.
\item $D=6$: the gain is of order $q^4$. Delorme's doubling gains $q^4+O(q^3)$; with an exact whisker set (Theorem~\ref{thm:exact} for odd $q$, Theorem~\ref{thm:exact2} for even $q\ge4$, a computer-found set for $q=2$) the gain is $2q^4+4q^3+4q+10$, and the single-level part attains the counting bound with equality.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Diameter $3$.} Is there a five-hat structure, or any modification of a projective plane of order $q$ with $\Delta=q+2$, diameter $3$ and more than $2(q^2+q+6)$ elements?
\item \emph{Diameter $4$.} For odd $q\ge9$, are there $q^2-2q-2$ shadow lines on $\Qf(4,q)$ with one ovoid of clones (Proposition~\ref{prop:shadowbound})? Do weighted $3$-ovoids of $\Wq(q)$ exist for all odd $q$? Do the $2$-ovoids of Proposition~\ref{prop:C} coincide with known ones \cite{BLP09,CCEM08}?
\item \emph{Diameter $6$.} Is there a balanced doubling of $\Hq(q)$, with level-$1$ centers at points as well as lines, that is regular without deleting repeated lines? The Feit--Higman theorem rules out hexagons of order $(q-1,q+1)$, so there is no direct analogue of $\AS(q)$; are there weighted distance-$2$ ovoids of $\Hq(q)$ that help?
\item \emph{Diameter $8$.} The Ree--Tits octagon of order $(2,4)$ has the shape $(q-1,q+1)$ with $q=3$; a weighted $2$-ovoid in it would give a $5$-regular structure of diameter $8$ with $5850$ elements.
\end{enumerate}
