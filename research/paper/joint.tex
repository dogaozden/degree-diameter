\begin{abstract}
Let $\nb(\Delta,D)$ be the largest order of a bipartite graph of maximum degree at most $\Delta$ and diameter at most
$D$. We give constructions from generalized polygons that improve the lower bounds in the 2013 table of Miller and
\v{S}ir\'a\v{n} at diameters 3, 4, 6 and 7, and Haar graphs that improve it at diameters 5, 8, 9 and 10. At diameter 3
we add a fourth hat to the three hats of Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez and obtain $\nb(q+2,3)\ge 2(q^2+q+6)$ with regular
graphs. At diameter 4 we copy points and lines of a generalized quadrangle with weights and stand-ins; a weighted
2-ovoid of the Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle built from two elliptic quadrics gives $\nb(q+2,4)\ge 2q^2(q+2)$ for odd
$q$, and Singer 2-ovoids of $W(3,q)$ give $\nb(q+3,4)\ge 2(q^2+1)(q+3)$, both with regular graphs. At diameter 6 an
explicit set of $q^2(q^2-1)$ lines of the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$ (``whiskers'') added to Delorme's doubling gives
$\nb(q+m,6)\ge 2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(2q^4+2q^3+2q+2+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr)$, and a regular version. At diameter 7
we join $2r+1$ copies of the incidence graph of $H(q)$ by matchings, delete matching edges at suitable sets and attach
new vertices; we prove which sets are allowed. All constructions are given in projective coordinates with the exact
neighbourhood of every new vertex. We also show that the graphs of G\'omez behind six diameter-8 entries of the table
have diameter at least 9 as defined, and that relabelling their copies by automorphisms does not help.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

A bipartite graph of maximum degree $\Delta\ge2$ and diameter $D\ge2$ has at most
\[
\Mb(\Delta,D)=2\bigl(1+(\Delta-1)+\dots+(\Delta-1)^{D-1}\bigr)
\]
vertices (the bipartite Moore bound). Let $\nb(\Delta,D)$ be the largest order of such a graph.\todo{Notation: the lab's
notes use $n_b(\Delta,D)$ and $M_b(\Delta,D)$, the repository README $N^b(\Delta,D)$. Use the notation of [AL20] and the
survey if you prefer.} For $D\ge3$ and
$\Delta\ge3$ the bound is attained only by the incidence graphs of generalized polygons: projective planes ($D=3$),
generalized quadrangles ($D=4$) and generalized hexagons ($D=6$) of order $(\Delta-1,\Delta-1)$
\cite{FeitHigman,MS13}. These are known when $\Delta-1$ is a prime power. For the other cells only lower bounds are
known. They are collected in Table~4 of the survey of Miller and \v{S}ir\'a\v{n} \cite{MS13} (version of 2013), which
the CombinatoricsWiki \cite{CW} reproduces; since 2013 the wiki has changed only at $(13,3)$ and $(15,3)$, by the work of
Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20}. We call this table ``the 2013 table''.

This paper gives constructions that improve the 2013 table in the cells listed in Table~\ref{tab:summary}. Most of them
are built from generalized polygons in their classical coordinates, by three kinds of operations: copying points and
lines (with Delorme's doubling with levels \cite{D85a} as the basic tool), changing a bounded number of incidences, and
joining several copies of an incidence graph by matchings and new vertices. The remaining cells are Haar graphs
(bi-Cayley graphs) of explicit groups.

\subsection*{Results}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Diameter 3} (Section~\ref{sec:d3}). Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20} added three ``hats'' to
  $\PG(2,q)$ and obtained $\nb(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+5)$. We add a fourth hat (Theorem~\ref{thm:hat4}):
  $\nb(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+6)$ for every prime power $q\ge4$, attained by a $(q+2)$-regular graph; the construction works
  in every projective plane of order $q\ge3$.
\item \emph{Diameter 4} (Section~\ref{sec:d4}). Theorem~\ref{thm:B}: in any generalized quadrangle, clones of points
  with arbitrary weights and copies of lines with ``stand-ins'' keep diameter 4. With a weighted 2-ovoid of the
  Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle $\AS(q)$ made of two elliptic quadrics (Theorem~\ref{thm:A}) this gives
  $\nb(q+2,4)\ge2q^2(q+2)$ for odd $q$; with the Singer 2-ovoids of $W(3,q)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:C}) it gives
  $\nb(q+3,4)\ge2(q^2+1)(q+3)$ for odd $q$; both with regular graphs. For every prime power $q\le\Delta-1$,
  $\nb(\Delta,4)\ge2q^2\Delta$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:B2}).
\item \emph{Diameter 6} (Section~\ref{sec:d6}). A set of $q^2(q^2-1)$ lines of the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$, given by
  an explicit rule (Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskers}), can be added at level 0 to Delorme's doubling of $H(q)$ along a
  Hermitian spread. This gives Theorem~\ref{thm:D}, and deleting repeated lines and completing gives regular graphs
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:R}).
\item \emph{Diameter 7} (Section~\ref{sec:d7}). The 2013 values at $(7,7)$ and $(9,7)$--$(16,7)$ are the orders
  $(2r+1)\cdot2|H(q)|$ of $2r+1$ copies of the incidence graph of $H(q)$ joined by matchings. We delete matching
  edges at suitable sets of elements and attach new vertices to the freed slots. Theorem~\ref{thm:assembly} reduces the
  diameter to one condition per pair of copies; Theorems~\ref{thm:autotrans} and~\ref{thm:localdual} give two
  sufficient conditions (segment sets at automorphic transitions; local dualities), the first also necessary. With
  classical sets this improves all nine cells (Corollaries~\ref{cor:d7-91}--\ref{cor:d7-r}); explicit larger sets give
  the values of Table~\ref{tab:summary}.
\item \emph{Diameter 8} (Section~\ref{sec:d8}). Six entries of the 2013 table at $D=8$ are the orders of the compound
  graphs $\Om_q$ of G\'omez \cite{Go86}, for which the thesis states diameter at most 8 without proof. As defined there,
  these graphs have diameter at least 9 (Theorems~\ref{thm:proj} and~\ref{thm:twolabels}), and relabelling the copies
  by automorphisms of the hexagon does not give diameter 8 (Theorems~\ref{thm:geomq8} and~\ref{thm:geomq7}).
\item \emph{Haar graphs} (Section~\ref{sec:haar}). We state the covering criterion that decides the diameter of a Haar
  graph from two breadth-first searches, and list Haar graphs that improve two cells at $D=5$ and 33 cells at
  $D=8,9,10$ (with one further lift at $(3,8)$). These are single explicit graphs, checked by computer.
\end{itemize}

Problem~4 of \cite{AL20} asks to generalize the hat construction to regular bipartite graphs of diameters 4 and 6. The
hat lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}) says in what sense it generalizes, and Corollaries~\ref{cor:A1} and~\ref{cor:C1} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:R} give regular graphs at $D=4$ and $D=6$ that beat the 2013 table.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Incidence structures}
An \emph{incidence structure} $\cS=(\cP,\cL,\I)$ has points, lines and a symmetric incidence relation; repeated points
and repeated lines (with the same incidences) are allowed. Its \emph{incidence graph} $\Gamma(\cS)$ has vertex set
$\cP\cup\cL$ and the incident pairs (\emph{flags}) as edges; it is bipartite with the points on one side. A non-incident
point--line pair is an \emph{anti-flag}. Every bipartite graph with a chosen 2-colouring is of this form, so
\[
\nb(\Delta,D)=\max\{|\cP|+|\cL|:\ \text{every element has at most }\Delta\text{ neighbours},\ \diam\Gamma(\cS)\le D\}.
\]
Distances $d(x,y)$ are taken in $\Gamma(\cS)$, and $\diam\cS$ means $\diam\Gamma(\cS)$. Two distinct points on a
common line are \emph{collinear}; two distinct lines on a common point are \emph{concurrent}. $\F=\GF(q)$ is the field
of order $q$, and for $q$ odd $\chi$ is its quadratic character, with $\chi(0)=0$.

\begin{lemma}[diameter in geometric terms]\label{lem:diamgeom}
Let $\cS$ have no isolated element and let $D\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $D$ is even, $\diam\cS\le D$ if and only if $d(p,L)\le D-1$ for every anti-flag $(p,L)$.
\item If $D$ is odd, $\diam\cS\le D$ if and only if any two points, and any two lines, are at distance at most $D-1$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $\diam\cS\le3$ if and only if any two points are collinear and any two lines are concurrent, and
$\diam\cS\le4$ if and only if for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ some point of $L$ is collinear with $p$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $p\ne p'$ be points and $L$ a line on $p'$. If $p\I L$ then $d(p,p')=2$; otherwise
$d(p,p')\le d(p,L)+1\le D$. Lines likewise. (ii) For an anti-flag $(p,L)$ take $p'\I L$; then
$d(p,L)\le d(p,p')+1\le D$. The converses hold by parity.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[adding flags, deleting twins]\label{lem:flags}
(a) Adding flags to an incidence structure keeps its incidence graph bipartite and does not increase any distance.
(b) If two lines have the same set of points, deleting one of them does not change any distance between the remaining
elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) is clear. (b) A shortest path through the deleted line can use its twin instead; it does not already contain the
twin, since then it could be shortened.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Generalized polygons}
A \emph{generalized $n$-gon} is an incidence structure whose incidence graph has diameter $n$ and girth $2n$; it has
\emph{order $(s,t)$} if every line has $s+1$ points and every point is on $t+1$ lines. It is \emph{thick} if
$s,t\ge2$. A generalized 3-gon of order $(q,q)$ is a projective plane of order $q$, with $q^2+q+1$ points and as many
lines. A generalized quadrangle (GQ) of order $(s,t)$ has $(s+1)(st+1)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)$ lines, and its axiom
is: for every anti-flag $(p,L)$ exactly one point of $L$ is collinear with $p$. A generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$
has $(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)$ points and as many lines. By Feit and Higman \cite{FeitHigman}, thick generalized $n$-gons exist
only for $n\in\{2,3,4,6,8\}$, and a generalized octagon of order $(s,s)$ does not exist; so $\Mb(\Delta,D)$ is attained,
for $\Delta\ge3$, only at $D\in\{2,3,4,6\}$.

\subsubsection*{The projective plane}
Points of $\PG(2,q)$ are written $(x:y:z)$ and lines $[u:v:w]$, with $(x:y:z)\I[u:v:w]$ if and only if $ux+vy+wz=0$.

\subsubsection*{Quadrangles}
$W(3,q)$ is the symplectic quadrangle: the points of $\PG(3,q)$ and the lines of $\PG(3,q)$ that are totally isotropic
for a non-degenerate alternating form; it is a GQ of order $(q,q)$ \cite{PT}. $Q(4,q)$ is the parabolic quadric of
$\PG(4,q)$ with the lines it contains; it is a GQ of order $(q,q)$, dual to $W(3,q)$ \cite[3.2.1]{PT}. The
Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle $\AS(q)$ ($q$ odd) is introduced in coordinates in Section~\ref{ssec:A}.

\subsubsection*{The split Cayley hexagon}\label{ssec:Hq}
Let $V=\F^7$ with coordinates $(X_0,\dots,X_6)$,
\[
Q(X)=X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6-X_3^2,\qquad b(X,Y)=Q(X+Y)-Q(X)-Q(Y),
\]
and let $Q(6,q)$ be the parabolic quadric $\{\langle x\rangle:Q(x)=0\}$ of $\PG(6,q)$. Points $\langle x\rangle,\langle
y\rangle$ of $Q(6,q)$ are \emph{perpendicular}, $x\perp y$, if $b(x,y)=0$, i.e.\ if the line $xy$ lies on $Q(6,q)$. The
\emph{split Cayley hexagon} $H(q)$ \cite{T59}, \cite[\S2.4]{VM98} has as points all
$N=N(q)=(q^6-1)/(q-1)=(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)$ points of $Q(6,q)$, and as lines the lines of $Q(6,q)$ whose Grassmann
coordinates $p_{ij}=x_iy_j-x_jy_i$ satisfy Tits' equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tits}
p_{12}=p_{34},\quad p_{54}=p_{32},\quad p_{20}=p_{35},\quad p_{65}=p_{30},\quad p_{01}=p_{36},\quad p_{46}=p_{31}.
\end{equation}
For example, the $q+1$ lines through $e_0=(1,0,\dots,0)$ are $\langle e_0,\lambda e_5+\mu e_6\rangle$,
$(\lambda:\mu)\in\PG(1,q)$; they lie in the totally singular plane $\langle e_0,e_5,e_6\rangle$. The same equations
define $H(q)$ for $q$ even, with the quadric written $X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6+X_3^2$ (Section~\ref{ssec:evenq}).
\todo{The referee of the diameter-7 note recalls that this form of the quadric and of Tits' equations is

\begin{fact}[\cite{T59}, {\cite[\S2.4]{VM98}}]\label{fact:hex}
$H(q)$ is a generalized hexagon of order $(q,q)$. The lines of $H(q)$ through a point $x$ are the lines through $x$ of a
totally singular plane $\pi_x\subset x^\perp$. The collineation group contains $G_2(q)$; for $A\in\SL(3,q)$ the map
$(v,a,w)\mapsto(Av,a,A^{-T}w)$, with $x=(v,a,w)$, $v=(x_0,x_1,x_2)$, $a=x_3$, $w=(x_4,x_5,x_6)$, is a collineation. $H(q)$
has a duality (an incidence-preserving bijection exchanging points and lines) if and only if $q$ is a power of $3$.
\end{fact}

Let $I=I(q)$ be the incidence graph of $H(q)$, with $2N$ vertices, and $\Gamma_i(x)=\{y:d(x,y)=i\}$. Two elements are
\emph{opposite} if they are at distance 6, and $\opp(x)=\Gamma_6(x)$. The following facts are standard; we include the
short proofs because Sections~\ref{sec:d7} and~\ref{sec:d8} use them repeatedly.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hexfacts}
Let $x,y$ be vertices of $I$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $d(x,y)\le5$ there is a unique geodesic from $x$ to $y$. If $1\le d(x,y)=k\le5$, exactly one neighbour of $y$ is
  at distance $k-1$ from $x$ and the other $q$ at distance $k+1$; if $k=6$ all $q+1$ neighbours of $y$ are at distance
  5. Hence $|\Gamma_i(x)|=(q+1)q^{i-1}$ for $1\le i\le5$ and $|\Gamma_6(x)|=q^5$.
\item \emph{(covering)} There is $z\in\Gamma_2(x)$ with $d(z,y)\le3$ if $x,y$ have different types, and with
  $d(z,y)\le4$ if they have the same type.
\item \emph{(orthogonality)} For points $x,y$: $d(x,y)\le4$ if and only if $x\perp y$. So $\opp(x)\cap\cP=\cP\setminus
  x^\perp$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Two geodesics would close a cycle of length at most $10<12$. Neighbours of $y$ are at distance $k\pm1$ from $x$;
the predecessor on the geodesic is at $k-1$, and a second one would give a second geodesic; at $k=6$ the diameter
forces $5$. The counts follow by induction. (b) If $2\le d(x,y)\le6$ take the vertex at distance 2 from $x$ on a
geodesic to $y$; if $d(x,y)=1$ take $z\in\Gamma_1(y)\setminus\{x\}$; if $x=y$ any $z\in\Gamma_2(x)$. The largest value
of $d(z,y)$ is $6-2=4$ (same type) or $5-2=3$ (different types). (c) If $d(x,y)\le4$ there is a point $z$ with $x,y$
on lines of $H(q)$ through $z$ ($z=x$ if $d(x,y)\le2$); these lines lie in $\pi_z$, so $b(x,y)=0$. Conversely
$x^\perp\cap Q(6,q)$ is a cone with vertex $x$ over a $Q(4,q)$ and has $1+q(q^3+q^2+q+1)$ points, which by (a) is the
number $1+(q+1)q+(q+1)q^3$ of points at distance at most 4 from $x$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Doubling with levels, and the hat lemma}\label{ssec:doubling}
A \emph{duplicate} of a vertex (a new vertex with the same neighbours) does not increase the diameter (see
\cite{DF84}, which we have not seen, and \cite[\S7]{D85a}).\todo{If you have access to [DF84], please check what it
contains about duplicates.} Delorme \cite[\S7]{D85a} refined this into a doubling with
levels, stated there without proof, and used it for the 2013 values at $D=4$ and $D=6$ \cite[\S8(a),(b)]{D85a}. We
state it for incidence structures and give a proof.

Let $\cA=(\cA_0\supseteq\cA_1\supseteq\dots\supseteq\cA_r\supseteq\cA_{r+1}=\emptyset)$ be a filtration of a set
$\cA_0$ of elements (points and lines may be mixed). The \emph{level} of $x$ is $\lambda(x)=\max\{k:x\in\cA_k\}$, and
$\lambda(x)=-1$ if $x\notin\cA_0$. Fix $m\ge2$. Two words over $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$ are \emph{comparable} if one is a
prefix of the other.

\begin{definition}[Delorme]\label{def:doubling}
The $m$-fold doubling $\cS^{(m)}_\cA$ has the \emph{versions} $x^w$, for $x$ an element of $\cS$ and
$w\in[m]^{\lambda(x)+1}$ ($x^w$ is a point if $x$ is a point and a line if $x$ is a line), and
\[
x^w\I y^u\iff x\I y\ \text{and}\ w,u\ \text{are comparable}.
\]
\end{definition}

So an element of level $-1$ has one version (identified with $x$), adjacent to every version of each of its neighbours;
and a version $x^w$ of level $k\ge0$ is adjacent, for each neighbour $y$ of $x$, to the single version
$y^{w_1\cdots w_{j+1}}$ if $\lambda(y)=j\le k$, and to the $m^{j-k}$ versions $y^{wu}$ ($|u|=j-k$) if $\lambda(y)=j>k$.
This is the exact neighbourhood of every version. The map $x^w\mapsto x$ preserves incidence, so
$d(x^w,y^u)\ge d(x,y)$.

The conditions on $\cA$ are (with $D\ge2$):
\begin{itemize}
\item[(L0)] every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_0$;
\item[(L1)] for every $i\ge0$: every element incident with an element of $\cA_{i+1}$ lies in $\cA_i$, and every element
  of $\cA_i\setminus\cA_{i+1}$ is incident with at most one element of $\cA_{i+1}$;
\item[(C$_D$)] for any two elements $x,y$ (possibly $x=y$) in the same connected component of the substructure induced
  on $\cA_0$, there is $z\notin\cA_0$ with $d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le D$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[Delorme {\cite[\S7]{D85a}}, (a)--(c)]\label{thm:delorme}
Let $\cS$ have diameter at most $D\ge2$ and let $m\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If (C$_D$) holds, $\cS^{(m)}_\cA$ has diameter at most $D$.
\item If (L0) and (L1) hold, every version satisfies $\deg x^w\le\deg x+m-1$, with equality only if $x$ is incident
  with an element of level $\lambda(x)+1$.
\item $\cS^{(m)}_\cA$ has $|\cP|+(m-1)\sum_{k\ge0}m^k|\cA_k\cap\cP|$ points and
  $|\cL|+(m-1)\sum_{k\ge0}m^k|\cA_k\cap\cL|$ lines.
\item Conversely, if $\cS^{(m)}_\cA$ has diameter at most $D$, the conclusion of (C$_D$) holds for every pair
  $x,y\in\cA_0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Delorme states (a)--(c) without proof (``je ne donne pas la preuve''); (d) is not in \cite{D85a}.

\begin{proof}
(c) An element of level $k$ has $m^{k+1}=1+(m-1)(1+m+\dots+m^k)$ versions.

(b) Let $\lambda(x)=k$. By the description after Definition~\ref{def:doubling}, only neighbours of level $>k$
contribute more than one version. If $k=-1$, (L0) gives at most one neighbour in $\cA_0$; it has level exactly 0 (a
neighbour in $\cA_1$ would put $x$ in $\cA_0$ by (L1)), and contributes $m$ versions. If $k\ge0$, (L1) with $i=k$ gives
at most one neighbour in $\cA_{k+1}$, and (L1) with $i=k+1$ gives none in $\cA_{k+2}$; that neighbour contributes $m$
versions. So $\deg x^w\le\deg x-1+m$.

(a) For an infinite word $\omega$ the \emph{sheet} $\Sigma_\omega$ is the set of versions $x^w$ with $w$ a prefix of
$\omega$. It contains one version of each element, and $x^w\mapsto x$ is an isomorphism from the substructure on
$\Sigma_\omega$ onto $\cS$. Two versions with comparable words lie in a common sheet, hence are at distance at most
$D$. Let $x^w,y^u$ have incomparable words; both are non-empty, so $x,y\in\cA_0$. If $x,y$ are in different components
of the substructure on $\cA_0$, a shortest path $x=z_0,\dots,z_k=y$ ($k\le D$) passes through some $z_i\notin\cA_0$,
whose unique version lies in every sheet. Lift $z_0,\dots,z_i$ into a sheet through $x^w$ and $z_i,\dots,z_k$ into a
sheet through $y^u$: this is a path of length $k$. If $x,y$ are in the same component (including $x=y$), (C$_D$) gives
$z\notin\cA_0$ with $d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le D$, and the same lifting gives a path of length at most $D$.

(d) Take versions $x^w,y^u$ with $w$ starting with the letter 1 and $u$ with the letter 2, and a path of length at most
$D$ between them. Consecutive versions on it have comparable words, and comparable non-empty words start with the same
letter; so some version on the path has the empty word, that is, it is an element $z\notin\cA_0$. Projecting the path
gives a walk $x\to z\to y$ of length at most $D$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[hat lemma]\label{lem:hat}
Let $\cS$ be a generalized $n$-gon of order $(s,t)$, $s,t\ge1$, and $\cA_0$ a set of elements. Then (C$_n$) holds if
and only if every connected component of the substructure induced on $\cA_0$ is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose a component $B$ contains a path $x=z_0,\dots,z_{n-1}=y$ of length $n-1$ (it does if $B$ has diameter at least
$n-1$, and if $B$ contains a cycle, since cycles have length at least $2n$). By the girth this is the only path of
length at most $n-1$ from $x$ to $y$, so $d(x,y)=n-1$. If $d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le n$ then by parity
$d(x,z)+d(z,y)=n-1$, so $z$ lies on a shortest $x$--$y$ path, i.e.\ $z=z_i\in\cA_0$. So (C$_n$) fails.

Conversely let $B$ be a tree of diameter at most $n-2$, $x,y\in B$, and $P$ the path from $x$ to $y$ in $B$, of length
$d$. The incidence graph is connected and has cycles, so some element of $B$ has a neighbour outside $\cA_0$. Let $k$
be the least distance in $B$ from $P$ to such an element $u$, and $z$ a neighbour of $u$ outside $\cA_0$. Then
$d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le d+2k+2$. If $k=0$ this is at most $n$. If $k\ge1$, every element of $B$ at distance less than $k$ from
$P$ has all its neighbours in $B$, and every element has at least two neighbours. So we can walk $k$ steps away from
$P$ starting at $x$, and $k$ steps away from $P$ starting at $y$ (along two different neighbours if $x=y$); in the tree
$B$ this gives a path of length $d+2k$. Hence $d+2k\le n-2$ and $d(x,z)+d(z,y)\le n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hat}
In a generalized $n$-gon of order $(s,t)$, $s,t\ge1$, for any filtration and $m\ge2$, $\cS^{(m)}_\cA$ has diameter at
most $n$ if and only if every component of the substructure on $\cA_0$ is a tree of diameter at most $n-2$. For $n=3$
the allowed components are single elements and flags; for $n=4$ also \emph{stars} (a point with some lines on it, or a
line with some of its points); for $n=6$ trees of diameter at most 4.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[diameter 3 needs patches]\label{prop:patches}
In a projective plane, if $\cA$ satisfies (L0), (L1) and (C$_3$), then $\cA_0$ has at most one point and at most one
line, and $\cA_1=\emptyset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $x\ne y$ are points of $\cA_0$, the line $xy$ is either outside $\cA_0$ and incident with two elements of $\cA_0$
(against (L0)), or in $\cA_0$, and then $x,xy,y$ is a path of length $2>n-2$ in one component (against
Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}). Lines likewise. An element of $\cA_1$ would have its $q+1\ge3$ neighbours in $\cA_0$ by (L1).
\end{proof}

So pure doubling gains at most $2(m-1)$ elements in a projective plane; the hats of Section~\ref{sec:d3} double flags
and then change a bounded number of incidences.

%======================================================================
\section{Diameter 3: the fourth hat}\label{sec:d3}
%======================================================================

Araujo-Pardo and L\'opez \cite{AL20} add three \emph{hats} to $\PG(2,q)$ and obtain $\nb(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+5)$. We add
a fourth. By Proposition~\ref{prop:patches}, every hat must change some incidences of the plane; the fourth hat costs
one rotation of three incidences.

\subsection{Frame and notation}
Let $\ell=[1:0:0]$ (the line $x=0$) and $A=(1:0:0)\notin\ell$. The points of $\ell$ and the lines on $A$ are
\[
X_t=(0:t:1),\quad P=(0:1:0);\qquad \lambda_t=[0:1:-t],\quad \lambda_\infty=[0:0:1]\qquad(t\in\F).
\]
Every point is listed once among $A$, $X_t$ ($t\in\F$), $P$, $(x:y:1)$ ($x\ne0$), $(x:1:0)$ ($x\ne0$); every line
once among $\ell$, $\lambda_t$, $\lambda_\infty$, $[u:1:w]$ ($u\ne0$), $[1:0:w]$ ($w\ne0$). We use these facts
(one-line computations):
\begin{itemize}
\item[(F1)] $\lambda_t\cap\ell=X_t$, $\lambda_\infty\cap\ell=P$; the points of $\lambda_t$ are $A$ and $(x:t:1)$,
  $x\in\F$ ($X_t$ at $x=0$); those of $\lambda_\infty$ are $A$ and $(x:1:0)$, $x\in\F$.
\item[(F2)] The lines on $X_t$ are $\ell$ and $[w:1:-t]$, $w\in\F$ ($\lambda_t$ at $w=0$); the lines on $P$ are $\ell$,
  $\lambda_\infty$ and $[1:0:w]$, $w\ne0$.
\item[(F3)] If $u=(x:y:z)$ with $x\ne0$, then $u$ and $X_s$ lie on $[(sz-y)/x:1:-s]\ne\ell$.
\item[(F4)] A line $[u:v:w]$ with $u\ne0$ meets $\lambda_s$ in $(-(vs+w)/u:s:1)\ne A$; $[u:1:w]$ meets $\ell$ in
  $X_{-w}$.
\end{itemize}
Let $q\ge4$, let $a,c\in\F\setminus\{0,1\}$ be distinct, and put
\[
S=\{0,1,a,c\},\qquad T=\{0,1,c\},\qquad \sigma=(0\ c\ 1):\ 0\mapsto c\mapsto1\mapsto0 .
\]

\subsection{The construction}

\begin{definition}\label{def:hat4}
$\cS(q;a,c)$ has the points of $\PG(2,q)$ and five new points $Q,H_0,H_1,H_a,H_c$, the lines of $\PG(2,q)$ and five
new lines $M,K_0,K_1,K_a,K_c$, and exactly these incidences:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(C1)] every incidence of $\PG(2,q)$ except $X_s\I\lambda_s$ ($s\in S$), and the \emph{rotation}
  $X_0\I\lambda_c$, $X_c\I\lambda_1$, $X_1\I\lambda_0$ (that is, $X_t\I\lambda_{\sigma(t)}$ for $t\in T$);
\item[(C2)] $H_s$ lies on $[w:1:-s]$ for every $w\in\F$ ($s\in S$): the lines on $X_s$ other than $\ell$;
\item[(C3)] $K_s$ contains $(x:s:1)$ for every $x\in\F$ ($s\in S$): the points of $\lambda_s$ other than $A$;
\item[(C4)] $M$ contains $P$, $X_t$ ($t\in\F\setminus T$), and $H_0,H_1,H_a,H_c$;
\item[(C5)] $Q$ lies on $\lambda_\infty$, $\lambda_t$ ($t\in\F\setminus T$), and $K_0,K_1,K_a,K_c$;
\item[(C6)] the \emph{links} $A\I K_a$, $H_a\I\ell$, $H_0\I K_c$, $H_c\I K_1$, $H_1\I K_0$ (that is, $H_t\I
  K_{\sigma(t)}$ for $t\in T$).
\end{itemize}
$G(q;a,c)$ is its incidence graph. $R(q;a,c)$ is $G(q;a,c)$ together with the \emph{filler} edges
\[
(x:y:1)\sim[x:1:-y]\quad(x\ne0,\ y\notin S),\qquad (x:1:0)\sim[1:0:-1/x]\quad(x\ne0).
\]
\end{definition}

The hat pair $(H_s,K_s)$ is a copy of the flag $(X_s,\lambda_s)$ with the flag ``crossed'', and $(Q,M)$ is a copy of the
anti-flag $(A,\ell)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hat4}
For every prime power $q\ge4$ and distinct $a,c\in\F\setminus\{0,1\}$, the graphs $G(q;a,c)$ and $R(q;a,c)$ are
bipartite, have $2(q^2+q+6)$ vertices, maximum degree $q+2$ and diameter 3, and $R(q;a,c)$ is $(q+2)$-regular. Hence
\[
\nb(q+2,3)\ge2(q^2+q+6),
\]
attained by a regular graph.
\end{theorem}

The construction and the proof use only the axioms of a projective plane together with an anti-flag $(A,\ell)$ and four
points $X_s$ of $\ell$, so a coordinate-free version holds in every projective plane of order $q\ge3$ (in coordinates
the four points are labelled by $0,1,a,c$, which needs $q\ge4$). It also has a regular completion there. At $q=3$ the
four points $X_s$ are all of $\ell$, there is no deficient element, and the structure is already 5-regular. For $q\ge4$
put $N_0=(q-1)(q-3)$, the number of points of degree $q+1$ and of lines of degree $q+1$ (see the table below). A
deficient point $p$ lies on exactly $q-4$ deficient lines: the lines on $p$ other than $Ap$ meet $\ell$ in the $q$
points other than $Ap\cap\ell$, four of which are the $X_s$. Dually, a deficient line has exactly $q-4$ deficient
points. So the bipartite graph of non-incident pairs between deficient points and deficient lines is regular of degree
$N_0-(q-4)=q^2-5q+7\ge1$, and a regular bipartite graph of positive degree has a perfect matching (K\H{o}nig); adding
it as flags completes the structure. In coordinates, the filler of Definition~\ref{def:hat4} is such a matching.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hat4}}

\emph{Degrees.} Each row of Table~\ref{tab:hat4} is (F1) or (F2) corrected by (C1)--(C6), and within a row the listed
neighbours are distinct. For instance: $A$ keeps its $q+1$ lines and gains only $K_a$ ($A$ is not of the form
$(x:s:1)$); $X_a$ loses $\lambda_a$ and gains $M$ (as $a\notin T$) and $K_a$; $X_t$ ($t\in T$) loses $\lambda_t$, gains
$\lambda_{\sigma(t)}$ and $K_t$, and is not on $M$; a point $(x:y:1)$ with $x\ne0$ is not on $\ell$, so it loses
nothing, and it gains $K_s$ if and only if $y=s\in S$; $Q$ is on $1+(q-3)+4=q+2$ lines; $H_s$ is on $q$ lines by (C2),
on $M$, and on exactly one link line. The line rows are the dual count: $\ell$ gains only $H_a$; $\lambda_a$ loses
$X_a$ and gains $Q,H_a$; $\lambda_t$ ($t\in T$) loses $X_t$ and gains $X_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$ and $H_t$; $[u:1:w]$ ($u\ne0$)
gains $H_s$ if and only if $s=-w\in S$; $M$ has $1+(q-3)+4$ points; $K_s$ has $q$ points, $Q$, and one link point.

The filler map $\delta:(x:y:z)\mapsto[x:z:-y]$ is a correlation of $\PG(2,q)$ (since $ux+wz+(-v)(-y)=ux+vy+wz$), a point
lies on its image if and only if $x^2=0$, i.e.\ if and only if it is on $\ell$, and $\delta$ maps $(x:y:1)\mapsto[x:1:-y]$
and $(x:1:0)\mapsto[x:0:-1]=[1:0:-1/x]$. So the filler is a bijection from the deficient points to the deficient lines,
and its pairs are anti-flags. Hence $G(q;a,c)$ has maximum degree $q+2$ and $R(q;a,c)$ is $(q+2)$-regular.

\emph{Reduction.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:diamgeom}(ii) it suffices to show that any two points are collinear and any two lines
concurrent. In the next two lemmas ``incident'' means incident in $\cS(q;a,c)$. The only incidences of $\PG(2,q)$ that are
missing are $X_s\I\lambda_s$, $s\in S$; so an incidence $u\I L$ of the plane survives whenever $u\notin\ell$ or $L$ is
not on $A$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hat4points}
Any two points $u\ne v$ of $\cS(q;a,c)$ are collinear.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(P1) Both in $\PG(2,q)$; let $L=uv$. If both incidences survive, use $L$. Otherwise, say $u=X_s$ and $L=\lambda_s$ with
$s\in S$, and $v=A$ or $v=(x:s:1)$ with $x\ne0$. If $v=(x:s:1)$: use $K_s$. If $v=A$ and $s=a$: $K_a$ (by (C3), (C6)). If
$v=A$ and $s\in T$: $\lambda_{\sigma(s)}$ (rotation).

(P2) $Q$ and $u$. If $u\in\{A,P\}$ or $u=(x:1:0)$: $\lambda_\infty$. If $u=X_t$, $t\notin S$: $\lambda_t$. If $u=X_s$, $s\in
S$: $K_s$. If $u=(x:y:1)$, $x\ne0$: $\lambda_y$ if $y\notin T$, and $K_y$ if $y\in T$.

(P3) $H_s$ and $u$. If $u\notin\ell$ (including $u=A$): the line of (F3), which contains $H_s$ by (C2). If $u=X_s$:
$[1:1:-s]$. If $u=P$, or $u=X_t$ with $t\notin T$, $t\ne s$: $M$. If $u=X_t$ with $t\in T\setminus\{s\}$ and $s=a$: $\ell$.
If $u=X_t$ with $t\in T\setminus\{s\}$ and $s\in T$: as $\sigma$ is a 3-cycle, $t=\sigma(s)$ or $t=\sigma^{-1}(s)$; use
$K_{\sigma(s)}$ (it contains $X_{\sigma(s)}$ and $H_s$) or $\lambda_s$ (it contains $X_{\sigma^{-1}(s)}$ by the rotation, and
$H_s$ by (C2) with $w=0$).

(P4) Two new points. $H_s,H_{s'}$: $M$. $Q,H_a$: $\lambda_a$. $Q,H_t$ ($t\in T$): $K_{\sigma(t)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hat4lines}
Any two lines $L\ne N$ of $\cS(q;a,c)$ are concurrent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(N1) Both in $\PG(2,q)$; let $p=L\cap N$. If both incidences survive, use $p$. Otherwise, say $p=X_s$ and $L=\lambda_s$,
and $N=\ell$ or $N=[w:1:-s]$ with $w\ne0$. If $N=[w:1:-s]$: $H_s$. If $N=\ell$ and $s=a$: $H_a$. If $N=\ell$ and $s\in T$:
$X_{\sigma^{-1}(s)}$ (rotation).

(N2) $M$ and $L$. If $L$ is on $P$ ($L=\ell$, $\lambda_\infty$ or $[1:0:w]$): $P$. If $L=\lambda_t$, $t\notin S$: $X_t$. If
$L=\lambda_s$, $s\in S$: $H_s$. If $L=[u:1:w]$, $u\ne0$: it meets $\ell$ in $X_r$, $r=-w$ (F4); use $X_r$ if $r\notin T$,
and $H_r$ if $r\in T$ ($H_r$ is on $[u:1:-r]$ and on $M$).

(N3) $K_s$ and $L$. If $L$ is not on $A$ ($L=\ell$ or $L=[u:v:w]$ with $u\ne0$): $L\cap\lambda_s=(x:s:1)$ (F1, F4), which
is on $K_s$, and its incidence with $L$ survives. If $L=\lambda_s$: $(1:s:1)$. If $L=\lambda_\infty$ or $L=\lambda_t$,
$t\notin T$: $Q$. If $L=\lambda_t$ with $t\in T\setminus\{s\}$ and $s=a$: $A$. If $L=\lambda_t$ with $t\in T\setminus\{s\}$
and $s\in T$: if $t=\sigma(s)$, use $X_s$ (on $\lambda_{\sigma(s)}$ and $K_s$); if $t=\sigma^{-1}(s)$, use $H_t$ (on
$\lambda_t$ and on $K_{\sigma(t)}=K_s$).

(N4) Two new lines. $K_s,K_{s'}$: $Q$. $M,K_a$: $X_a$. $M,K_t$ ($t\in T$): $H_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hat4}]
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:hat4points} and~\ref{lem:hat4lines} and Lemma~\ref{lem:diamgeom}(ii), $G(q;a,c)$ has diameter at
most 3, and adding the filler does not increase it (Lemma~\ref{lem:flags}). The graphs are bipartite and not complete
bipartite ($A\notin\ell$), so the diameter is exactly 3. The order is $2(q^2+q+1)+10$.
\end{proof}

The proof uses only that $\F$ is a field and that $0,1,a,c$ are distinct. The correlation $\delta$, extended by
$H_s\leftrightarrow K_s$ and $Q\leftrightarrow M$, maps $\cS(q;a,c)$ built with $\sigma$ onto the dual of the structure
built with $\sigma^{-1}$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:hat4lines} is also Lemma~\ref{lem:hat4points} for $\sigma^{-1}$; we wrote both
out so that every case can be checked directly. Both the rotation and the link $H_1\I K_0$ are needed: without either,
the diameter is 4 in every case we checked by computer (every prime power $q\le13$ and every admissible $(a,c)$).

\subsection{Clones and shadow lines}
Copies of points and lines of a GQ go back to Delorme \cite[\S\S7--8]{D85a}: his doubling may copy any induced
substructure, points and lines together, and his $D=4$ construction (Example~\ref{ex:delorme4}) doubles a spread.
Araujo-Pardo, Dalf\'o, Fiol and L\'opez \cite[Sec.~4]{ADFL21} double every line of a GQ to get biregular graphs. The
construction below is not a doubling: a clone is joined to every old line through its point, so no tree condition on the
copied set is needed, and it allows weights larger than one, repeated lines, and ``stand-ins''. (A level-0 line all of
whose points are at level $-1$ is a shadow line with trivial stand-ins.)

\begin{definition}[clones and shadow lines]\label{def:clones}
Let $\cS=(\cP,\cL)$ be a GQ of order $(s,t)$, let $\omega:\cP\to\Z_{\ge0}$ be a \emph{weight}, and $\cM$ a finite
multiset of lines. For each entry $\mu$ of $\cM$, with underlying line $L_\mu$, choose for every point $p\I L_\mu$ a
\emph{stand-in} $\theta_\mu(p)\in\{p,p^1,\dots,p^{\omega(p)}\}$. The incidence structure $\cS[\omega,\cM,\theta]$ has
\begin{itemize}
\item points: $\cP$ and the \emph{clones} $p^1,\dots,p^{\omega(p)}$ of each $p\in\cP$;
\item lines: $\cL$ and one \emph{shadow line} $L^\mu$ for each entry $\mu$ of $\cM$;
\item incidences: those of $\cS$; $p^i\I L$ whenever $p\I L\in\cL$; and $\theta_\mu(p)\I L^\mu$ for $p\I L_\mu$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{table}[ht]
\caption{Neighbourhoods in $\cS[\omega,\cM,\theta]$.}\label{tab:clones}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{6.2cm} p{4.2cm}@{}}
\toprule vertex & neighbours & degree\\ \midrule
clone $p^i$ & the $t+1$ old lines on $p$; the shadow lines $L^\mu$ with $\theta_\mu(p)=p^i$ &
$t+1+\#\{\mu:\theta_\mu(p)=p^i\}$\\
shadow line $L^\mu$ & $\theta_\mu(p)$ for the $s+1$ points $p$ of $L_\mu$ & $s+1$\\
old point $p$ & its $t+1$ lines; the shadow lines $L^\mu$ with $\theta_\mu(p)=p$ & $t+1+\#\{\mu:\theta_\mu(p)=p\}$\\
old line $L$ & its $s+1$ points and all their clones & $s+1+\sum_{p\I L}\omega(p)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

When all stand-ins are old points ($\theta=\mathrm{id}$), a shadow line is adjacent exactly to the old points of $L_\mu$,
and clones are adjacent only to old lines.

\begin{theorem}[clones and shadow lines]\label{thm:B}
Let $\cS$ be a GQ of order $(s,t)$, $s,t\ge1$, and $\cS'=\cS[\omega,\cM,\theta]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\cS'$ has diameter 4.
\item $\cS'$ has maximum degree at most $\Delta$ if and only if $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$, $\sum_{p\I L}\omega(p)\le\Delta-s-1$
  for every line $L$ of $\cS$, and every point of $\cS'$ (old point or clone) is the stand-in for at most $\Delta-t-1$
  entries of $\cM$.
\item $\cS'$ has $(s+1)(st+1)+\sum_p\omega(p)$ points and $(t+1)(st+1)+|\cM|$ lines.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(b) and (c) are read off Table~\ref{tab:clones}. (a) Write $\pi$ for the map sending a clone or a shadow line to its
underlying point or line. Every point $x$ of $\cS'$ is incident with every old line through $\pi(x)$. We use
Lemma~\ref{lem:diamgeom}(i); no element is isolated. Let $(x,Y)$ be an anti-flag of $\cS'$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $Y$ old. Then $\pi(x)\notin Y$. Let $r$ be the point of $Y$ collinear with $\pi(x)$, on the line $N$. Then
  $x\I N\I r\I Y$.
\item $Y=L^\mu$ and $\pi(x)\notin L_\mu$. Let $r\I L_\mu$ be collinear with $\pi(x)$, on the line $N$. Then $x\I N\I
  \theta_\mu(r)\I L^\mu$.
\item $Y=L^\mu$ and $\pi(x)\I L_\mu$. Pick $r\I L_\mu$, $r\ne\pi(x)$ ($s\ge1$). Then $x\I L_\mu\I\theta_\mu(r)\I L^\mu$.
\end{itemize}
So $d(x,Y)\le3$ for every anti-flag, and the diameter is at most 4. Two non-collinear old points exist (a point is
collinear with $s(t+1)<(s+1)(st+1)-1$ others) and stay non-collinear: they share no old line, a shadow line $L^\mu$
contains only points over points of $L_\mu$, and an old point lies over itself only. So the diameter is 4.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[exact weights, regular completion]\label{cor:exact}
Let $\Delta\ge\max(s,t)+1$, let $\omega$ be a weighted $(\Delta-s-1)$-ovoid (that is,
$\sum_{p\I L}\omega(p)=\Delta-s-1$ for every line $L$) and $\cM$ a weighted $(\Delta-t-1)$-cover (every point is on
exactly $\Delta-t-1$ entries of $\cM$). Then $\cS[\omega,\cM,\mathrm{id}]$ has diameter 4, maximum degree $\Delta$ and
$2\Delta(st+1)$ elements. It becomes $\Delta$-regular, with the same order and diameter 4, by the following explicit
flags: number the clones $c_0,c_1,\dots$ and the shadow lines $y_0,\dots,y_{b-1}$ ($b=|\cM|$), put $k=\Delta-t-1$, and
join $c_i$ to $y_j$ for $j\equiv ik,ik+1,\dots,ik+k-1\pmod b$. (If $b=0$ or $\sum_p\omega(p)=0$, the structure is already
$\Delta$-regular.)
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Counting flags gives $\sum_p\omega(p)=(\Delta-s-1)(st+1)$ and $|\cM|=(\Delta-t-1)(st+1)$; the total is
$(s+t+2+\Delta-s-1+\Delta-t-1)(st+1)=2\Delta(st+1)$. Old points and lines have degree $\Delta$, clones $t+1$, shadow lines
$s+1$. The added pairs are anti-flags (with $\theta=\mathrm{id}$ no clone is on a shadow line); the blocks
$\{ik,\dots,ik+k-1\}$, $i=0,1,\dots$, run through $0,1,\dots,k\sum_p\omega(p)-1=b(\Delta-s-1)-1$, so modulo $b$ every
residue is hit exactly $\Delta-s-1$ times, and within a block the residues are distinct since $k\le b$. Adding flags
does not increase distances (Lemma~\ref{lem:flags}), and two non-collinear old points still share no line.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Delorme's $D=4$ construction {\cite[\S8(a)]{D85a}}; the 2013 values]\label{ex:delorme4}
In $W(3,q)$ take a spread $\Sigma$ (a set of $q^2+1$ lines partitioning the points), a line $v\in\Sigma$, and
$\cA_1=\{v\}$, $\cA_0=\Sigma\cup\{\text{points of }v\}$. (L1): the elements on $v$ are its points; a point of $v$ meets
only $v$ in $\cA_1$, and a line of $\Sigma\setminus\{v\}$ meets nothing in $\cA_1$. (L0): a point off $v$ is on exactly
one line of $\Sigma$ and on no other line of $\cA_0$; a line not in $\Sigma$ has at most one point on $v$. The
components of the substructure on $\cA_0$ are the star formed by $v$ and its points, and single lines, so (C$_4$) holds
(Lemma~\ref{lem:hat}). Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme} gives maximum degree $q+m$, diameter 4 and
$2(q+1)(q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl((q^2+1)+(q+1)+m\bigr)$ elements. For $m=2$ this is $2q^3+3q^2+3q+6$: 346, 1734, 3064, 4946 at
$q=5,9,11,13$; for $m=3$, $q=13$ it is $4760+2\cdot187=5134$. These are the 2013 values at $\Delta=7,11,13,15,16$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[a limit for shadow lines on $Q(4,q)$]\label{rem:shadowlimit}
Let $q$ be odd, $\cO$ an ovoid of $Q(4,q)$ (a set of $q^2+1$ points meeting every line once), $\omega=\mathbf 1_\cO$,
and suppose the maximum degree is $q+2$. Then at most $q^2-q+2$ shadow lines are possible. Indeed every old point and
every clone serves at most one shadow line, so a point off $\cO$ is on at most one entry of $\cM$ and a point of $\cO$ on
at most two. Suppose some $o\in\cO$ is on two entries. They are distinct lines: if both were copies of one line $N$, each
of the $q$ points of $N$ other than $o$ is off $\cO$, has no clone, and would be the stand-in of both entries, giving it
degree $q+3$. Each other entry $L$ misses $o$ and so has a unique point $y$ collinear with $o$; $y\notin\cO$ (points of
an ovoid are pairwise non-collinear); the line $oy$ is not an entry of $\cM$ (otherwise $y\notin\cO$ would be on two
entries), so it is one of the $q-1$ lines on $o$ other than the two entries through $o$, and $y$ is one of its $q$ points
other than $o$; distinct $L$ give distinct $y$. So $|\cM|\le2+q(q-1)$. Otherwise every point is on at most one entry,
$\cM$ is a partial spread, and $|\cM|\le q^2-q+1$ (Tallini \cite{Tal91}; see also \cite[\S1]{CDPS22}).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[$(7,4)\ge359$]\label{ex:359}
In $Q(4,5)$ take one clone of each point of an ovoid $\cO$ that is an elliptic quadric section ($\omega=\mathbf
1_\cO$), and 21 shadow lines with stand-ins. Theorem~\ref{thm:B} gives diameter 4, maximum degree 7, and
$2\cdot6\cdot26+26+21=359$ elements. Appendix~\ref{app:359} lists the 21 lines and the stand-ins in coordinates, so that
the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:B}(b) can be checked by hand. Every ovoid of $Q(4,p)$, $p$ prime, is an elliptic
quadric \cite{BGS06}, so the choice of ovoid does not matter. By Remark~\ref{rem:shadowlimit} at most $q^2-q+2=22$ shadow
lines are possible in this setting; 21 is the maximum for this family (an integer program, solved to optimality).
This example is not a member of a proved infinite family. The best regular graph we know at $(7,4)$ has 350 vertices
(Corollary~\ref{cor:A1} at $q=5$).
\end{example}

\subsection{The Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle and two elliptic quadrics}\label{ssec:A}

\subsubsection*{Coordinates}
Let $q$ be odd. Let $W(q)=W(3,q)$ be the symplectic quadrangle of $\PG(3,q)$ for the alternating form
\[
\beta(u,v)=u_0v_1-u_1v_0+u_2v_3-u_3v_2,
\]
and let $x=(1:0:0:0)$; then $x^\perp$ is the plane $X_1=0$. Payne's derived quadrangle $\AS(q)=P(W(q),x)$
\cite{P71}, \cite[3.1.4]{PT} has
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{points}: the $q^3$ points $(a:1:b:c)$, $a,b,c\in\F$ (the points off $x^\perp$); we write $(a,w)$ with
  $w=(b,c)$;
\item \emph{lines of type (i)} (\emph{fibres}), $q^2$ of them: $\{(a_0+\mu:1:w_0):\mu\in\F\}$, the line
  $\langle x,(a_0:1:w_0)\rangle$ minus $x$;
\item \emph{lines of type (ii)}, $q^2(q+1)$ of them: for a point $(a_0:1:w_0)$ and a direction $d=(d_1:d_2)\in\PG(1,q)$,
  \[
  \ell(a_0,w_0;d)=\{(a_0+\lambda\rho:1:w_0+\lambda d):\lambda\in\F\},\qquad \rho=\det(w_0,d)=b_0d_2-c_0d_1,
  \]
  the totally isotropic line through $(a_0:1:w_0)$ and $(\rho:0:d_1:d_2)$, minus the latter point.
\end{itemize}
(Check: $\beta((a_0,1,b_0,c_0),(\rho,0,d_1,d_2))=-\rho+b_0d_2-c_0d_1=0$. A point off $x^\perp$ is on $q+1$ totally isotropic
lines, none through $x$, each meeting $x^\perp$ in one point $(\rho:0:d)$, $d\ne0$; so each point of $\AS(q)$ is on one
fibre and on $q+1$ lines of type (ii), and every line has $q$ points.) $\AS(q)$ is a GQ of order $(q-1,q+1)$ \cite{P71},
\cite[3.1.4, 3.2.6]{PT}; for $q$ odd it is the Ahrens--Szekeres quadrangle \cite{AS69}. The fibres form a spread.

Fix a non-square $\eps\in\F$ and put $N(b,c)=b^2-\eps c^2$, an anisotropic form ($N(w)=0$ only for $w=0$). For
$\kappa\in\F^*$ let $\cE_\kappa$ be the quadric $X_0X_1=\kappa N(X_2,X_3)$. It is elliptic (a hyperbolic pair plus an
anisotropic binary form), passes through $x$ with tangent plane $x^\perp$, and
$\cE_\kappa\setminus\{x\}=\{(\kappa N(w):1:w):w\in\F^2\}$ is a set of $q^2$ points of $\AS(q)$, one on each fibre.

\begin{theorem}[a weighted 2-ovoid of $\AS(q)$]\label{thm:A}
Let $q$ be odd, $\eps$ a non-square and $\kappa_1\kappa_2=1/(4\eps)$. Every line of $\AS(q)$ meets
$\cE_{\kappa_1}\setminus\{x\}$ and $\cE_{\kappa_2}\setminus\{x\}$ in exactly two points in total (a point in both counted
twice). The two quadrics meet only in $x$ and $e=(0:1:0:0)$. So
$\varpi=\mathbf 1_{\cE_{\kappa_1}\setminus x}+\mathbf 1_{\cE_{\kappa_2}\setminus x}$ is a weighted 2-ovoid of $\AS(q)$, with
$\varpi(e)=2$ and $\varpi\in\{0,1\}$ elsewhere.
\end{theorem}

The choice $\kappa_1=\frac12$, $\kappa_2=\frac1{2\eps}$ gives the two quadrics
\[
\cE^{(1)}:\ 2X_0X_1=X_2^2-\eps X_3^2,\qquad \cE^{(2)}:\ 2\eps X_0X_1=X_2^2-\eps X_3^2 .
\]

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:A1}
For every odd prime power $q$, adjoining to $\AS(q)$ one clone of each point of $\cE^{(1)}\setminus\{x\}$ and one of each
point of $\cE^{(2)}\setminus\{x\}$ (so $e$ gets two clones) gives a $(q+2)$-regular incidence structure of diameter 4 with
$2q^2(q+2)$ elements. Hence $\nb(q+2,4)\ge2q^2(q+2)$, attained by a regular graph.
\end{corollary}

\emph{New vertices.} For $P=(\kappa_iN(w):1:w)\in\cE_{\kappa_i}\setminus\{x\}$ ($i=1,2$, $w\in\F^2$), the clone $P^{(i)}$ is
adjacent exactly to the $q+2$ lines of $\AS(q)$ through $P$: the fibre $\{(\kappa_iN(w)+\mu:1:w):\mu\in\F\}$ and, for each
$d\in\PG(1,q)$, the line $\ell(\kappa_iN(w),w;d)$. The two clones of $e$ are adjacent to the same $q+2$ lines. Changed old
neighbourhoods: the fibre over $w_0$ gains the clones of $(\kappa_1N(w_0):1:w_0)$ and $(\kappa_2N(w_0):1:w_0)$; the line
$\ell(a_0,w_0;d)$ gains the clones of its points on $\cE_{\kappa_1}$ and on $\cE_{\kappa_2}$, which correspond to the roots
$\lambda$ of the quadratic \eqref{eq:quadratic} below with $\kappa=\kappa_1,\kappa_2$ (two clones in total). Old points
are unchanged.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}]
\emph{Fibres.} The fibre over $w_0$ meets $\cE_\kappa$ only at $\mu=\kappa N(w_0)-a_0$: one point of each quadric.

\emph{Lines of type (ii).} Let $B=N(w_0+d)-N(w_0)-N(d)=2(b_0d_1-\eps c_0d_2)$. The point with parameter $\lambda$ of
$\ell(a_0,w_0;d)$ lies on $\cE_\kappa$ if and only if $a_0+\lambda\rho=\kappa N(w_0+\lambda d)$, that is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
\kappa N(d)\lambda^2+(\kappa B-\rho)\lambda+\kappa N(w_0)-a_0=0.
\end{equation}
As $N(d)\ne0$ this is a quadratic, with $1+\chi(\mathrm{disc}_\kappa)$ roots. A direct expansion gives
$B^2-4N(w_0)N(d)=4\eps(b_0d_2-c_0d_1)^2=4\eps\rho^2$, hence
\[
\mathrm{disc}_\kappa=(\kappa B-\rho)^2-4\kappa N(d)(\kappa N(w_0)-a_0)=4\kappa N(d)\,(a_0-a^*_\kappa),\qquad
a^*_\kappa=-\frac{4\eps\kappa\rho^2-2B\rho+\rho^2/\kappa}{4N(d)} .
\]
Then $a^*_{\kappa_1}-a^*_{\kappa_2}=-\frac{\rho^2}{4N(d)}(\kappa_1-\kappa_2)\bigl(4\eps-\frac1{\kappa_1\kappa_2}\bigr)=0$; call
the common value $a^*$. The line meets the two quadrics in
\[
\bigl(1+\chi(\kappa_1)\chi(N(d)(a_0-a^*))\bigr)+\bigl(1+\chi(\kappa_2)\chi(N(d)(a_0-a^*))\bigr)=2
\]
points, because $\chi(\kappa_1)\chi(\kappa_2)=\chi(1/(4\eps))=\chi(\eps)=-1$, so $\chi(\kappa_1)+\chi(\kappa_2)=0$. (If
$a_0=a^*$, the line is tangent to both quadrics and meets each in one point.)

\emph{Intersection.} $\kappa_1\ne\kappa_2$, since $\kappa_1^2=1/(4\eps)$ is impossible for a non-square $\eps$. So
$\kappa_1N(w)=\kappa_2N(w)$ forces $N(w)=0$, $w=0$, and the common points are $x$ and $e$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:A1}]
Theorem~\ref{thm:B} and Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} with $(s,t)=(q-1,q+1)$, $\omega=\varpi$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:A} and
$\cM=\emptyset$: every line has $q$ points and gains exactly two clones, every old point is on $q+2$ lines, and every
clone is on the $q+2$ lines of its point. The order is $q^3+q^2(q+2)+2q^2=2q^2(q+2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Weights are necessary: $\AS(3)$ has no 1- or 2-ovoid and $\AS(5)$ has no $m$-ovoid for $m=1,\dots,4$ (computer-checked
by integer programming). No choice of clones and shadow lines on $\AS(q)$ does better at $\Delta=q+2$: every point of
$\AS(q)$ is already on $t+1=q+2$ lines, so no shadow line can be added, and each line has $q$ points and takes at most 2
clones, so $\sum_p\omega(p)\le2q^2$. Corollary~\ref{cor:A1} beats Delorme's $2q^3+3q^2+3q+6$ by $q^2-3q-6>0$ for $q\ge5$.
\end{remark}

\noindent\emph{Cells.} $(11,4)$: 1782 (2013: 1734); $(13,4)$: 3146 (3064); $(15,4)$: 5070 (4946). All regular.

\subsection{Singer 2-ovoids of $W(3,q)$}

\subsubsection*{The cyclic model}
Let $q$ be odd, $\F=\GF(q)\subset K=\GF(q^2)\subset E=\GF(q^4)$, and $g$ a primitive element of $E$. As $E$ is a
4-dimensional $\F$-space, $\PG(3,q)=E^*/\F^*$ (any $\F$-basis of $E$ gives homogeneous coordinates); the point $\langle
g^i\rangle$ has index $i$ modulo $(q+1)(q^2+1)$. Write $\bar y=y^{q^2}$ (the involution of $E/K$), $k'=k^q$ for $k\in K$,
$\Nm(y)=y\bar y\in K$ and $\Tr=\Tr_{K/\F}$. Let $d=g^{(q^2+1)/2}$; then $\bar d=-d$, and $h=d^2=g^{q^2+1}$ generates
$K^*$. The form
\[
b(y,z)=\Tr_{E/\F}(d\,y\bar z)=\Tr\bigl(d(y\bar z-\bar yz)\bigr)
\]
is alternating and non-degenerate; $W(3,q)$ is its symplectic quadrangle. Multiplication by $g^{q+1}$ multiplies $b$ by
$g^{(q+1)(q^2+1)}\in\F^*$, so it is a collineation of $W(3,q)$. Its point orbits are the classes
\[
C_a=\{\langle g^i\rangle:\ i\equiv a\pmod{q+1}\},\qquad |C_a|=q^2+1,
\]
and $\langle y\rangle\in C_a$ if and only if $\Nm(y)\in h^a\F^*$ (as $\Nm(g^i)=h^i$ and $K^*/\F^*$ is cyclic of order
$q+1$, generated by $h$). These are Ebert's elliptic quadrics partitioning $\PG(3,q)$ \cite{E85}.

The $K$-subspaces $yK$ ($y\in E^*$) are lines of $\PG(3,q)$ (the regular spread), with points
$\langle g^{i+(q^2+1)j}\rangle$, $j=0,\dots,q$. They are totally isotropic: for $k_1,k_2\in K$ we have
$\overline{yk_i}=\bar yk_i$ (as $\bar k=k^{q^2}=k$), so $b(yk_1,yk_2)=\Tr\bigl(d\,\Nm(y)(k_1k_2-k_1k_2)\bigr)=0$.
So $\Sigma=\{yK\}$ is a spread of $W(3,q)$ with $q^2+1$ lines.

\begin{proposition}[Singer 2-ovoids]\label{prop:C}
Let $q$ be odd.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Every line of $\PG(3,q)$ meets each class $C_a$ in at most two points.
\item Every totally isotropic line of $W(3,q)$ meets $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ in exactly two points.
\end{enumerate}
So the $(q+1)/2$ sets $C_a\cup C_{q-a}$, $0\le a\le(q-1)/2$, are 2-ovoids of $W(3,q)$ partitioning its points. (The pairing
$a\leftrightarrow q-a$ has no fixed point, as $2a\equiv-1\pmod{q+1}$ is impossible for $q+1$ even.)
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $L=\langle y,z\rangle_\F$ and $u=sy+tz$ ($s,t\in\F$). Then $\Nm(u)=A_1s^2+\tau st+A_2t^2$ with $A_1=\Nm(y)$,
$A_2=\Nm(z)$, $\tau=y\bar z+\bar yz$, all in $K$. Put $\theta=y\bar z-\bar yz$. As $\bar\theta=-\theta$, $\theta/d\in K$; write
$\theta=dk_0$ with $k_0\in K$. Then
\[
\mathfrak d:=\tau^2-4A_1A_2=\theta^2=hk_0^2,\qquad b(y,z)=\Tr(hk_0).
\]
Fix $\varkappa\in K^*$ with $\varkappa'=-\varkappa$. For $\mu\in K^*$ put $\nu=\varkappa/\mu$; the kernel of
$k\mapsto\Tr(\nu k)$ is the $\F$-subspace $\mu\F$. So $\langle u\rangle$ lies in
$\widetilde C_\mu:=\{\langle u\rangle:\Nm(u)\in\mu\F^*\}$ (thus $C_a=\widetilde C_{h^a}$) if and only if $(s:t)$ is a zero of
the binary form
\[
f_\nu(s,t)=\Tr(\nu A_1)s^2+\Tr(\nu\tau)st+\Tr(\nu A_2)t^2 .
\]
(a) Suppose $f_\nu\equiv0$. Then $A_1,\tau,A_2\in\mu\F$. If $\mathfrak d\ne0$, then $hk_0^2=\mathfrak d\in\mu^2\F^*$, so
$h\in(K^*)^2\F^*=(K^*)^2$ (every element of $\F^*$ is a square in $K$, since $q-1$ divides $(q^2-1)/2$); but $h$ generates
$K^*$, which has even order. If $\mathfrak d=0$, then $\Nm(sy+tz)=\mu(\alpha_0s^2+\beta_0st+\gamma_0t^2)$ with
$\alpha_0,\beta_0,\gamma_0\in\F$, $\beta_0^2=4\alpha_0\gamma_0$ and $\alpha_0\ne0$ (as $A_1\ne0$), which vanishes at
$(s:t)=(-\beta_0:2\alpha_0)$: impossible, since $\Nm(u)\ne0$ for $u\ne0$. So $f_\nu\not\equiv0$, and it has
$1+\chi(\mathrm{disc}(\nu))\le2$ projective zeros, where
$\mathrm{disc}(\nu)=\Tr(\nu\tau)^2-4\Tr(\nu A_1)\Tr(\nu A_2)$.

(b) Expanding with $\Tr(\nu k)=\nu k+\nu'k'$,
\[
\mathrm{disc}(\nu)=\Tr(\nu^2\mathfrak d)+\nu\nu'\Psi,\qquad \Psi=2\tau\tau'-4A_1A_2'-4A_1'A_2\in\F .
\]
If $L$ is totally isotropic, then $\Tr(hk_0)=0$, so $hk_0\in\varkappa\F$ and $\eta:=h\mathfrak d=(hk_0)^2\in\varkappa^2\F\subseteq\F$.
Then $\Tr(h^2\nu'^2\mathfrak d)=\eta(h\nu'^2+h'\nu^2)=hh'\,\Tr(\nu^2\mathfrak d)$, and since $(h\nu')(h\nu')'=hh'\nu\nu'$,
\[
\mathrm{disc}(h\nu')=\Nm_{K/\F}(h)\,\mathrm{disc}(\nu).
\]
$\Nm_{K/\F}(h)=h^{q+1}$ generates $\F^*$, so it is a non-square and $\chi(\mathrm{disc}(h\nu'))=-\chi(\mathrm{disc}(\nu))$. The
class belonging to $h\nu'$ is $\varkappa/(h\nu')=-\mu'/h\equiv(h\mu)^{-1}$ modulo $\F^*$ (using $\mu^{q+1}\in\F^*$); in
indices, $a\mapsto-a-1\equiv q-a\pmod{q+1}$. Scaling $\nu$ by $\F^*$ multiplies $\mathrm{disc}$ by a square. So $L$ meets
$C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ in $(1+\chi(\mathrm{disc}(\nu)))+(1-\chi(\mathrm{disc}(\nu)))=2$ points.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:C1}
Let $q$ be odd, $0\le a\le(q-1)/2$, $\cO=C_a\cup C_{q-a}$ and $\Sigma$ the regular spread $\{yK\}$. Adjoin to $W(3,q)$
\begin{itemize}
\item one clone $P'$ of each point $P\in\cO$, incident with the $q+1$ totally isotropic lines on $P$ (the lines $\langle
  P,Z\rangle$ with $b(P,Z)=0$);
\item two shadow lines $L',L''$ for each $L\in\Sigma$, each incident with the $q+1$ points of $L$.
\end{itemize}
The result is bipartite, has diameter 4, maximum degree $q+3$ (old points and lines have degree $q+3$, clones and shadow
lines $q+1$) and $2(q^2+1)(q+3)$ elements. Joining clone $c_i$ to shadow lines $y_{2i},y_{2i+1}$ (indices modulo
$2(q^2+1)$, any numbering) makes it $(q+3)$-regular with diameter 4. Hence $\nb(q+3,4)\ge2(q^2+1)(q+3)$, attained by a
regular graph.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$W(3,q)$ is a GQ of order $(q,q)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:C}(b), $\omega=\mathbf 1_\cO$ is a 2-ovoid ($=\Delta-s-1$ for
$\Delta=q+3$), and $\cM=2\Sigma$ is a 2-cover (every point is on exactly one spread line). Apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} with $k=2$.
\end{proof}

This is the point--line dual of clones of an ovoid of $Q(4,q)$ with a dual 2-cover; written in $W(3,q)$ it needs no
Klein correspondence. More generally, $j$ copies of the spread and $j/2$ copies of the 2-ovoid give
$\nb(q+1+j,4)\ge2(q^2+1)(q+1+j)$ for even $j$.

\begin{corollary}[all degrees]\label{cor:B2}
For every prime power $q$ and every $\Delta\ge q+1$ there is a $\Delta$-regular incidence structure of diameter 4 with
$2\Delta(q^2+1)$ elements if $q$ is even or $\Delta-q-1$ is even, and with $2\Delta q^2$ elements otherwise. In particular
$\nb(\Delta,4)\ge2q^2\Delta$ for every prime power $q\le\Delta-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Put $j=\Delta-q-1$. In the first two cases we apply Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} in $W(3,q)$ ($s=t=q$, so
$\Delta-s-1=\Delta-t-1=j$). If $q$ is even, $W(3,q)$ has an ovoid $\cO_0$ and a spread $\Sigma$ \cite[3.4.1]{PT}; take
$\omega=j\mathbf 1_{\cO_0}$ and $\cM=j\Sigma$. If $q$ is odd and $j$ even: $\omega=(j/2)\mathbf 1_\cO$ with $\cO$ a 2-ovoid of
Proposition~\ref{prop:C}, and $\cM=j\Sigma$. If $q$ is odd and $j$ odd, then $\Delta\ge q+2$, and we apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} in $\AS(q)$ ($s=q-1$, $t=q+1$, so $\Delta-s-1=\Delta-q$ and $\Delta-t-1=\Delta-q-2$), with
$(\Delta-q)/2$ copies of the weighted 2-ovoid of Theorem~\ref{thm:A} and $\Delta-q-2$ copies of the fibre spread.
\end{proof}

Throughout Sections~\ref{ssec:d6coords}--\ref{ssec:thmD}, $q$ is odd; even $q$ is treated in

\subsection{Elliptic hyperplane sections and Hermitian spreads}\label{ssec:d6coords}
Let $v=\langle e_1,e_6\rangle$. It is a line of $Q(6,q)$ ($e_1,e_6$ are singular and $b(e_1,e_6)=0$) and a line of $H(q)$
(no equation of \eqref{eq:tits} involves $p_{16}$). Fix $c\in\F$ with $-c$ a non-square. For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in\F^2$ let
\[
\Pi_z:\ X_0+cX_4+\alpha X_2+\beta X_5=0,\qquad \Pi=\Pi_{(0,0)},
\]
and let $S_z$ be the set of lines of $H(q)$ contained in $\Pi_z$, $S=S_{(0,0)}$. Every $\Pi_z$ contains $v$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Piz}
For every $z\in\F^2$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\Pi_z\cap Q(6,q)$ is an elliptic quadric $Q^-(5,q)$.
\item Every point of $H(q)$ in $\Pi_z$ is on exactly one line of $S_z$. So $|S_z|=q^3+1$, the lines of $S_z$ are pairwise
  opposite, and $v\in S_z$ ($S_z$ is a Hermitian spread \cite{T80}).
\item A line of $H(q)$ not in $S_z$ meets $\Pi_z$ in exactly one point.
\item The points of $H(q)$ at distance at most 3 from $v$ are exactly the points of $Q(6,q)$ with $X_2=X_5=0$; among them
  only the points of $v$ lie in $\Pi_z$.
\item $S_z\cap S_{z'}=\{v\}$ for $z\ne z'$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) On $\Pi_z$ substitute $X_0=-cX_4-\alpha X_2-\beta X_5$:
$X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6-X_3^2=(X_1-\beta X_4)X_5+X_2(X_6-\alpha X_4)-(X_3^2+cX_4^2)$, two hyperbolic planes plus the
anisotropic form $X_3^2+cX_4^2$. So the form on $\Pi_z$ is non-degenerate of Witt index 2.

(b) Let $y\in\Pi_z\cap Q(6,q)$. By (a), $\Pi_z\cap Q(6,q)$ contains no totally singular plane, so $\pi_y\not\subseteq\Pi_z$
and $\pi_y\cap\Pi_z$ is one line through $y$: the only line of $H(q)$ on $y$ inside $\Pi_z$. $Q^-(5,q)$ has
$(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points, so $|S_z|=q^3+1$. Two lines of $S_z$ are not concurrent (uniqueness). If two were at distance 4, a
line $N$ of $H(q)$ would meet both in distinct points, so $N\subseteq\Pi_z$, $N\in S_z$, concurrent with them: a
contradiction. So they are opposite.

(c) A line not in the hyperplane $\Pi_z$ meets it in one point.

(d) Let $y=ae_1+be_6$ be on $v$ and $yu$ a line of $H(q)$. Then $p_{12}=p_{34}$ gives $au_2=0$, $p_{65}=p_{30}$ gives
$bu_5=0$, and $b(y,u)=0$ gives $au_5+bu_2=0$; so $u_2=u_5=0$. Conversely $Q(6,q)\cap\{X_2=X_5=0\}$ is $X_0X_4=X_3^2$, a
cone with vertex $v$ over a conic, with $(q+1)+(q+1)q^2$ points: exactly the number of points at distance at most 3 from
$v$ ($q+1$ on $v$, and $q$ more on each of the $q(q+1)$ other lines meeting $v$). If such a point is in $\Pi_z$, then
$X_0=-cX_4$ and $X_3^2+cX_4^2=0$, so $X_0=X_3=X_4=0$ and the point is on $v$.

(e) If $\ell\in S_z\cap S_{z'}$ with $\ell\ne v$, then $(\alpha-\alpha')X_2+(\beta-\beta')X_5=0$ on $\ell$, so some point of
$\ell$ has $X_2=X_5=0$ and is at distance at most 3 from $v$ by (d); but $\ell$ and $v$ are opposite by (b), so every point
of $\ell$ is at distance 5 from $v$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Delorme's levels and whiskers}
Let $\cP(v)$ be the points of $v$, $\cL_v$ the $q^2+q$ lines other than $v$ that meet $v$, and $\cP_\Pi$ the
$(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points of $H(q)$ in $\Pi$. A point is \emph{free} if it is not in $\Pi$ and is at distance 5 from $v$; by
Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(d) there are $(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)-(q+1)-q^2(q+1)-q^3(q+1)=q^3(q^2-1)$ of them.

\begin{definition}\label{def:whiskers}
A \emph{whisker set} is a set $W$ of lines of $H(q)$, each opposite $v$ and not in $S$, such that no free point is on two
lines of $W$. It is \emph{exact} if every free point is on exactly one line of $W$.
\end{definition}

A line opposite $v$ and not in $S$ meets $\Pi$ in one point, not on $v$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(c),(d)), and its other $q$
points are free (every point of a line opposite $v$ is at distance 5 from $v$). So $|W|\le q^3(q^2-1)/q=q^2(q^2-1)$, with
equality if and only if $W$ is exact.

\begin{theorem}[doubling with whiskers]\label{thm:whiskerdoubling}
Let $W$ be a whisker set and $m\ge2$. Put
\[
\cA_2=\{v\},\qquad \cA_1=\{v\}\cup\cP(v)\cup S,\qquad \cA_0=\cA_1\cup\cP_\Pi\cup\cL_v\cup W .
\]
Then $\cA$ satisfies (L0), (L1) and (C$_6$). Hence $H(q)^{(m)}_\cA$ is bipartite with maximum degree $q+m$, diameter 6,
and
\[
2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(q^4+2q^3+q^2+2q+2+|W|+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr)
\]
elements.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(L1), $i=1$: the elements on $v$ are its points (in $\cA_1$); a point of $v$ is on one element of $\cA_2$, a line of
$S\setminus\{v\}$ on none. (L1), $i=0$: the elements incident with $\cA_1$ are the points of $v$, the lines of $\cL_v$
and the points of $\Pi$, all in $\cA_0$; a line of $\cL_v$ meets $v$ in one point and contains no other point of $\cA_1$;
a point of $\cP_\Pi\setminus\cP(v)$ is on exactly one line of $S$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(b)) and not on $v$; a whisker has
no point on $v$. (L0): a point outside $\cA_0$ is off $\Pi$. If it is at distance 3 from $v$, it is on exactly one line of
$\cL_v$ (two would close a circuit of length at most 6) and on no whisker (a whisker through it would be at distance at
most 4 from $v$). If it is free, it is on no line of $\cL_v$ and on at most one whisker. A line outside $\cA_0$ is not in
$\Pi$ (the lines in $\Pi$ are those of $S$), so it meets $\Pi$ in one point, its only point in $\cA_0$. (C$_6$): a line of
$\cL_v$ has no point of $\Pi$ other than its point on $v$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(d)), and a whisker has exactly one point of
$\Pi$, not on $v$. So the components of the substructure on $\cA_0$ are
\[
\{v\}\cup\cP(v)\cup\cL_v\qquad\text{and}\qquad \{n\}\cup\cP(n)\cup\{h\in W:h\text{ meets }n\}\quad(n\in S\setminus\{v\}),
\]
trees of diameter 4 (the ball of radius 2 about $v$; a line, its points and the whiskers hanging from them).
Lemma~\ref{lem:hat} gives (C$_6$), and Theorem~\ref{thm:delorme} gives degree at most $q+1+(m-1)$, diameter at most 6 and
the count, with $|\cA_2|=1$, $|\cA_1|=(q^3+1)+(q+1)$ and $|\cA_0|=(q^3+1)+(q^2+q)+|W|+(q+1)(q^3+1)$. The diameter is
exactly 6: a free point $p$ (level $-1$) has $q^5$ opposite points, and only $(q+1)(q^3+1)<q^5$ points lie in $\cA_0$, so
some point $p'$ opposite $p$ has level $-1$, and $d(p,p')\ge6$ by projection.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An exact whisker set for every odd $q$}
For a point $X$ of $H(q)$ in $\Pi$, not on $v$, put
\[
\nu(X)=\frac{X_2X_3-X_4X_5}{cX_2^2+X_5^2}.
\]
The denominator vanishes only if $X_2=X_5=0$, that is (Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(d)) only on $v$. Let
\[
G(a,b)=(c_0a+c_1b)(a^2+cb^2)-b^3,\qquad F(z)=\frac{G(\alpha,\beta)}{\alpha^2+c\beta^2}\quad(z=(\alpha,\beta)\ne0),
\]
with $c_0,c_1\in\F$ chosen so that $G$ has no zero on $\F^2\setminus\{0\}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cubic}
Such $c_0,c_1$ exist for every odd $q$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$G(a,0)=c_0a^3$, so $c_0\ne0$ is needed. For $b\ne0$ and $t=a/b$, $G(a,b)=b^3\bigl((c_0t+c_1)(t^2+c)-1\bigr)$, and
$t^2+c\ne0$. So $G$ has a zero with $b\ne0$ if and only if $(c_0,c_1)$ lies on one of the $q$ lines $c_0t+c_1=1/(t^2+c)$,
$t\in\F$, of the $(c_0,c_1)$-plane; each has $q-1$ points with $c_0\ne0$. The lines for $t=0$ and $t=1$ meet at
$c_0=1/(1+c)-1/c=-1/(c(1+c))\ne0$ ($c\ne-1$, as $-c$ is a non-square). So these lines cover at most $q(q-1)-1$ of the
$q(q-1)$ points with $c_0\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[exact whiskers]\label{thm:whiskers}
Let $q$ be odd, $-c$ a non-square, and $c_0,c_1$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic}. Then
\[
W=\bigl\{\ell\in S_z\setminus\{v\}:\ z\in\F^2\setminus\{0\},\ \nu(\ell\cap\Pi)=F(z)\bigr\}
\]
is an exact whisker set: every free point lies on exactly one line of $W$, and $|W|=q^2(q^2-1)$.
\end{theorem}

Here $\ell\cap\Pi$ is one point not on $v$: $\ell\subseteq\Pi_z$ and $\ell\ne v$, so $\ell\notin S$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(e), and $\ell$ is opposite $v$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(b). The proof uses a complex structure on each
$\Pi_z$. Write a point of $\Pi_z$ as $x=(x_1,\dots,x_6)$, meaning
$(X_0,\dots,X_6)=(-cx_4-\alpha x_2-\beta x_5,x_1,\dots,x_6)$; on $\Pi_z$ the quadratic form is
$Q_z(x)=x_1x_5+x_2x_6-x_3^2-cx_4^2-\alpha x_2x_4-\beta x_4x_5$, with polar form $b_z$. Put
$\mathrm{num}(y)=y_2y_3-y_4y_5$ and $\mathrm{den}(y)=cy_2^2+y_5^2$, and define the linear map
\[
J_zx=\bigl(-\beta x_3+\alpha x_4-x_6,\ x_5,\ -cx_4-\beta x_5,\ x_3+\beta x_2,\ -cx_2,\
cx_1+\alpha\beta x_2+\alpha x_3+c\beta x_4+\beta^2x_5\bigr);
\]
for $z=0$, $J_0x=(-x_6,x_5,-cx_4,x_3,-cx_2,cx_1)$.

\begin{lemma}[complex structure]\label{lem:Jz}
For every $z$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $J_z^2=-c$, $Q_z(J_zx)=cQ_z(x)$ and $b_z(x,J_zx)=0$.
\item The six differences $p_{12}-p_{34}$, $p_{54}-p_{32}$, $p_{20}-p_{35}$, $p_{65}-p_{30}$, $p_{01}-p_{36}$, $p_{46}-p_{31}$ of
  the Grassmann coordinates of $x\wedge J_zx$ equal $Q_z(x)$ times $1,0,0,-c,\alpha,-\beta$.
\item For every point $x$ of $Q(6,q)\cap\Pi_z$, the line $\langle x,J_zx\rangle$ is the line of $S_z$ through $x$.
\item $\mathrm{num}(sx+tJ_0x)=(s^2+ct^2)\,\mathrm{num}(x)$ and $\mathrm{den}(sx+tJ_0x)=(s^2+ct^2)\,\mathrm{den}(x)$. So $\nu$
  is constant on every line of $S\setminus\{v\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a), (b) and (d) are polynomial identities with integer coefficients, verified by direct expansion. For instance, for
$z=0$: $Q_0(J_0x)=cx_2x_6+cx_1x_5-c^2x_4^2-cx_3^2=cQ_0(x)$; $p_{12}=x_1x_5+x_2x_6$ and $p_{34}=x_3^2+cx_4^2$, so
$p_{12}-p_{34}=Q_0(x)$; in (d), $\mathrm{num}(J_0x)=c\,\mathrm{num}(x)$ and $\mathrm{den}(J_0x)=c\,\mathrm{den}(x)$, and the
polar forms $x_2y_3+x_3y_2-x_4y_5-x_5y_4$ and $2cx_2y_2+2x_5y_5$ vanish at $y=J_0x$. (c) $J_z$ has no eigenvector ($-c$ is
a non-square), so $x$ and $J_zx$ span a line; if $Q_z(x)=0$, then by (a) the whole line is singular and by (b) its
Grassmann coordinates satisfy \eqref{eq:tits}, so it is a line of $H(q)$ in $\Pi_z$ through $x$, the one of
Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(b). (d) By (c), a line $n\in S\setminus\{v\}$ is $\{sx+tJ_0x\}$ for any of its points $x$, and
$s^2+ct^2\ne0$.
\end{proof}

Let $p$ be a free point. By Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(d), $(p_2,p_5)\ne(0,0)$; as $p\notin\Pi$ we normalise $p_0+cp_4=1$. Then
$p\in\Pi_z$ if and only if $z$ lies on the affine line
\[
A_p=\{(\alpha,\beta):\ \alpha p_2+\beta p_5=-1\}\subseteq\F^2\setminus\{0\},
\]
which has $q$ points and direction $(p_5,-p_2)$. For $z\in A_p$ let $\ell_p(z)$ be the line of $S_z$ on $p$ and
$X(z)=\ell_p(z)\cap\Pi$.

\begin{lemma}[key identity]\label{lem:key}
For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in A_p$,
\[
\Phi_p(z):=\nu(X(z))+\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+c\beta^2}=\frac{p_2p_3-p_4p_5+\beta p_2^2}{cp_2^2+p_5^2}.
\]
So $\Phi_p$ is affine on $A_p$, with increment $-p_2^3/(cp_2^2+p_5^2)$ along $(p_5,-p_2)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $\Theta=cp_2^2+p_5^2\ne0$ and $\gamma=-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2$. On $\Pi_z$ the linear form $\Lambda(y)=y_0+cy_4$ equals
$-\alpha y_2-\beta y_5$, and $\Pi=\{\Lambda=0\}$. We have $\Lambda(p)=1$ and $\Lambda(J_zp)=-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2=\gamma$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:Jz}(c), $\ell_p(z)=\langle p,J_zp\rangle$, so $X(z)=\gamma p-J_zp$, with
\[
X_2=\gamma p_2-p_5,\quad X_3=\gamma p_3+cp_4+\beta p_5,\quad X_4=\gamma p_4-p_3-\beta p_2,\quad X_5=\gamma p_5+cp_2 .
\]
Expanding, $\mathrm{den}(X)=(\gamma^2+c)\Theta$ and
$\mathrm{num}(X)=(\gamma^2+c)(p_2p_3-p_4p_5)+\beta(2\gamma p_2p_5+cp_2^2-p_5^2)$. Solving $\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=-1$,
$-\alpha p_5+c\beta p_2=\gamma$ (determinant $\Theta$) gives $\alpha=-(cp_2+\gamma p_5)/\Theta$ and
$\beta=(\gamma p_2-p_5)/\Theta$, hence $\alpha^2+c\beta^2=(\gamma^2+c)/\Theta\ne0$ and
$\beta^3/(\alpha^2+c\beta^2)=\beta(\gamma p_2-p_5)^2/((\gamma^2+c)\Theta)$. The two $\beta$-terms add up to
$\beta\bigl(2\gamma p_2p_5+cp_2^2-p_5^2+(\gamma p_2-p_5)^2\bigr)/((\gamma^2+c)\Theta)=\beta p_2^2/\Theta$. Along $(p_5,-p_2)$,
$\beta$ changes by $-p_2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskers}]
Let $p$ be free. The lines of $\bigcup_{z\ne0}S_z\setminus\{v\}$ on $p$ are exactly the $q$ lines $\ell_p(z)$, $z\in
A_p$, and they are distinct (a common line would lie in $S_z\cap S_{z'}=\{v\}$); for the same reason each line of $W$
belongs to a single $S_z$. As $F(z)=c_0\alpha+c_1\beta-\beta^3/(\alpha^2+c\beta^2)$, we have $\ell_p(z)\in W$ if and only if
$\Phi_p(z)-(c_0\alpha+c_1\beta)=0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:key} the left side is affine on $A_p$ with increment
\[
-\frac{p_2^3}{\Theta}-(c_0p_5-c_1p_2)=-\frac{G(p_5,-p_2)}{\Theta}\ne0
\]
along $(p_5,-p_2)$, so it vanishes at exactly one of the $q$ points of $A_p$: $p$ is on exactly one line of $W$. The lines
of $W$ are opposite $v$ and not in $S$. Counting incidences between free points and $W$ gives $|W|=q^3(q^2-1)/q$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The bound}\label{ssec:thmD}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:D}
For every odd prime power $q$ and every $m\ge2$, the structure of Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskerdoubling} with the exact whisker
set of Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskers} is bipartite, has maximum degree $q+m$ and diameter 6, and
\[
\nb(q+m,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl(2q^4+2q^3+2q+2+m(q^3+q+2)+m^2\bigr).
\]
In particular $\nb(q+2,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+2q^4+4q^3+4q+10$ and
$\nb(q+3,6)\ge2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+4q^4+10q^3+10q+34$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Theorems~\ref{thm:whiskerdoubling} and~\ref{thm:whiskers}, with $|W|=q^4-q^2$.
\end{proof}

With $W$ exact, every element outside $\cA_0$ is incident with exactly one element of $\cA_0$. For a doubling of a
generalized $n$-gon of order $(q,q)$ with $N$ points, counting incidences gives $q|\cA_0|+2k\le2N$, $k$ the number of
components of $\cA_0$; here $k=q^3+1$ and $q(2q^4+2q^3+2q+2)+2(q^3+1)=2N$, so the single-level part is as large as this
counting bound allows for $q^3+1$ components.

\begin{remark}[Delorme's construction; the 2013 values]\label{rem:delorme6}
$W=\emptyset$ is Delorme's doubling of a generalized hexagon \cite[\S8(b)]{D85a}: he takes $q^3+1$ pairwise opposite
elements (citing \cite{T80}; here the Hermitian spread $S$), puts one of them in $\cA_2$, its neighbours and the others in
$\cA_1$, and their neighbours in $\cA_0$, so $|\cA_1|=q^3+q+2$ and $|\cA_0|=q^4+2q^3+q^2+2q+2$, exactly as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskerdoubling} with $W=\emptyset$; at $q=5$ he states $7812+912+2\cdot132+4=8992$. At $m=2$ this has
$2(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+q^4+4q^3+q^2+4q+10$ elements: 8992, 142464, 374452, 842048 at $q=5,9,11,13$; at $q=13$, $m=3$, it has
884062. These are the 2013 values at $\Delta=7,11,13,15,16$. The exact whiskers add $(m-1)q^2(q^2-1)$.
\end{remark}

\emph{Worked counts.} $q=5$, $m=2$: $H(5)$ has $2\cdot6\cdot651=7812$ elements, $|\cA_0|=1512$ (of which $|W|=600$),
$|\cA_1|=132$, $|\cA_2|=1$, so the order is $7812+1512+2\cdot132+4=9592$. $q=13$, $m=3$: $H(13)$ has 804468 elements,
$|\cA_0|=61544$ ($|W|=28392$), $|\cA_1|=2212$, so the order is $804468+2(61544+3\cdot2212+9)=940846$.

\noindent\emph{Cells.} $m=2$: $(7,6)$ 9592 (2013: 8992), $(11,6)$ 148944 (142464), $(13,6)$ 388972 (374452), $(15,6)$ 870440
(842048). $m=3$, $q=13$: $(16,6)$ 940846 (884062). These graphs are not regular; see Section~\ref{ssec:thmR}.

\subsection{Even $q$}\label{ssec:evenq}
Let $q=2^e$, $\Tr$ the absolute trace of $\F$, and fix $\delta\in\F$ with $\Tr(\delta)=1$, so that $t^2+t+\delta$ is
irreducible and $a^2+ab+\delta b^2$ is anisotropic. Now $Q(6,q)$ is $X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6+X_3^2=0$, with nucleus $e_3$,
and $H(q)$ is given by the same equations \eqref{eq:tits}. Put
\[
\Pi_z:\ X_3=X_0+\delta X_4+\alpha X_2+\beta X_5\qquad(z=(\alpha,\beta)\in\F^2),\qquad \Pi=\Pi_{(0,0)},
\]
$S_z$ the lines of $H(q)$ in $\Pi_z$, and $v=\langle e_1,e_6\rangle\subseteq\Pi_z$ as before.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Piz-even}
Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz} holds verbatim for even $q$ with these $\Pi_z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) On $\Pi_z$, $X_0X_4+X_1X_5+X_2X_6+X_3^2=(X_0^2+X_0X_4+\delta^2X_4^2)+(X_1+\beta^2X_5)X_5+X_2(X_6+\alpha^2X_2)$. The first
term is anisotropic ($\Tr(\delta^2)=\Tr(\delta)=1$) and the polar form is non-degenerate on $\Pi_z$ (which misses $e_3$), so
$\Pi_z\cap Q(6,q)$ is an elliptic quadric $Q^-(5,q)$. (b), (c), (e) are proved as in Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}. (d) The same
three equations give $u_2=u_5=0$; on $X_2=X_5=0$ the quadric is the cone $X_0X_4=X_3^2$, and in $\Pi_z$ this becomes
$X_0^2+X_0X_4+\delta^2X_4^2=0$, so $X_0=X_4=X_3=0$, a point of $v$.
\end{proof}

So Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskerdoubling} holds for even $q$ without change. For $X\in\Pi$ not on $v$ put
\begin{gather*}
\nu(X)=\frac{X_2X_3+X_2X_4+X_4X_5}{\delta X_2^2+X_2X_5+X_5^2},\\
G(a,b)=(c_0a+c_1b)(a^2+ab+\delta b^2)+b^3,\qquad F(z)=\frac{G(\alpha,\beta)}{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2}.
\end{gather*}
The denominator of $\nu$ is anisotropic and vanishes only on $v$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cubic-even}
There are $c_0,c_1\in\F$ with $G$ zero-free on $\F^2\setminus\{0\}$ if and only if $q\ge4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$G(a,0)=c_0a^3$ needs $c_0\ne0$. For $b\ne0$ and $t=a/b$: $G=0$ if and only if $c_0t+c_1=r(t):=1/(t^2+t+\delta)$, a line in
the $(c_0,c_1)$-plane with $q-1$ points having $c_0\ne0$. If $q\ge4$ take $\varpi\notin\{0,1\}$; then $r(\varpi)\ne r(0)$
(as $\varpi^2+\varpi\ne0$), so the lines for $t=0,\varpi$ meet at $c_0=(r(0)+r(\varpi))/\varpi\ne0$, and the $q$ lines cover
fewer than $q(q-1)$ points with $c_0\ne0$. If $q=2$ then $\delta=1$, $r(0)=r(1)=1$, and the lines $c_1=1$ and $c_0+c_1=1$
contain both points with $c_0=1$.
\end{proof}

On $\Pi_z$ use coordinates $x=(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_4,x_5,x_6)$, with $X_3=x_0+\delta x_4+\alpha x_2+\beta x_5$, and
\begin{multline*}
J_zx=\bigl((\delta+1)x_0+\delta\alpha x_2+\delta^2x_4+(\alpha+\delta\beta)x_5,\ \beta x_0+x_1+\alpha\beta x_2+(\alpha+\delta\beta)x_4+\beta^2x_5+x_6,\
x_2+x_5,\\ x_0+(\alpha+\beta)x_2+\delta x_4+\beta x_5,\ \delta x_2,\ (\alpha+\beta)x_0+\delta x_1+\alpha^2x_2+\delta\alpha x_4+\alpha\beta
x_5\bigr).
\end{multline*}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Jz-even}
With $Q$ the quadratic form and $b$ its polar form: (a) $J_z^2+J_z+\delta=0$, $Q(J_zx)=\delta Q(x)$ and $b(x,J_zx)=Q(x)$;
(b) the six differences of \eqref{eq:tits} for $x\wedge J_zx$ are $Q(x)$ times $1,0,0,\delta,\alpha,\beta$; (c) for every
point $x$ of $Q(6,q)\cap\Pi_z$, $\langle x,J_zx\rangle$ is the line of $S_z$ on $x$; (d) with
$\mathrm{num}=X_2X_3+X_2X_4+X_4X_5$ and $\mathrm{den}=\delta X_2^2+X_2X_5+X_5^2$ on $\Pi$,
$\mathrm{num}(sx+tJ_0x)=(s^2+st+\delta t^2)\mathrm{num}(x)$, and likewise for $\mathrm{den}$; so $\nu$ is constant on every
line of $S\setminus\{v\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a), (b), (d) are polynomial identities over $\F_2[\delta,\alpha,\beta,x]$, verified by expansion. (c) $J_z$ has no
eigenvector since $t^2+t+\delta$ is irreducible; if $Q(x)=0$ then $Q(sx+tJ_zx)=(s^2+st+\delta t^2)Q(x)=0$ by (a), and by (b)
the line satisfies \eqref{eq:tits}, so it is the line of Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz-even}(b). In (d), $s^2+st+\delta t^2\ne0$ for
$(s,t)\ne0$.
\end{proof}

Let $p$ be free; then $(p_2,p_5)\ne(0,0)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz-even}(d), and we normalise $p_0+p_3+\delta p_4=1$. With
$\Lambda(y)=y_0+y_3+\delta y_4$ we have $\Pi=\{\Lambda=0\}$ and $\Lambda=\alpha y_2+\beta y_5$ on $\Pi_z$, so $p\in\Pi_z$ if and
only if $z$ lies on the affine line $A_p:\ \alpha p_2+\beta p_5=1$ (direction $(p_5,p_2)$). For $z\in A_p$ let $\ell_p(z)$ be
the line of $S_z$ on $p$ and $X(z)=\ell_p(z)\cap\Pi$.

\begin{lemma}[key identity, characteristic 2]\label{lem:key-even}
For $z=(\alpha,\beta)\in A_p$,
\[
\Phi_p(z):=\nu(X(z))+\frac{\beta^3}{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2}=\frac{p_2p_3+p_2p_4+p_4p_5+\beta
p_2^2}{\delta p_2^2+p_2p_5+p_5^2},
\]
which is affine on $A_p$ with increment $p_2^3/\Theta$ along $(p_5,p_2)$, $\Theta=\delta p_2^2+p_2p_5+p_5^2\ne0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\Lambda(p)=1$ and $\gamma:=\Lambda(J_zp)=\alpha(p_2+p_5)+\delta\beta p_2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:Jz-even}(c), $X(z)=\gamma p+J_zp$
(then $\Lambda(X)=2\gamma=0$); $X_2=(\gamma+1)p_2+p_5$ and $X_5=\delta p_2+\gamma p_5$. Expanding,
$\mathrm{den}(X)=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\Theta$ and
$\mathrm{num}(X)=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)(p_2p_3+p_2p_4+p_4p_5)+\beta\bigl((\gamma+\delta+1)p_2^2+p_5^2\bigr)$, with
$\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta\ne0$. Solving $\alpha p_2+\beta p_5=1$, $\alpha(p_2+p_5)+\delta\beta p_2=\gamma$ (determinant $\Theta$):
$\alpha=(\delta p_2+\gamma p_5)/\Theta$ and $\beta=((\gamma+1)p_2+p_5)/\Theta$, so
$\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2=(\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)/\Theta$ and
$\beta^3/(\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2)=\beta((\gamma+1)p_2+p_5)^2/((\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\Theta)$. The $\beta$-terms add to
$\beta\bigl((\gamma+\delta+1)+(\gamma+1)^2\bigr)p_2^2/((\gamma^2+\gamma+\delta)\Theta)=\beta p_2^2/\Theta$. Along $(p_5,p_2)$,
$\beta$ changes by $p_2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[exact whiskers, even $q\ge4$]\label{thm:whiskers-even}
With $c_0,c_1$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-even}, $W=\{\ell\in S_z\setminus\{v\}:z\ne0,\ \nu(\ell\cap\Pi)=F(z)\}$ is an
exact whisker set.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
As for Theorem~\ref{thm:whiskers}: the lines of $\bigcup_{z\ne0}S_z\setminus\{v\}$ on a free point $p$ are the $q$
distinct lines $\ell_p(z)$, $z\in A_p$, and $\ell_p(z)\in W$ if and only if $\Phi_p(z)+c_0\alpha+c_1\beta=0$, since
$F(z)=c_0\alpha+c_1\beta+\beta^3/(\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\delta\beta^2)$. The left side is affine on $A_p$ with increment
$p_2^3/\Theta+c_0p_5+c_1p_2=G(p_5,p_2)/\Theta\ne0$, so it vanishes at exactly one point.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:evenq}
Theorem~\ref{thm:D} and Theorem~\ref{thm:R} hold for every even $q\ge4$. For $q=2$ there is also an exact whisker set
(of $12=q^2(q^2-1)$ lines, given explicitly and computer-checked), so they hold for $q=2$ as well.
\end{corollary}

None of this changes a cell with $\Delta\le16$, for any $m$: for $q\in\{2,4,8\}$ and $\Delta=q+m\le16$, either $\Delta-1$ is
a prime power (a cell where the Moore bound is attained by $H(\Delta-1)$), or $\Delta\in\{7,11,13,15,16\}$, where
Theorem~\ref{thm:D} with the odd $q'=\Delta-2$ (or $q'=13$, $m=3$ at $\Delta=16$) gives much more (for instance 9592
against 4468 from $q=4$, $m=3$ at $\Delta=7$).

\subsection{Regular graphs of diameter 6}\label{ssec:thmR}
The structure of Theorem~\ref{thm:D} has equally many points and lines in $\cA_0$ (exact whiskers force this), but $\cA_1$
has $q^3+1$ lines and only $q+1$ points, and $\cA_2$ is a line. So it has $(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$ more lines than points. We
delete repeated lines and then add anti-flags.

\begin{lemma}[repeated lines]\label{lem:repeated}
In $H(q)^{(m)}_\cA$, for $n\in S\setminus\{v\}$ and $i\in[m]$, the $m$ lines $n^{i1},\dots,n^{im}$ all have the point set
$\{p^i:p\in n\}$; for $i,j\in[m]$ the lines $v^{ij1},\dots,v^{ijm}$ all have the point set $\{p^{ij}:p\in v\}$
(Table~\ref{tab:versions}). So there are $mq^3+m^2$ classes of $m$ repeated lines.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[completion]\label{lem:completion}
Let a finite incidence structure have maximum degree at most $\Delta$, every element of degree at least $\Delta-r$, and
$\sum_{\text{points}}(\Delta-\deg)=\sum_{\text{lines}}(\Delta-\deg)=:T$. If $T>2r\Delta$, there is a set $J$ of anti-flags
such that adding $J$ makes it $\Delta$-regular.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose a multiset $J$ of point--line pairs in which each element $x$ occurs $\Delta-\deg x$ times (possible as the sums
agree). Call an entry bad if it is a flag or a repeat of another entry, and take $J$ with the fewest bad entries. If
$(p,L)$ is bad: $L$ is joined (in $\I\cup J$, with multiplicity) to $\Delta$ points, each in at most $r$ entries of $J$;
likewise $p$. So at most $2r\Delta<T$ entries $(p',L')$ have $p'$ joined to $L$ or $L'$ joined to $p$, and some entry
$(p',L')$ has neither (it is not $(p,L)$ itself). Replacing $(p,L),(p',L')$ by $(p,L'),(p',L)$ removes a bad entry and
creates none. So the minimum is 0.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:R}
For every odd prime power $q$ and $m\ge2$ there is a $(q+m)$-regular incidence structure of diameter at most 6 with
\[
2(q+1)\bigl(q^4+q^2+1+(m-1)(q^3+m+1)\bigr)
\]
elements: $2(q+1)(q^4+q^3+q^2+4)$ for $m=2$ and $2(q+1)(q^4+2q^3+q^2+9)$ for $m=3$. For the five table instances below the
order exceeds $\Mb(q+m,5)$, so the diameter is exactly 6.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start from Theorem~\ref{thm:D}. Fix a set $R$ of $q$ lines of $S\setminus\{v\}$, and delete the lines $v^{ijk}$ with
$k\ge2$ ($(m-1)m^2$ lines) and $n^{ij}$ with $j\ge2$ for $n\notin R$ ($(m-1)m(q^3-q)$ lines): in total
$(m-1)m(q^3-q+m)$, the excess, so points and lines are now equally many. Each class of Lemma~\ref{lem:repeated} keeps at
least one line, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:flags}(b) no distance between remaining elements increases. After the deletion the
degrees are: $p^{ij}$ ($p\in v$): $q+1$; $p^i$ on a line $n\notin R$ of $S$: $q+1$; $p^i$ on $n\in R$: $q+m$; $v^{ij1}$,
$n^{i1}$ ($n\notin R$), $n^{ij}$ ($n\in R$) and $h^i$ ($h\in W$): $q+1$; all others as in Table~\ref{tab:versions} ($q+m$).
So every deficit is at most $r=m-1$. Points and lines are equally many, so both deficit sums equal
$\Delta|\cP|-|E|=\Delta|\cL|-|E|$ ($\Delta=q+m$, $E$ the set of flags), as Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} requires; and their
common value $T$ is at least the deficit of the $m|W|$ whisker versions: $T\ge(m-1)mq^2(q^2-1)>2(m-1)(q+m)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} completes the structure; the added flags join a point to a line, so the graph stays bipartite,
and they do not increase distances. The number of points is $(q+1)(q^4+q^2+1)+(m-1)\bigl((q+1)(q^3+1)+m(q+1)\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Neighbourhoods.} Every neighbourhood is that of Table~\ref{tab:versions}, minus the deleted lines listed in the
proof, plus the completing anti-flags: exactly $m-1$ of them at each element of degree $q+1$, none elsewhere. The
completing anti-flags are given by Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}, not by a formula; neither the maximum degree nor the
diameter depends on the choice.
\todo{Theorem~\ref{thm:R}: the completing anti-flags are only shown to exist (as in the Lean proof). If you want every
neighbourhood written out, we need an explicit completion (for instance a formula on the deficient elements); this is open
work.}

($q=13$, $m=3$). Each beats the 2013 value of its cell (8992, 142464, 374452, 842048, 884062), and Delorme's graphs behind

\subsection{Sets of points and lines}

\begin{definition}\label{def:hexsets}
A set of points of $H(q)$ is \emph{non-collinear} if no two of its points are collinear (a partial distance-2 ovoid),
and a \emph{partial ovoid} if its points are pairwise opposite; an \emph{ovoid} is a partial ovoid with $q^3+1$ points.
Dually, a set of lines is \emph{non-concurrent}, a \emph{partial spread} (pairwise opposite lines) or a \emph{spread}
($q^3+1$ pairwise opposite lines).
\end{definition}

We follow \cite{VM98}: an ovoid of a generalized hexagon is a distance-3 ovoid. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(c), partial ovoids of $H(q)$ are exactly the sets of pairwise non-perpendicular points of
$Q(6,q)$, i.e.\ partial ovoids of the polar space $Q(6,q)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hexsets}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $\Sigma$ is a 3-space of $\PG(6,q)$ with $\Sigma\cap Q(6,q)$ an elliptic quadric $Q^-(3,q)$, its $q^2+1$ points
  form a partial ovoid of $H(q)$, for every $q$.
\item (Hermitian spread \cite{T80}.) If $\Pi$ is a hyperplane with $\Pi\cap Q(6,q)$ an elliptic quadric $Q^-(5,q)$, the
  lines of $H(q)$ contained in $\Pi$ form a spread, for every $q$.
\item If $S$ is a spread, every line not in $S$ meets exactly one line of $S$.
\item If $q=3^h$ and $\varphi$ is a duality, $\varphi(S)$ is an ovoid for every spread $S$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) If $x\ne y$ are points of $Q^-(3,q)$ with $b(x,y)=0$, then $Q(\lambda x+\mu y)=\lambda\mu b(x,y)=0$, so the line $xy$
lies in $Q^-(3,q)$, which contains no line. So $b(x,y)\ne0$ and $x,y$ are opposite by Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(c). (b) The
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:Piz}(b) applies to every hyperplane with an elliptic section (it uses only that $\Pi\cap Q(6,q)$
contains no plane); examples of such hyperplanes for every $q$ are in Lemmas~\ref{lem:Piz} and~\ref{lem:Piz-even}.
(c) A line meeting two lines of $S$ would put them at distance at most 4. So the lines of $S$ together with the $q(q+1)$
lines meeting each of them are $(q^3+1)(q^2+q+1)=N$ distinct lines, that is, all lines. (d) $\varphi$ preserves
distances.
\end{proof}

Ovoids of $H(q)$ (equivalently of $Q(6,q)$) exist for $q=3^h$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:hexsets}(d), \cite{Th81}); they do not exist
for $q$ even \cite{Th81} or for $q$ prime, $q\ge5$ \cite{BGS06}.

\begin{definition}[segment sets]\label{def:segment}
A set $F$ of points is a \emph{segment set} if for every $p\in F$ the points of $F\setminus\{p\}$ collinear with $p$ all
lie on one line through $p$. Equivalently, there are no $p,p',p''\in F$ with $d(p,p')=d(p',p'')=2$ and $d(p,p'')=4$ (a
path of two collinearities that turns at $p'$). Dually, a set $G$ of lines is a \emph{dual segment set} if for every
$\ell\in G$ the lines of $G\setminus\{\ell\}$ meeting $\ell$ all pass through one point of $\ell$.
\end{definition}

So a segment set is a disjoint union of ``segments'' (at least two points on a line) and isolated points, such that no two
points from different pieces are collinear. Examples: every non-collinear set; every set in which each point is collinear
with at most one other point of the set; every subset of the point set of a partial spread; dually, the set of all lines
through the points of a partial ovoid.

\begin{proposition}[size of segment sets]\label{prop:segmentsize}
A segment set $F$ has $|F|\le(q+1)(q^3+1)$, with equality if and only if $F$ is the point set of a spread. Dually, a dual
segment set has at most $(q+1)(q^3+1)$ lines, with equality if and only if it is the set of lines through the points of an
ovoid.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $F_1$ be the points of $F$ collinear with no other point of $F$, and $C_1,\dots,C_k$ the segments (maximal sets of at
least two points of $F$ on a line $\ell_i$). A line meeting $F$ in exactly one point is a \emph{private} line of that point.
A point of $F_1$ has $q+1$ private lines and a point of a segment $C_i$ has $q$ (all its lines except $\ell_i$, by the
segment property). Private lines of different points are different, and different from the $\ell_i$. So
$(q+1)|F_1|+q\sum_i|C_i|+k\le N$. Since $k\ge\sum_i|C_i|/(q+1)$, the left side is at least $(q+\frac1{q+1})|F|$, which
gives $|F|\le N(q+1)/(q^2+q+1)=(q+1)(q^3+1)$. Equality forces $F_1=\emptyset$, $|C_i|=q+1$ and $k=q^3+1$. The lines
$\ell_i$ are then pairwise opposite: if $\ell_i$ and $\ell_j$ met in a point $z$, then $z\in F$ would have other points of
$F$ on two lines; if $d(\ell_i,\ell_j)=4$, a point of $\ell_i$ would be collinear with a point of $\ell_j$ and would also
have other points of $F$ on $\ell_i$. So they form a spread. Conversely the point set of a spread is a segment set of this
size. The dual statement is the same proof in the dual hexagon.
\end{proof}

For comparison, a non-collinear set has at most $N/(q+1)=q^4+q^2+1$ points (equality means a distance-2 ovoid; these are
known only for $q=2,3,4$ \cite{DWVM08}, \cite{BI16}).

\subsection{Level graphs}\label{ssec:d7levels}
Fix $r\ge1$; there are $2r+1$ \emph{levels}, indexed by $j\in\Z_{2r+1}$. Each level gets a \emph{type}
$\tau_j\in\{H,D\}$ (the hexagon or its dual). For a type $\tau$ let
\[
\mathsf A(H)=\cP,\quad\mathsf B(H)=\cL,\qquad\mathsf A(D)=\cL,\quad\mathsf B(D)=\cP .
\]
A \emph{transition} is a pair $t=(j,k)$ with $j\in\Z_{2r+1}$ and $1\le k\le r$; its source level is $j$ and its target
level $j+k$. It is \emph{friendly} if $\tau_j\ne\tau_{j+k}$; then $\mathsf A(\tau_j)=\mathsf B(\tau_{j+k})$.

\begin{definition}[level graph]\label{def:levelgraph}
Given types $\tau$ and, for every transition $t=(j,k)$, a bijection $M_t:\mathsf A(\tau_j)\to\mathsf B(\tau_{j+k})$, the
graph $X=X(\tau,M)$ has vertex set $(\cP\cup\cL)\times\Z_{2r+1}$ and edges
\begin{itemize}
\item $(x,j)\sim(y,j)$ whenever $x$ and $y$ are incident (\emph{level edges});
\item $(a,j)\sim(M_{(j,k)}(a),j+k)$ for every $j$, every $1\le k\le r$ and every $a\in\mathsf A(\tau_j)$ (\emph{matching
  edges}).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Colour $(a,j)$, $a\in\mathsf A(\tau_j)$, with colour $\mathsf A$ and $(b,j)$, $b\in\mathsf B(\tau_j)$, with colour
$\mathsf B$; every edge joins different colours, so $X$ is bipartite. The neighbourhoods are
\begin{align*}
N_X(a,j)&=\Gamma_1(a)\times\{j\}\ \cup\ \{(M_{(j,k)}(a),j+k):1\le k\le r\}\qquad(a\in\mathsf A(\tau_j)),\\
N_X(b,j)&=\Gamma_1(b)\times\{j\}\ \cup\ \{(M^{-1}_{(j-k,k)}(b),j-k):1\le k\le r\}\qquad(b\in\mathsf B(\tau_j)),
\end{align*}
so $X$ is $(q+1+r)$-regular of order $(2r+1)\cdot2N$. When all levels have type $H$ and all $M_t$ equal one bijection
$\cP\to\cL$, $X$ has the order of the 2013 table value.

\begin{theorem}[level graphs]\label{thm:levelgraph}
For all types and all bijections $M_t$, $X(\tau,M)$ has diameter at most 7. More precisely, two vertices of one level are
joined by a path of length at most 6 inside that level, and for every transition $t=(j,k)$ every vertex of level $j$ is
joined to every vertex of level $j+k$ by a path of length at most 7 that uses only level edges of levels $j$ and $j+k$ and
matching edges of $t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Inside a level this is the diameter of $I(q)$. Any two distinct levels differ by $\pm k$ for some $1\le k\le r$, so it is
enough to join $(u,j)$ to $(v,j')$, $j'=j+k$, with $M=M_{(j,k)}$. (1) $u\in\mathsf A(\tau_j)$: $(u,j)\to(M(u),j')\leadsto
(v,j')$, of length at most $1+6$. (2) $u\in\mathsf B(\tau_j)$, $v\in\mathsf B(\tau_{j'})$: with $a=M^{-1}(v)$,
$(u,j)\leadsto(a,j)\to(v,j')$, of length at most $5+1$ (elements of different types are at distance at most 5). (3)
$u\in\mathsf B(\tau_j)$, $v\in\mathsf A(\tau_{j'})$: pick $b\in\Gamma_1(v)$ and $a=M^{-1}(b)$; then
$(u,j)\leadsto(a,j)\to(b,j')\to(v,j')$, of length at most $5+1+1$.
\end{proof}

This is the routine argument for such graphs, and we do not claim it (Section~\ref{ssec:d7prior}).

\subsection{Surgery and hubs}

\begin{definition}[surgery and hubs]\label{def:surgery}
Start from a level graph $X(\tau,M)$ and choose for every transition $t=(j,k)$ a set $R_t\subseteq\mathsf A(\tau_j)$ of
\emph{freed elements} (possibly empty).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Deletion.} Delete the matching edges $(a,j)\sim(M_t(a),j+k)$, $a\in R_t$. Call the result $X_R$. Each deleted
  edge leaves a \emph{free $\mathsf A$-slot} $(a,t)$ at the vertex $(a,j)$ and a \emph{free $\mathsf B$-slot} $(M_t(a),t)$
  at the vertex $(M_t(a),j+k)$. Level $j$ has
  \[
  \alpha_j=\sum_{k=1}^r|R_{(j,k)}|\ \text{free }\mathsf A\text{-slots},\qquad \beta_j=\sum_{k=1}^r|R_{(j-k,k)}|\ \text{free
  }\mathsf B\text{-slots}.
  \]
\item \emph{Hubs.} Add $K_{\mathsf B}\le\min_j\alpha_j$ new vertices of colour $\mathsf B$ (\emph{$\mathsf B$-hubs}) and
  $K_{\mathsf A}\le\min_j\beta_j$ new vertices of colour $\mathsf A$ (\emph{$\mathsf A$-hubs}). Each $\mathsf B$-hub $h$ takes
  exactly one free $\mathsf A$-slot in every level $j$ and is joined to its vertex, written $(\omega_j(h),j)$; each
  $\mathsf A$-hub $g$ likewise takes one free $\mathsf B$-slot in every level, at the vertex $(\omega'_j(g),j)$. Every slot is
  taken at most once. (The assignment is otherwise arbitrary; a vertex with several free slots may receive several hubs.)
\item \emph{Hub graph.} Add a bipartite graph $Y$ between the $\mathsf B$-hubs and the $\mathsf A$-hubs.
\end{enumerate}
Call the result $X^+_R$.
\end{definition}

\emph{Exact neighbourhoods.} For a level vertex, the neighbourhood is that of Definition~\ref{def:levelgraph} minus the
deleted matching edges plus one hub for each taken free slot. For hubs,
\[
N_{X^+_R}(h)=\{(\omega_j(h),j):j\in\Z_{2r+1}\}\cup N_Y(h),\qquad N_{X^+_R}(g)=\{(\omega'_j(g),j):j\in\Z_{2r+1}\}\cup N_Y(g).
\]
Level vertices have degree at most $q+1+r$ (with equality when all their free slots are used), and hubs have degree
$2r+1+\deg_Y$. So $X^+_R$ is bipartite of maximum degree at most $q+1+r$ as soon as $Y$ has maximum degree at most $q-r$,
and $|X^+_R|=(2r+1)\cdot2N+K_{\mathsf A}+K_{\mathsf B}$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:repaired}
A transition $t=(j,k)$ is \emph{repaired} (by $R_t$) if in $X_R$ every vertex of level $j$ is joined to every vertex of
level $j+k$ by a path of length at most 7 that uses only level edges of levels $j$, $j+k$ and the remaining matching edges
of $t$.
\end{definition}

Whether $t$ is repaired depends only on $M_t$ and $R_t$.

\begin{lemma}[hubs]\label{lem:hubs}
In $X^+_R$ every hub is within distance 7 of every level vertex, and every $\mathsf B$-hub is within distance 7 of every
$\mathsf A$-hub.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A $\mathsf B$-hub $h$ and a level vertex $(v,j)$: $h\to(\omega_j(h),j)\leadsto(v,j)$ inside level $j$, of length at most
$1+6$ (level edges are never deleted). A $\mathsf B$-hub $h$ and an $\mathsf A$-hub $g$: in level 0,
$\omega_0(h)\in\mathsf A(\tau_0)$ and $\omega'_0(g)\in\mathsf B(\tau_0)$ have different types, so
$h\to(\omega_0(h),0)\leadsto(\omega'_0(g),0)\to g$ has length at most $1+5+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[assembly]\label{thm:assembly}
Suppose that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(T)] every transition $t$ is repaired by $R_t$, and
\item[(HG)] any two $\mathsf B$-hubs, and any two $\mathsf A$-hubs, are within distance 6 in $X^+_R$.
\end{itemize}
Then $X^+_R$ has diameter at most 7. If moreover $Y$ has maximum degree at most $q-r$, then
\[
\nb(q+1+r,7)\ge(2r+1)\cdot2N(q)+K_{\mathsf A}+K_{\mathsf B}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Pairs of level vertices: same level by the diameter of $I(q)$, different levels by (T), since every pair of levels is the
pair of some transition. Pairs with a hub: Lemma~\ref{lem:hubs} and (HG).
\end{proof}

(HG) holds for instance when $Y$ alone has all same-colour distances at most 6; it can also use paths through the levels.

\subsection{Transitions that extend to an automorphism}
A transition $t=(j,k)$ is \emph{automorphic} if $M_t$ is the restriction to $\mathsf A(\tau_j)$ of an automorphism $\psi$
of $I(q)$ with $\psi(\mathsf A(\tau_j))=\mathsf B(\tau_{j+k})$. The two cases used here are: a friendly transition with
$M_t=\mathrm{id}$; and an $H\to H$ transition with $M_t=\varphi|_\cP$ for a duality $\varphi$ ($q=3^h$), or a $D\to D$
transition with $M_t=\varphi|_\cL$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:segment}
Let $F$ be a segment set of points. For all elements $u,v$ of $H(q)$ there is a point $p\notin F$ with
$d(u,p)+d(p,v)\le6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If some point $p$ on a geodesic from $u$ to $v$ is not in $F$, then $d(u,p)+d(p,v)=d(u,v)\le6$. Assume that all points on
all geodesics from $u$ to $v$ lie in $F$. A geodesic with three points $p,p',p''$ in a row ($p-m-p'-m'-p''$ with lines
$m\ne m'$) cannot have all three in $F$ (a turning path). A geodesic between two points at distance 4 or 6, a point and a
line at distance 5, or two lines at distance 6 contains three points in a row. So only the following cases remain; in each
we exhibit $p\notin F$ with $d(u,p)+d(p,v)\le6$. We use repeatedly: \emph{if $p_0\in F$ has a point of $F$ on the line $s$
through it, then no point $p\ne p_0$ collinear with $p_0$ off $s$ is in $F$}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $u=v$ a point of $F$. Let $s$ be a line through $u$ containing every point of $F$ collinear with $u$ (any line if there
  is none). Take a line $y\ne s$ through $u$ and $p\in y\setminus\{u\}$: $p\notin F$ and $d(u,p)+d(p,u)=4$.
\item $u,v$ collinear points of $F$ on the line $s$. Take $y\ne s$ through $u$ and $p\in y\setminus\{u\}$: $p\notin F$,
  $d(u,p)=2$, $d(p,v)\le4$.
\item $u\in F$ a point and $v$ a line through $u$. If $v$ has a point $p\ne u$ outside $F$: $2+1$. Otherwise all points of
  $v$ are in $F$; take $y\ne v$ through $u$ and $p\in y\setminus\{u\}$: $p\notin F$, $d(u,p)=2$, $d(p,v)\le3$.
\item $u$ a point and $v$ a line with $d(u,v)=3$, geodesic $u-s-p_2-v$ with $u,p_2\in F$. A point $p\in v\setminus\{p_2\}$
  has $d(u,p)=4$ and $d(p,v)=1$. If all of them were in $F$, $p_2$ would have points of $F$ on the two lines $s$ and $v$.
\item $u,v$ lines meeting in $p_1\in F$. Not both $u$ and $v$ contain a second point of $F$ (else $p_1$ turns). Say $u$ does
  not: take $p\in u\setminus\{p_1\}$; then $p\notin F$, $d(u,p)=1$, $d(p,v)=3$.
\item $u,v$ lines at distance 4, geodesic $u-p_1-y-p_3-v$ with $p_1,p_3\in F$. No other point $p$ of $u$ is in $F$ (else
  $p_1$ turns: $p$ on $u$, $p_3$ on $y$), and $d(u,p)=1$, $d(p,v)\le d(p,p_1)+d(p_1,v)=2+3$.
\item $u=v$ a line. If some point of $u$ is not in $F$: $1+1$. Otherwise take $p_1\in u$, a line $y\ne u$ through $p_1$ and
  $p\in y\setminus\{p_1\}$: $p\notin F$ and $d(u,p)=3$, so $3+3=6$.
\end{enumerate}
The cases with $u$ a line and $v$ a point are (c), (d) with $u$ and $v$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[automorphic transitions]\label{thm:autotrans}
Let $t=(j,k)$ be an automorphic transition. If $\mathsf A(\tau_j)=\cP$ and $R_t$ is a segment set of points, or
$\mathsf A(\tau_j)=\cL$ and $R_t$ is a dual segment set of lines, then $t$ is repaired.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\psi$ extend $M=M_t$, $j'=j+k$, and take $(u,j)$, $(v,j')$. For every $a\in\mathsf A(\tau_j)\setminus R_t$ the path
$(u,j)\leadsto(a,j)\to(\psi(a),j')\leadsto(v,j')$ has length $d(u,a)+1+d(\psi(a),v)=d(u,a)+1+d(a,\psi^{-1}(v))$, since $\psi$
is an isometry of $I(q)$. If $\mathsf A(\tau_j)=\cP$, Lemma~\ref{lem:segment} applied to $u$, $\psi^{-1}(v)$ and $F=R_t$
gives $a$ with $d(u,a)+d(a,\psi^{-1}(v))\le6$. If $\mathsf A(\tau_j)=\cL$, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:segment} in the dual
hexagon.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the converse]\label{rem:converse}
An automorphic transition is repaired if and only if its freed set is a segment set (a dual segment set for lines).
Suppose that $p,p',p''\in R_t$ turn at $p'$ ($p-m-p'-m'-p''$, $m\ne m'$) and take a line $u\ne m'$ through $p''$. Then
$d(p,u)=5$, with the unique geodesic $p-m-p'-m'-p''-u$. A path from $(u,j)$ to $(\psi(p),j')$ inside the two levels uses an
odd number $2h+1$ of matching edges of $t$, and (transporting the segments in level $j'$ back by $\psi^{-1}$) has length at
least $2h+1+d(u,p)=2h+6$. So a path of length at most 7 uses one matching edge, at a point $a$ with $d(u,a)+d(a,p)=5$, that
is $a\in\{p,p',p''\}$; all three edges are deleted, and the distance within the two levels is more than 7.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:autotrans}
The following transitions are repaired:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a friendly transition with $M_t=\mathrm{id}$ and $R_t$ a non-collinear set of points (if $\tau_j=H$) or a
  non-concurrent set of lines (if $\tau_j=D$);
\item the same with $R_t$ a set in which every element is collinear (concurrent) with at most one other element of $R_t$;
\item an $H\to H$ transition with $M_t$ a duality and $R_t$ any subset of the point set of a partial spread (for example all
  $(q+1)(q^3+1)$ points of the Hermitian spread, $q=3^h$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Local dualities}
For transitions that are not automorphic (an $H\to H$ or $D\to D$ transition when $q\ne3^h$, or one whose matching is not
the restriction of a duality) we ask $M_t$ to behave like a duality only near the freed elements.

\begin{theorem}[local dualities]\label{thm:localdual}
Let $t=(j,k)$ be any transition, $M=M_t$ and $R=R_t\subseteq\mathsf A(\tau_j)$. Suppose
\begin{itemize}
\item[(H1)] no two elements of $R$ are at distance 2;
\item[(H2)] no two elements of $M(R)$ are at distance 2;
\item[(H3)] $M(\Gamma_2(x))=\Gamma_2(M(x))$ for every $x\in R$ ($M$ is a \emph{local duality} at $x$ when it maps points to
  lines: it maps the points collinear with $x$ onto the lines concurrent with $M(x)$).
\end{itemize}
Then $t$ is repaired.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $j'=j+k$; ``$\mathsf A$'' and ``$\mathsf B$'' refer to $\mathsf A(\tau_j),\mathsf B(\tau_j)$ in level $j$ and to
$\mathsf A(\tau_{j'}),\mathsf B(\tau_{j'})$ in level $j'$. Take $(u,j)$ and $(v,j')$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $u\in\mathsf A\setminus R$: $(u,j)\to(M(u),j')\leadsto(v,j')$, length at most $1+6$.
\item $u\in\mathsf B$, $v\in\mathsf A$: the $q+1\ge2$ neighbours of $v$ are pairwise at distance 2, so by (H2) at most one is
  in $M(R)$; take $b\in\Gamma_1(v)\setminus M(R)$ and $a=M^{-1}(b)\notin R$: $(u,j)\leadsto(a,j)\to(b,j')\to(v,j')$, length
  at most $5+1+1$.
\item $u\in\mathsf B$, $v\in\mathsf B\setminus M(R)$: $(u,j)\leadsto(M^{-1}(v),j)\to(v,j')$, length at most $5+1$.
\item $u\in\mathsf B$, $v=M(x)$ with $x\in R$: by Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(b) there is $z\in\Gamma_2(x)$ with $d(z,u)\le3$;
  $z\notin R$ by (H1), and $M(z)\in\Gamma_2(v)$ by (H3): $(v,j')\leadsto(M(z),j')\to(z,j)\leadsto(u,j)$, length at most
  $2+1+3$.
\item $u=x\in R$, $v\in\mathsf A$: there is $w\in\Gamma_2(M(x))$ with $d(w,v)\le3$; by (H3) $w=M(z)$ with $z\in\Gamma_2(x)$,
  and $z\notin R$ by (H1): $(x,j)\leadsto(z,j)\to(w,j')\leadsto(v,j')$, length at most $2+1+3$.
\item $u=x\in R$, $v\in\mathsf B$: the same path, now with $d(w,v)\le4$: length at most $2+1+4$.
\end{enumerate}
These cases cover all pairs.
\end{proof}

For two points $x,y$ at distance 4 let $\mdp(x,y)$ be the unique point collinear with both, and likewise for lines.

\begin{proposition}[what (H1)--(H3) force]\label{prop:mirror-nec}
Under (H1)--(H3), $M$ restricted to $R$ preserves distances, and $M(\mdp(x,y))=\mdp(M(x),M(y))$ for all $x,y\in R$ at
distance 4.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x\ne y\in R$; then $d(x,y)\in\{4,6\}$ by (H1). If $d(x,y)=4$, $m=\mdp(x,y)\in\Gamma_2(x)\cap\Gamma_2(y)$, so by (H3)
$M(m)\in\Gamma_2(M(x))\cap\Gamma_2(M(y))$; hence $d(M(x),M(y))\le4$, equal to 4 by (H2), and $M(m)$ is the mid-line. If
$d(x,y)=6$, $\Gamma_2(x)\cap\Gamma_2(y)=\emptyset$, so $\Gamma_2(M(x))\cap\Gamma_2(M(y))=\emptyset$ by (H3), and
$d(M(x),M(y))=6$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[mirrored sets]\label{prop:mirror}
Let $R$ be a non-collinear set of points, $R^*$ a non-concurrent set of lines, and $f:R\to R^*$ a bijection such that
\begin{itemize}
\item[(S1)] every point is collinear with at most two points of $R$, and every line is concurrent with at most two lines of
  $R^*$;
\item[(S2)] for $x\ne y\in R$: $d(x,y)=4$ if and only if $d(f(x),f(y))=4$.
\end{itemize}
Then there is a bijection $M:\cP\to\cL$ with $M|_R=f$ satisfying (H1)--(H3) for $R$. One such $M$ is: $M(x)=f(x)$ for
$x\in R$; $M(\mdp(x,y))=\mdp(f(x),f(y))$ for $x,y\in R$ at distance 4; for each $x\in R$, any bijection from the remaining
points of $\Gamma_2(x)$ onto the remaining lines of $\Gamma_2(f(x))$; any bijection on the rest.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $U=\bigcup_{x\in R}\Gamma_2(x)$ and $U^*=\bigcup_{x\in R}\Gamma_2(f(x))$. By (H1) for $R$ and $R^*$, $R\cap U=\emptyset$ and
$R^*\cap U^*=\emptyset$. A point of $U$ lies in $\Gamma_2(x)$ for one or two $x\in R$ (S1); if two, $x$ and $y$ are at
distance 4 and the point is $\mdp(x,y)$. So the points of $U$ in two sets $\Gamma_2(\cdot)$ correspond to the pairs
$\{x,y\}\subseteq R$ at distance 4, and likewise for $U^*$; by (S2), $\{x,y\}\mapsto\{f(x),f(y)\}$ is a bijection between
these pairs. Hence $\mdp(x,y)\mapsto\mdp(f(x),f(y))$ is a bijection from the shared points of $U$ to the shared lines of
$U^*$, mapping the shared points of $\Gamma_2(x)$ onto the shared lines of $\Gamma_2(f(x))$. The remaining (private) points
of $\Gamma_2(x)$ and private lines of $\Gamma_2(f(x))$ are equally many (both $q(q+1)$ minus the number of $y\in R$ at distance
4 from $x$), and private sets of different $x$ are disjoint. So the pieces define a bijection $R\cup U\to R^*\cup U^*$ with
$M(\Gamma_2(x))=\Gamma_2(f(x))$ for every $x\in R$. As $|\cP\setminus(R\cup U)|=|\cL\setminus(R^*\cup U^*)|$, it extends to a
bijection $\cP\to\cL$. (H1) and (H2) are the hypotheses on $R$ and $R^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mirror}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $R$ is a partial ovoid and $R^*$ a partial spread with $|R|=|R^*|$, every bijection $f:R\to R^*$ satisfies (S1)
  and (S2) (there are no pairs at distance 4).
\item Let $R_0$ and $X$ be disjoint partial ovoids, $f_0:R_0\to S_0$ a bijection onto a partial spread $S_0$, and $x\mapsto
  L_x$ an injective map from $X$ onto a partial spread disjoint from $S_0$. Put $R=R_0\cup X$, $R^*=S_0\cup\{L_x:x\in X\}$,
  $f=f_0$ on $R_0$ and $f(x)=L_x$ on $X$, and assume that $R$ is non-collinear and $R^*$ non-concurrent. Then (S1) holds:
  three points of $R$ collinear with one point would be pairwise at distance 4, but two of them lie in $R_0$ or two in $X$,
  which are pairwise opposite; likewise for lines. And (S2) says: for $r\in R_0$ and $x\in X$, $d(r,x)=4$ if and only if
  $d(f_0(r),L_x)=4$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Typed level layouts}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:assembly} the order is $(2r+1)\cdot2N+K_{\mathsf A}+K_{\mathsf B}$ with

\subsection{Hub graphs}\label{ssec:hubgraphs}
Write $\mathrm{HG}(K_1,K_2;\delta)$ for a bipartite graph with colour classes of sizes $K_1,K_2$, maximum degree at most
$\delta$, and all same-colour distances at most 6 in the graph itself. If $Y$ is such a graph with $\delta\le q-r$, both
conditions on $Y$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:assembly} hold.

\begin{lemma}[plane with pendant trees]\label{lem:pendant}
Let $s$ be a prime power and $t,u\ge0$ integers with $s+1+t\le\delta$ and $u+1\le\delta$. Then $\mathrm{HG}(K,K;\delta)$
exists for every $K$ with $s^2+s+1\le K\le(s^2+s+1)(1+t+tu)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Start from the incidence graph of $\PG(2,s)$ (the \emph{core}: diameter 3, same-colour distance 2). Attach to some core
vertices up to $t$ pendant vertices each (depth 1, of the opposite colour), and to some depth-1 vertices up to $u$ pendant
vertices each (depth 2, of the colour of the core vertex). Degrees are at most $s+1+t$, $u+1$ and 1. For two vertices $x,y$
of the same colour with core ancestors $c(x),c(y)$, $d(x,y)\le\mathrm{depth}(x)+d(c(x),c(y))+\mathrm{depth}(y)$. The core
ancestors have the same colour when the depths have the same parity (core distance at most 2) and different colours
otherwise (core distance at most 3); the possible totals are $0+2+0$, $0+3+1$, $0+2+2$, $1+2+1$, $1+3+2$, $2+2+2$, all at
most 6. To get $K$ vertices of each colour, give $c$ depth-1 children to the points and $c$ to the lines, and $g$ depth-2
children below each of these two families, with $c=\lceil(K-s^2-s-1)/(1+u)\rceil\le(s^2+s+1)t$ and $g=K-s^2-s-1-c\le uc$.
\end{proof}

With $t=\delta-s-1$ and $u=\delta-1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:pendant} gives hub graphs with up to
\[
\kappa(\delta):=\max_s\,(s^2+s+1)\bigl(1+(\delta-s-1)\delta\bigr)\approx\tfrac4{27}\delta^4
\]
vertices of each colour ($s$ over prime powers at most $\delta-1$); $\kappa(3)=7$, $\kappa(4)=35$, $\kappa(7)=315$,
$\kappa(8)=527$, $\kappa(9)=868$, $\kappa(10)=1271$, $\kappa(11)=1938$. For $\delta\ge3$ the intervals for $s=2$ and for the
optimal $s$ overlap, so $\mathrm{HG}(K,K;\delta)$ exists for every $7\le K\le\kappa(\delta)$.

\subsection{Bounds for every $q$ from classical sets}\label{ssec:d7general}

\begin{corollary}[three levels, every $q\ge4$]\label{cor:d7-91}
Let $R$ be the $q^2+1$ points of an elliptic quadric section $Q^-(3,q)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:hexsets}(a)), $R^*$ any $q^2+1$ lines
of a Hermitian spread, and $\sigma$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:mirror} (with Corollary~\ref{cor:mirror}(a)). All three
levels of type $H$, $M_t=\sigma$ and $R_t=R$ for all three transitions, $K=\min(q^2+1,\kappa(q-1))$ hubs of each colour and
the hub graph of Lemma~\ref{lem:pendant} give
\[
\nb(q+2,7)\ge6N(q)+2\min\bigl(q^2+1,\ \kappa(q-1)\bigr),
\]
which is $6N(q)+2(q^2+1)$ for every $q\ge5$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every transition is repaired by Theorem~\ref{thm:localdual}; every level has $|R|$ free slots of each colour, and
Definition~\ref{def:surgery} allows $K\le|R|$ hubs (the unused slots stay free). Theorem~\ref{thm:assembly} with
$Y=\mathrm{HG}(K,K;q-1)$.
\end{proof}

At $q=4$ ($\kappa(3)=7<17$) the bound is $6N(4)+14=8204$, below the table's $(6,7)=8310$; the corollary improves the table
only for $q\ge5$.

\subsection{Prior work}\label{ssec:d7prior}
The level graph of Definition~\ref{def:levelgraph} is a bipartite analogue of the star product of Bermond, Delorme and
Farhi \cite{BDF82,BDF84} (see \cite[\S3.3.2]{MS13}) and of the compound graphs of G\'omez and Fiol \cite{GF85}: copies of a
graph indexed by the vertices of a tournament, with a matching for each arc. The cells $(7,7)$ and $(9,7)$--$(16,7)$ of the
2013 table have the form $(2r+1)\cdot2N(q)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:levelgraph} and are credited to \cite{DGQ}, which we could
not find in print. The values $(6,7)=8310$ and $(8,7)=40\,586$ equal the order $2q(q^4+q^3+3q^2+q+1)$ ($q=5,7$) of Delorme's
construction \cite[\S9]{D85a}, in which vertices are inserted on $2q^3$ edges of the incidence graph of a generalized
hexagon and a vertex set is then duplicated. Delorme also builds cyclic multi-level graphs from a duality of a generalized
quadrangle or hexagon \cite[\S3]{D85a}, on the vertex set $\Z/m\Z\times Q^k$ with $Q$ the point set; those graphs are not
copies of $I(q)$ joined by matchings. Sets of $q^3+1$ pairwise opposite elements of $H(q)$ are used in \cite[\S8(b)]{D85a}
to raise the degree at diameter 6 (Remark~\ref{rem:delorme6}).

The closest prior work is a 2026 preprint of R.~Rajiv \cite{Ra26} (unrefereed). It builds compounds of copies of the
incidence graph of a generalized $n$-gon along a tournament, with point-to-line and type-preserving matchings; its Lemma~5
deletes the type-preserving matching edges at a pairwise non-collinear (or non-concurrent) set and keeps the diameter;
its Theorem~3 attaches new vertices, each joined to one point and one line of every copy, connected by a further graph. In
our language these are friendly transitions with $M_t=\mathrm{id}$, Corollary~\ref{cor:autotrans}(1), and hubs as in
Definition~\ref{def:surgery}. The setting differs (general graphs of diameter $n=4,6$, not bipartite graphs of diameter 7;
hubs with one point slot and one line slot per copy, not hubs of one colour), but the ideas are the same. F.~Comellas'
table of general graphs \cite{Co26} records in October 2026 graphs of diameter 6 obtained from $H(q)$ by deleting or
subdividing incidence edges and attaching new vertices. Replacing or adding vertices in hexagon-based graphs of diameter 6
is older \cite{CG94,GPB99,PGMP09}. In the sources we searched we found nothing on: the bipartite diameter-7 version with
hubs of one colour (Definition~\ref{def:surgery}, Theorem~\ref{thm:assembly}); typed levels as the 2-colouring; local
dualities at non-automorphic transitions (Theorem~\ref{thm:localdual}, Propositions~\ref{prop:mirror-nec},
\ref{prop:mirror}); segment sets, Proposition~\ref{prop:segmentsize}, Theorem~\ref{thm:autotrans} beyond non-collinear sets
and its converse (Remark~\ref{rem:converse}). Proposition~\ref{prop:segmentsize} and Remark~\ref{rem:converse} are
elementary and may well be known.

\subsection{The construction}
\cite[\S2.9.3, pp.~43--45]{Go86} takes 5 copies of a bipartite graph $B_1$ of even diameter $D_1$ and 10 sets of $N_1/2$
vertices each (copies of $K_1$); every 10 such vertices, one from each set, are joined to the copies as in Fig.~2.21a of the
thesis, so that their degree is 2, and the degree is then raised to 4 by joining them to 5 more copies as in Fig.~2.21b. In
general, if $\Delta_1+2=2\alpha+\beta$, one takes $5\alpha$ copies of $B_1$, using either of the two structures, so that the
copies of $K_1$ get degree $2\alpha$; the result $\Omega B_1$ has $N=5(\alpha+1)N_1$, $\Delta=\Delta_1+2$ and, as stated,
$D\le D_1+2$. For $B_1=H_q$: $D_1=6$, $\Delta_1=q+1$, $N_1=2N$.

\emph{Labels.} In \cite[\S2.7.1]{Go86} both colour classes of $B_1$ are labelled by $\Z/n_1\Z$, $n_1=N_1/2$, and every
adjacency rule of \S\S2.7--2.9 is written in these labels, the same in every copy; \S2.8.9, which \S2.9.3 cites for its
copies of $K_1$, joins a copy of $K_1$ with label $t$ only to vertices labelled $t$, in either colour class. For $B_1=H_q$ we
write the labelling as two bijections $P_0:\Z/N\Z\to\cP$ and $L_0:\Z/N\Z\to\cL$; the thesis does not fix them, so
$\sigma=L_0P_0^{-1}$ (point $t\mapsto$ line $t$) is an arbitrary bijection $\cP\to\cL$.

\emph{The figures.} Both figures place five copies at the corners of a pentagon, inside a circle and a pentagram. Each
copy has an \emph{outer} dot on the circle and an \emph{inner} dot at the corner of the pentagram; each copy of $K_1$ in the
block is a dot on an arc or a pentagram edge, joined to the two dots at its ends. In Fig.~2.21a, the $K_1$-vertex $k$ lies on
the pentagram edge between copies $k-1$ and $k+1$ and $\bar k$ on the arc between copies $k+2$ and $k+3$; in Fig.~2.21b, $k$
lies on the arc between copies $k+3$ and $k+4$ and $\bar k$ on the pentagram edge between copies $k-1$ and $k+1$ (indices
mod 5). The figures do not say which colour class the inner dot is. \emph{Reading 1}: outer dot = points and inner dot =
lines in every copy. \emph{Reading 2}: unbarred $K_1$-vertices always on points and barred ones always on lines. The figures
also do not say which blocks are of type (a) and which of type (b), so we allow any pattern; and we allow the inner class to
be chosen freely in every copy, which contains both readings and their duals.

\begin{definition}[$\Om_q$]\label{def:omega}
Data: a prime power $q$ and $\alpha=\lfloor(q+3)/2\rfloor$; a block pattern $(\tau_0,\dots,\tau_{\alpha-1})\in\{a,b\}^\alpha$;
an inner class $\iota(c)\in\{\cP,\cL\}$ for every copy $c$; and a \emph{labelling}, that is, bijections $P_c:\Z/N\Z\to\cP$
and $L_c:\Z/N\Z\to\cL$ for every copy $c$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Copies.} $c=5B+i$ with block $B\in\{0,\dots,\alpha-1\}$ and position $i\in\Z_5$; there are $5\alpha$ copies.
  Copy vertices $(c,x)$, $x\in\cP\cup\cL$, with $(c,x)\sim(c,y)$ when $x$ and $y$ are incident.
\item \emph{Hubs} (the copies of $K_1$). $(t,k)$ and $(t,\bar k)$ for $t\in\Z/N\Z$ (the \emph{row}) and $k\in\Z_5$; the
  hub \emph{classes} are $k=\{(t,k)\}$ (unbarred) and $\bar k=\{(t,\bar k)\}$ (barred).
\item \emph{Copies met by a class} (positions mod 5):
  $C(k)=\{5B+k-1,5B+k+1:\tau_B=a\}\cup\{5B+k+3,5B+k+4:\tau_B=b\}$ and
  $C(\bar k)=\{5B+k+2,5B+k+3:\tau_B=a\}\cup\{5B+k-1,5B+k+1:\tau_B=b\}$.
\item \emph{Where a class sits.} In a copy of a block of type $a$, unbarred classes sit on the inner class $\iota(c)$ and
  barred classes on the outer class; in a block of type $b$, unbarred classes on the outer and barred classes on the inner
  class.
\item \emph{Hub edges.} $(t,Y)\sim(c,P_c(t))$ if $Y$ sits on points in $c$, and $(t,Y)\sim(c,L_c(t))$ if $Y$ sits on lines in
  $c$, for every $c\in C(Y)$.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{thesis labelling} is $P_c=P_0$, $L_c=L_0$ for all $c$. Reading 1 is $\iota(c)=\cL$ for all $c$; reading 2 is
$\iota(c)=\cP$ in $a$-blocks and $\cL$ in $b$-blocks. The graph $\Om_q$ of the thesis is the thesis labelling with any
pattern and either reading.
\end{definition}

\emph{Exact neighbourhoods.} Write $m_c(t)$ for the inner mark of row $t$ in copy $c$ ($P_c(t)$ if $\iota(c)=\cP$, $L_c(t)$
if $\iota(c)=\cL$) and $\bar m_c(t)$ for the outer mark. For $c=5B+i$, the copy vertex $(c,x)$ is adjacent to $(c,y)$ for the
$q+1$ elements $y$ incident with $x$, and to two hubs: $(t,i-1),(t,i+1)$ if $\tau_B=a$ and $x=m_c(t)$;
$(t,\overline{i+2}),(t,\overline{i+3})$ if $\tau_B=a$ and $x=\bar m_c(t)$; $(t,\overline{i-1}),(t,\overline{i+1})$ if
$\tau_B=b$ and $x=m_c(t)$; $(t,i+1),(t,i+2)$ if $\tau_B=b$ and $x=\bar m_c(t)$. The hub $(t,k)$ is adjacent to
$(c,m_c(t))$ for $c=5B+k\pm1$ in blocks with $\tau_B=a$, and to $(c,\bar m_c(t))$ for $c=5B+k+3,5B+k+4$ in blocks with
$\tau_B=b$; the hub $(t,\bar k)$ is adjacent to $(c,\bar m_c(t))$ for $c=5B+k+2,5B+k+3$ in blocks with $\tau_B=a$, and to
$(c,m_c(t))$ for $c=5B+k\pm1$ in blocks with $\tau_B=b$.

\begin{lemma}[parameters]\label{lem:omegaparams}
For every pattern, inner classes and labelling, $\Om_q$ is a simple bipartite graph on $10(\alpha+1)N=5(\alpha+1)N_1$
vertices; copy vertices have degree $q+3$ and hubs degree $2\alpha$, so $\Delta=q+3$. Every hub meets $2\alpha$ of the
$5\alpha$ copies and misses $3\alpha$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A copy at position $i$ of an $a$-block is met by the unbarred classes $i\pm1$ and the barred classes $i+2,i+3$; of a
$b$-block, by the unbarred classes $i+1,i+2$ and the barred classes $i\pm1$. So each copy is met by exactly two classes of
each bar, the two of one bar sit on the same type, and each class has one hub per row. Every element of a copy is the mark
of exactly one row of its type, so it gets exactly two hubs. A class meets two distinct copies in each block. For the
colouring, give unbarred hubs colour 0, in every copy the type on which the unbarred classes sit colour 1 and the other type
colour 0, and barred hubs colour 1.
\end{proof}

\subsection{One label: a projection argument}

\begin{theorem}[projection]\label{thm:proj}
Let $B$ be a graph and $G$ a graph whose vertices are of two kinds: \emph{copy vertices} $v$, each with a copy index $c(v)$
and a label $f(v)\in V(B)$, and \emph{new vertices} $x$, each with a label $f(x)\in V(B)$. Assume
\begin{itemize}
\item[(h1)] if $u\sim w$ are copy vertices, then $c(u)=c(w)$ and $f(u)\sim f(w)$ in $B$;
\item[(h2)] every neighbour $w$ of a new vertex $x$ is a copy vertex with $f(w)=f(x)$;
\item[(h3)] no two new vertices are adjacent.
\end{itemize}
If $x$ is a new vertex with no neighbour in copy $j$, and $v$ is a copy vertex with $c(v)=j$, then
$d_G(x,v)\ge d_B(f(x),f(v))+3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $(x=w_0,w_1,\dots,w_l=v)$ be a walk. By (h2), $w_1$ is a copy vertex with $f(w_1)=f(x)$. Let $m$ be the number of new
vertices among $w_1,\dots,w_{l-1}$. A new vertex $w_i$ ($0<i<l$) has copy vertices $w_{i-1},w_{i+1}$ on both sides, with
$f(w_{i-1})=f(w_i)=f(w_{i+1})$ by (h2); every other edge $w_iw_{i+1}$ ($i\ge1$) joins two copy vertices and maps to an edge of
$B$ by (h1). Deleting the $m$ new vertices and one of the two equal labels around each, the labels of $w_1,\dots,w_l$ form a
walk in $B$ from $f(x)$ to $f(v)$ of length $l-1-2m$. So $l\ge d_B(f(x),f(v))+1+2m$. If $m=0$, then $w_1,\dots,w_l$ are copy
vertices joined by copy edges, so by (h1) $c(w_1)=c(v)=j$, and $w_1$ is a neighbour of $x$ in copy $j$, a contradiction. So
$m\ge1$ and $l\ge d_B(f(x),f(v))+3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:proj}
Take the thesis labelling and inner classes for which every hub class sits on one type in all copies it meets (reading 2;
its dual; reading 1 when all blocks have one type). Then for every $q$, every pattern and every pairing $\sigma$: every hub
$x$ and every copy $j$ that $x$ misses, the $q^5$ vertices $(j,y)$ with $y$ opposite the mark of $x$ are at distance at
least 9 from $x$. So $\diam\Om_q\ge9$. More generally, for any bipartite $B_1$ with a pair of vertices at distance $D_1$,
$\Omega B_1$ in reading 2 has diameter at least $D_1+3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Label copy vertices by $f(c,y)=y$, $c(c,y)=c$, and a hub by its mark ($P_0(t)$ or $L_0(t)$, the same in all copies by
assumption). Copy edges satisfy (h1), hub edges (h2), and there are no hub--hub edges. Every hub misses $3\alpha\ge1$ copies
(Lemma~\ref{lem:omegaparams}), and every vertex of $I(q)$ has $q^5$ opposite vertices (Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(a)). Apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:proj}. For a general $B_1$, in reading 2 the unbarred classes sit on all labels of class 0 and the barred
classes on all labels of class 1, so whichever class contains the pair, some hub carries one of its labels.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Two labels}

\begin{theorem}[two labels]\label{thm:twolabels}
Let $q\ge7$. Take $\Om_q$ with the thesis labelling, any block pattern and any inner classes (so both readings and every
pairing $\sigma$). Let $x$ be a hub and $j$ a copy that $x$ misses. Then at least
\[
q^5-(A+B+1)=q^5-5q^4-8q^3-12q^2-16q-10>0
\]
vertices of copy $j$ are at distance at least 9 from $x$, where, with $s_0=1$ and $s_i=(q+1)q^{i-1}$ for $1\le i\le5$,
\[
A=\sum_{e_1+e_2\in\{0,2,4\}}s_{e_1}s_{e_2}=5q^4+8q^3+6q^2+4q+2,\qquad B=\sum_{e_1+e_2+e_3\le2}s_{e_1}s_{e_2}s_{e_3}=6q^2+12q+7
\]
(sums over $e_i\ge0$). In particular $\diam\Om_q\ge9$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\tau$ be the involution of $\cP\cup\cL$ with $\tau(P_0(s))=L_0(s)$ for every row $s$. Every hub $(s,\cdot)$ is adjacent,
in each copy it meets, to $P_0(s)$ or to $L_0(s)$. Let $x$ have row $t$, and let $T\in\{\cP,\cL\}$ be the type of the vertices
of copy $j$ that have the colour of the neighbours of $x$ (by Lemma~\ref{lem:omegaparams}, in each copy one type has that
colour). Let $z=P_0(t)$ if $T=\cP$ and $z=L_0(t)$ if $T=\cL$. We show that at most $A+B+1$ elements $w\in\opp(z)$ have
$d(x,(j,w))\le8$; since $|\opp(z)|=q^5$, the rest are at distance at least 9.

Fix $w\in\opp(z)$ of type $T$. The graph is bipartite and $(j,w)$ has the colour of the neighbours of $x$, so $d(x,(j,w))$ is
odd; suppose it is at most 7 and take a shortest path. It leaves $x$ to a vertex $(c_1,a_0)$ with $a_0\in\{z,\tau(z)\}$, then
alternates walks inside copies with passes copy $\to$ hub $\to$ copy. A pass through a hub of row $s$ goes from $(c,a)$ to
$(c',a')$ with $a,a'\in\{P_0(s),L_0(s)\}$, so $a'=a$ (a \emph{stay}) or $a'=\tau(a)$ (a \emph{switch}). Since $x$ misses $j$ there
is at least one pass. With $e$ copy edges and $p\ge1$ passes the length is $1+e+2p\le7$, and odd, so $e$ is even, $e\le4$ and
$p\le3-e/2$. Let $r\le p$ be the number of switches. Merging the stays, the sequence of labels is a walk in $I(q)$ from $a_0$
with $e$ edges in total, with $\tau$ applied $r$ times in between, ending at $w$. Every edge of $I(q)$ and every switch
changes the type, and $w$ has the type of $z$, so $r$ is even if $a_0=z$ and odd if $a_0=\tau(z)$. The set of ends of walks of
length $f$ from a vertex $u$ is $\bigcup\Gamma_g(u)$ over $g\le f$ with $g\equiv f\pmod 2$, of size at most the sum of the
$s_g$. So:
\begin{itemize}
\item $a_0=z$, $r=0$: $w$ is the end of a walk of length $e\le4$ from $z$, so $d(z,w)\le4$ and $w\notin\opp(z)$.
\item $a_0=z$, $r=2$: $p\ge2$, so $e\le2$; the label walk is $z\to(f_1\text{ edges})\to u_1$, switch, $\to(f_2)\to u_2$,
  switch, $\to(f_3)\to w$, with $f_1+f_2+f_3=e\le2$. At most $B$ such $w$.
\item $a_0=\tau(z)$, $r=1$: $\tau(z)\to(f_1)\to u_1$, switch, $\to(f_2)\to w$ with $f_1+f_2=e\in\{0,2,4\}$. At most $A$ such
  $w$.
\item $a_0=\tau(z)$, $r=3$: $p=3$, $e=0$, and $w=\tau^4(z)=z\notin\opp(z)$ (we count it as 1).
\end{itemize}
So at most $A+B+1$ elements of $\opp(z)$ are within distance 7 of $x$. The closed forms are direct expansions (for $A$:
$s_0s_0+2s_0s_2+s_1s_1+2s_0s_4+2s_1s_3+s_2s_2$). Finally, for $q\ge7$, $q^4(q-5)\ge2q^4\ge14q^3$, so
$q^5-5q^4-8q^3-12q^2-16q-10\ge6q^3-12q^2-16q-10\ge30q^2-16q-10>0$.
\end{proof}

At $q=7,8,9,11,13$ the bound is $1348$, $7286$, $19\,286$, $75\,560$ and $208\,666$ far vertices per hub and missed copy. The
proof uses only: the graph is bipartite; all copies carry the labels $P_0,L_0$; every hub of row $s$ is adjacent, in each copy
it meets, only to $P_0(s)$ or $L_0(s)$; there are no hub--hub edges; $x$ misses a copy. For $q\le6$ the count is not enough
($A+B+1>q^5$).

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:thesisD8}
As defined in the thesis (every copy carries the same labels, and a copy of $K_1$ with label $t$ is joined only to vertices
labelled $t$), $\Om_q$ has diameter at least 9 for $q=7,8,9,11,13$, under every reading of Figs.~2.21a/b, every pattern of
block types and every pairing of point labels with line labels. These are the six table entries.
\end{corollary}

\emph{Computer checks.} The diameter is exactly 9 for $\Om_3$, $\Om_5$ (195\,300 vertices, the thesis value at $(8,8)$),
$\Om_7$ and $\Om_9$ in both readings with both block types present, for several pairings $\sigma$ and patterns (all-pairs
breadth-first search for $q=3,5$; for $q=7,9$ breadth-first search from one vertex in every orbit of an automorphism group
that was checked edge by edge). For all six table values, breadth-first search from a hub gives eccentricity 9 in both
readings; in reading 2 the vertices at distance 9 from a hub are exactly the points opposite its mark in the $3\alpha$
copies it misses. With only one block type the diameter is 10.

\subsection{Relabelling the copies}\label{ssec:relabel}
Diameter at least 9 comes from the shared labels. A labelling that differs from copy to copy is not in the thesis, but it is
the obvious repair, and at $q=2,3$ it works (Section~\ref{ssec:d8open}). We show that it cannot work at the table's $q$ with
labellings that respect the geometry.

A labelling $(P_c,L_c)$ as in Definition~\ref{def:omega} gives each copy one point labelling and one line labelling, used by
every hub class of the corresponding type that meets the copy. For a hub class $Y$ and copies $c,d\in C(Y)$, the
\emph{transfer} $T_Y(c\to d)$ maps the mark of row $t$ in $c$ to the mark of row $t$ in $d$: it is $P_dP_c^{-1}$,
$L_dL_c^{-1}$, $L_dP_c^{-1}$ or $P_dL_c^{-1}$, depending on the types $Y$ sits on in $c$ and $d$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:geometric}
A labelling is \emph{geometric} if every transfer is the restriction (to $\cP$ or to $\cL$) of an automorphism of $I(q)$,
that is, of a collineation of $H(q)$ or, when $q$ is a power of 3, of a duality.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[one automorphism per copy]\label{lem:relabel}
Relabelling every copy $c$ of a graph $\Om_q$ by an automorphism $g_c$ of $I(q)$ (replacing every vertex $(c,y)$ by
$(c,g_c(y))$) gives an isomorphic graph. In particular, if $P_c=g_cP_0$ and $L_c=g_cL_0$ with collineations $g_c$ (or
$P_c=g_cL_0$, $L_c=g_cP_0$ with dualities $g_c$ and the inner class of $c$ exchanged), the graph is isomorphic to the thesis
graph it came from, and its diameter is at least 9.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[reduction]\label{lem:reduction}
Take a geometric labelling, with any pattern and inner classes.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $q$ is not a power of 3, every hub class sits on one type in all copies it meets, all unbarred classes sit on the
  same type, and all barred classes on the other: the inner classes are those of reading 2 or of its dual.
\item If $q$ is a power of 3, the graph is isomorphic to one with a geometric labelling in reading 2 (same pattern).
\item In reading 2 the graph is isomorphic to one with $P_c=P_0$ for all $c$ and $L_c=\lambda_cL_0$ with collineations
  $\lambda_c$ (the \emph{normal form}); in the dual of reading 2 likewise, with the unbarred hubs on lines. We call the bar
  that sits on points the \emph{point bar} $\mathfrak P$ and the other the \emph{line bar} $\mathfrak L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) A transfer from a copy where $Y$ sits on points to one where it sits on lines maps $\cP$ to $\cL$; an automorphism of
$I(q)$ doing that is a duality, which does not exist (Fact~\ref{fact:hex}). In every copy the two unbarred classes sit on the
same type. The copies are linked by unbarred classes: inside a block the pairs $\{k\pm1\}$ (type $a$) or $\{k+3,k+4\}$ (type
$b$), $k\in\Z_5$, form a 5-cycle on the positions, and every class meets every block. So all unbarred classes sit on one
type, and barred classes on the other type in every copy.
(b) Fix a duality $\delta$ and relabel by $\delta$ every copy in which the unbarred classes sit on lines
(Lemma~\ref{lem:relabel}). The new transfers are the old ones composed with $\delta^{\pm1}$, so they are still automorphisms,
and now all unbarred classes sit on points.
(c) In reading 2 the transfers of unbarred classes are restrictions of collineations to $\cP$, and the unbarred classes link
all copies; fixing a copy $c_0$, every $P_cP_{c_0}^{-1}$ is a product of transfers, so $P_c=a_cP_{c_0}$ with a collineation
$a_c$. In the same way (the barred classes also link all copies) $L_c=b_cL_{c_0}$. Relabel copy $c$ by $a_c^{-1}$: $P_c$
becomes $P_{c_0}$ and $L_c$ becomes $a_c^{-1}b_cL_{c_0}$. Unbarred rows and barred rows are different vertices, so renaming
the unbarred rows by $P_0^{-1}P_{c_0}$ and the barred rows by $L_0^{-1}L_{c_0}$ gives $P_c=P_0$ and $L_c=\lambda_cL_0$ with
$\lambda_c=a_c^{-1}b_c$. The dual reading is the same with the bars exchanged.
\end{proof}

From now on we use the normal form, with the rows of the point bar identified with points by $P_0$ (so the hub $(t,X)$,
$X\in\mathfrak P$, is adjacent to point $t$ of every copy in $C(X)$), and collineations $\lambda_c$ acting on $\cP$ and on
$\cL$. Write $B(z,\rho)$ for the ball of radius $\rho$ about $z$ in $I(q)$.

\begin{lemma}[opposite elements in balls]\label{lem:balls}
Let $t,z$ be vertices of $I(q)$ of the same type, $d=d(t,z)$. Then $|B(z,4)\cap\opp(t)|$ is 0 if $z=t$ and $q^4$ if
$d\in\{2,4,6\}$; and $|B(z,2)\cap\opp(t)|$ is 0 if $d\in\{0,2\}$ and $q^2$ if $d\in\{4,6\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A non-backtracking walk of length $i\le6$ in $I(q)$ is a geodesic, and for $i\le5$ each vertex of $\Gamma_i(z)$ is the end of
exactly one such walk from $z$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(a)). The vertices of $B(z,4)$ of the type of $t$ are at even
distance $i\in\{0,2,4\}$ from $z$; we follow $d(t,h_j)$ along the walk $z=h_0,h_1,\dots,h_i$ with Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(a):
from $h_j$ at distance $k\in[1,5]$ from $t$, if $h_{j-1}$ is the neighbour at distance $k-1$ all $q$ choices of $h_{j+1}$ go up
to $k+1$; if $h_{j-1}$ is at distance $k+1$, one choice goes down to $k-1$ and $q-1$ go up; from distance 6 all choices go
down to 5.
\begin{itemize}
\item $d=0$: $B(t,4)$ misses $\opp(t)$.
\item $d=2$: from distance 2, four steps reach distance 6 only if every step goes up: $q\cdot q^3$; none of $B(z,2)$.
\item $d=4$: $i=2$: both steps up, $q^2$. $i=4$: if $h_1$ goes down (one way), $h_2$ must go up ($q-1$ ways) and then
  $h_3,h_4$ up ($q^2$ ways): $(q-1)q^2$. If $h_1$ goes up ($q$ ways, distance 5), $h_2$ goes up to 6 ($q$ ways), $h_3$ goes
  down to 5 ($q$ ways), and $h_4\ne h_2$ must go up ($q-1$ ways): $q^3(q-1)$. Total $q^2+(q-1)q^2+(q-1)q^3=q^4$; and $B(z,2)$
  gives $q^2$.
\item $d=6$: $i=0$ gives 1; $i=2$: $(q+1)(q-1)$; $i=4$: $(q+1)(q-1)q+(q+1)(q-1)q(q-1)=(q+1)(q-1)q^2$. Total
  $1+(q^2-1)+(q^4-q^2)=q^4$, and $B(z,2)$ gives $q^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

For $X\in\mathfrak P$ and $c'\notin C(X)$, let $N(X,c')$ be the set of copies $c_1\in C(X)$ such that some class of $\mathfrak
L$ meets both $c_1$ and $c'$.

\begin{lemma}[routes reach balls]\label{lem:routes}
Let $X\in\mathfrak P$, $c'\notin C(X)$ and $t\in\cP$. Every point $v$ of copy $c'$ with $d((t,X),(c',v))\le7$ lies in
\[
B(t,4)\ \cup\bigcup_{c_1\in N(X,c')}B(\lambda_{c'}\lambda_{c_1}^{-1}(t),4)\ \cup\bigcup_{(c,d)\in W_2}B(\lambda_c\lambda_d^{-1}(t),2),
\]
where $W_2$ is the set of ordered pairs of distinct copies of the following three kinds: $(c',c_2)$ with $c_2$ sharing a class
of $\mathfrak P$ with some $c_1\in C(X)$ and a class of $\mathfrak L$ with $c'$; $(c',c_1)$ with $c_1\in C(X)$ and some $c_2$
sharing a class of $\mathfrak L$ with $c_1$ and with $c'$; $(c_2,c_1)$ with $c_1\in C(X)$ and $c_2$ sharing a class of
$\mathfrak L$ with $c_1$ and a class of $\mathfrak P$ with $c'$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A path from $h=(t,X)$ to $(c',v)$ goes to point $t$ of a copy $c_1\in C(X)$, then alternates walks inside copies (of lengths
$a_1,\dots,a_r\ge0$) with $r-1$ passes copy $\to$ hub $\to$ copy, ending in $c'$. A hub meets each copy at most once, so
consecutive copies differ; $c_1\ne c'$, so $r\ge2$. The length is $1+\sum a_i+2(r-1)\le7$, so $\sum a_i\le8-2r$ and $r\le4$. A
pass through a $\mathfrak P$-hub happens at a point and keeps it. A pass through an $\mathfrak L$-hub $(m,Y)$ happens at a line
and goes from $\lambda_cL_0(m)$ in $c$ to $\lambda_dL_0(m)$ in $d$: it applies $\lambda_d\lambda_c^{-1}$. Collineations
preserve distances, so by induction the end lies in $B(g(t),\sum a_i)$, $g$ the product of the maps applied. The walk starts
and ends at points; a segment before an $\mathfrak L$-pass ends at a line, so the segments around $\mathfrak L$-passes are
odd and $\sum a_i$ is even.
\begin{itemize}
\item $r=2$ ($\sum a_i\le4$): a $\mathfrak P$-pass gives $g=\mathrm{id}$; an $\mathfrak L$-pass needs a class of $\mathfrak L$
  meeting $c_1$ and $c'$, i.e.\ $c_1\in N(X,c')$, and gives $g=\lambda_{c'}\lambda_{c_1}^{-1}$.
\item $r=3$ ($\sum a_i\le2$): $\mathfrak P$ then $\mathfrak P$ gives $\mathrm{id}$. $\mathfrak P$ then $\mathfrak L$ forces
  $a_1=0$, $a_2=a_3=1$ and $g=\lambda_{c'}\lambda_{c_2}^{-1}$ (first kind). $\mathfrak L$ then $\mathfrak P$ forces $a_1=a_2=1$,
  $a_3=0$ and $g=\lambda_{c_2}\lambda_{c_1}^{-1}$ (third kind). $\mathfrak L$ then $\mathfrak L$ forces $a_1=a_3=1$, $a_2=0$:
  both passes use the same row, so $g=\lambda_{c'}\lambda_{c_1}^{-1}$ (second kind).
\item $r=4$: all $a_i=0$, so the walk never reaches a line, all passes are $\mathfrak P$-passes, and $v=t$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

Relabelling every copy $c$ by $\lambda_c^{-1}$ gives $L_c=L_0$ for all $c$ and $P_c=\lambda_c^{-1}P_0$, so the same statement
holds with points and lines exchanged and $\lambda_c$ replaced by $\lambda_c^{-1}$, for $X\in\mathfrak L$. For a pair
$(X,c')$, $X$ in either bar, let $r_4=|N(X,c')|$, and let $m_2$ be the number of pairs in $W_2$ that are not of the form
$(c',c_1)$ with $c_1\in N(X,c')$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:farcount}
Every hub of $X$ has at least $q^5-r_4q^4-m_2q^2$ vertices of $c'$ at distance at least 9 (points if $X\in\mathfrak P$, lines
if $X\in\mathfrak L$). If $r_4=\alpha$, then $m_2\le(4\alpha-1)+2\alpha\cdot2(2\alpha-1)=8\alpha^2-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Take $X\in\mathfrak P$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:balls}, $B(t,4)$ misses $\opp(t)$, each other ball of radius 4 meets it in at most
$q^4$ points and each ball of radius 2 in at most $q^2$. The remaining points of $\opp(t)$ in $c'$ are at distance more than 7
from $(t,X)$, and at odd distance (all points of all copies have one colour), so at distance at least 9. For $m_2$: the first
two kinds are pairs $(c',b)$ with $b\ne c'$, at most $5\alpha-1-r_4=4\alpha-1$ of them outside the radius-4 words; the third
kind has at most $2\alpha$ choices of $c_1$ and, since $c_1$ is met by two classes of $\mathfrak L$ that meet $2\alpha$ copies
each, at most $2(2\alpha-1)$ choices of $c_2$.
\end{proof}

Call $(X,c')$, with $X$ in either bar and $c'\notin C(X)$, \emph{starved} if $r_4=\alpha$.

\begin{lemma}[starved pairs]\label{lem:starved}
For every block pattern there are starved pairs with $X$ unbarred and starved pairs with $X$ barred: for $X=k$ unbarred,
$c'=5B'+k+3$ for a block $B'$ of type $a$ (if all blocks are of type $b$, $c'=5B'+k$); for $X=\bar k$ barred, $c'=5B'+k+3$ for
a block $B'$ of type $b$ (if all blocks are of type $a$, $c'=5B'+k+1$).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The classes meeting the copy at position $i$ are: in an $a$-block, unbarred $i\pm1$ and barred $i+2,i+3$; in a $b$-block,
unbarred $i+1,i+2$ and barred $i\pm1$. For $X=k$ (so $\mathfrak L$ is barred), $c'$ at position $k+3$ of an $a$-block has
barred classes $\{k,k+1\}$. In an $a$-block, $C(k)$ has positions $k-1$ (barred $\{k+1,k+2\}$: shares a class with $c'$) and
$k+1$ (barred $\{k+3,k+4\}$: does not). In a $b$-block, $C(k)$ has positions $k+3$ (barred $\{k+2,k+4\}$: no) and $k+4$
(barred $\{k,k+3\}$: shares). If all blocks are $b$, $c'$ at position $k$ has barred $\{k-1,k+1\}$; $C(k)$ has $k+3$ (barred
$\{k+2,k+4\}$: shares $k+4=k-1$) and $k+4$ (barred $\{k,k+3\}$: no). For $X=\bar k$ ($\mathfrak L$ unbarred), $c'$ at position
$k+3$ of a $b$-block has unbarred $\{k+4,k\}$. In an $a$-block $C(\bar k)$ has $k+2$ (unbarred $\{k+1,k+3\}$: no) and $k+3$
(unbarred $\{k+2,k+4\}$: shares); in a $b$-block, $k-1$ (unbarred $\{k,k+1\}$: shares) and $k+1$ (unbarred $\{k+2,k+3\}$: no).
If all blocks are $a$, $c'$ at position $k+1$ has unbarred $\{k,k+2\}$; $C(\bar k)$ has $k+2$ (unbarred $\{k+1,k+3\}$: no) and
$k+3$ (unbarred $\{k+2,k+4\}$: shares). In each case exactly one copy of $C(X)$ per block shares a class of the other bar with
$c'$, and $c'\notin C(X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[geometric labellings, $q\ge8$]\label{thm:geomq8}
Let $q\ge8$. For every block pattern, every choice of inner classes and every geometric labelling, $\Om_q$ has diameter at
least 9. More precisely, in the normal form, for every starved pair $(X,c')$ every hub $(t,X)$ has at least
$q^5-\alpha q^4-m_2q^2>0$ vertices of $c'$ at distance at least 9. For $q\ge10$ the proof is by hand; for $q=8,9$ it uses
Fact~\ref{fact:C4}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} we may assume the normal form, in reading 2 or its dual. Take a starved pair
(Lemma~\ref{lem:starved}) and apply Corollary~\ref{cor:farcount} with $r_4=\alpha$. For $q\ge10$, $m_2\le8\alpha^2-1$ and the
bound is positive when $q^2(q-\alpha)\ge8\alpha^2$; with $\alpha\le(q+3)/2$ it suffices that $q^2(q-3)\ge4(q+3)^2$, i.e.\
$q^3-7q^2-24q-36\ge0$, which holds at $q=10$ (value 24) and for larger $q$. For $q=8$ ($\alpha=5$), Fact~\ref{fact:C4} gives
$m_2\le140$ for every starved pair, so the bound is at least $32\,768-20\,480-8960=3328$; for $q=9$ ($\alpha=6$), $m_2\le201$
and the bound is at least $59\,049-39\,366-16\,281=3402$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{The case $q=7$}
At $q=7$ the bound of Corollary~\ref{cor:farcount} is negative ($16\,807-5\cdot2401-140\cdot49=-2058$), so the overlaps of the
five radius-4 balls must be used. Here $q$ is odd and 7 is not a power of 3, so the collineations are elements of $G_2(7)\le
\mathrm{SO}(7,7)$ and the reading is reading 2 or its dual. Fix a starved pair $(X,c')$ of the point bar, write $g_1,\dots,g_5$
for the elements $\lambda_{c'}\lambda_{c_i}^{-1}$, $c_i\in N(X,c')$, and $y_i=g_i(t)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:hexfacts}(c),
$B(y,4)\cap\cP=y^\perp\cap Q(6,q)$ for a point $y$, so with $C_i:=y_i^\perp\cap\opp(t)$ the points of $c'$ within distance 7 of
$(t,X)$ that are opposite $t$ lie in $C_1\cup\dots\cup C_5$ and in at most $m_2\le140$ balls of radius 2.

\begin{fact}[eigenvalues in $G_2$]\label{fact:G2eig}
For $h\in G_2(q)$ acting on $V$, the eigenvalues of $h$ (in the algebraic closure, with multiplicity) are $1,x_1^{\pm1},
x_2^{\pm1},x_3^{\pm1}$ with $x_1x_2x_3=1$. (The semisimple part of $h$ is conjugate over $\bar\F_q$ into the maximal torus
$\mathrm{diag}(a,b,c,1,a^{-1},b^{-1},c^{-1})$, $abc=1$, of the subgroup $\SL(3)$ of Fact~\ref{fact:hex}.)
\end{fact}

\begin{lemma}[overlaps, $q$ odd]\label{lem:overlaps}
Let $y,y',t$ be distinct points of $Q(6,q)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $y\perp y'$ and $t$ lies on the line $yy'$, then $y^\perp\cap y'^\perp\cap\opp(t)=\emptyset$. Otherwise
  $|y^\perp\cap y'^\perp\cap\opp(t)|\ge q^3-q$.
\item If $y,y',y''$ are points not on one line, $|y^\perp\cap y'^\perp\cap y''^\perp\cap Q(6,q)|\le(q+1)^2$.
\item If $y,y',y''$ lie on one totally singular line $\ell$ not through $t$, all the pairwise and triple intersections of
  $y^\perp\cap\opp(t)$, $y'^\perp\cap\opp(t)$, $y''^\perp\cap\opp(t)$ equal $E_\ell:=\ell^\perp\cap\opp(t)$, and
  $|E_\ell|\ge q^3-q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $y\perp y'$, the line $yy'$ is totally singular; if $t$ is on it, $y^\perp\cap y'^\perp=(yy')^\perp\subseteq t^\perp$. If
$y,y'$ are not perpendicular, the line $yy'$ meets $Q(6,q)$ only in $y,y'$, so $t$ is not on it. In both remaining cases
$y^\perp\cap y'^\perp$ is the perp of a line, a 5-dimensional space: a parabolic $Q(4,q)$ (if $y\not\perp y'$) or a cone with
vertex the line $yy'$ over a conic (if $y\perp y'$); both have $q^3+q^2+q+1$ points. Its points not opposite $t$ are those of
$\Pi^\perp$, $\Pi=\langle y,y',t\rangle$ a plane. For three independent singular vectors spanning $\Pi$ the Gram matrix of $b$
has zero diagonal, so its rank is 3, 2 or 0 (a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal and rank 1 would be $vv^{\mathsf T}$ with
all $v_i^2=0$). The radical of $\Pi^\perp$ is $\Pi\cap\Pi^\perp$, of dimension 0, 1 or 3. In the first case $\Pi^\perp$
(dimension 4) carries a non-degenerate form with $(q+1)^2$ or $q^2+1$ singular points; in the second it is a cone over a
conic, with $q^2+q+1$ points; in the third $\Pi$ is totally singular and $\Pi^\perp=\Pi\oplus\langle w\rangle$ with $Q(w)\ne0$
($Q(6,q)$ has no totally singular 4-space), so its singular points are those of $\Pi$, $q^2+q+1$. So $|\Pi^\perp\cap
Q(6,q)|\le(q+1)^2$, which gives (a) and, with $\Pi=\langle y,y',y''\rangle$, (b). For (c), $y^\perp\cap y'^\perp=\ell^\perp
\subseteq y''^\perp$, and $|E_\ell|\ge q^3-q$ by (a).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[coincidences]\label{lem:coinc}
If two of $t,y_1,\dots,y_5$ coincide, the hub $(t,X)$ has at least $q^5-4q^4-140q^2=343$ vertices of $c'$ at distance at least
9 (using $m_2\le140$ from Fact~\ref{fact:C4}).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At most four distinct centres other than $t$ remain; apply Corollary~\ref{cor:farcount}.
\end{proof}

Now let $t,y_1,\dots,y_5$ be distinct. Call $y_i,y_j$ \emph{aligned} if they lie on one totally singular line through $t$ (then
$C_i\cap C_j=\emptyset$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:overlaps}(a)); the aligned classes partition $\{y_1,\dots,y_5\}$. Call a set of at
least three $y_i$ lying on one line of $\PG(6,q)$ not through $t$, and maximal with this property, a \emph{rich line}. Three
collinear singular points span a totally singular line, so every collinear triple of $y$'s is inside an aligned class or a
rich line; a rich line meets each aligned class at most once, and two rich lines share at most one point.

\begin{lemma}[configuration types at $q=7$]\label{lem:types}
Let $A_2$ be the number of aligned pairs, $T_3$ the number of triples meeting three different aligned classes, and
$R_3=\sum\binom m3$ over the rich lines ($m$ points each). Then
\[

\]
except when one rich line holds all five points, where $U:=5q^4-4(q^3-q)$. Classified by the sizes of the aligned classes and of
the rich lines there are exactly 17 kinds of configuration. Table~\ref{tab:types} lists them for $q=7$ with
$F=16\,807-U-6860$ and the number $p$ of pairs among $\{t,y_1,\dots,y_5\}$ that every configuration of the kind has
perpendicular: $p=\sum(s+\binom s2)$ over aligned classes of size $s\ge2$, plus $\sum\binom m2$ over rich lines. In the 9 kinds
with $F\le0$, $p\ge6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[few points are moved to perpendicular points]\label{lem:perp}
Let $q$ be odd with $3\mid q-1$ and let $h\in G_2(q)$ fix no point of $Q(6,q)$. Then $\mathrm{Perp}(h):=\{s\in\cP:h(s)\perp s\}$
has at most $(q^5-1)/(q-1)+q^{3.5}+q^3+(q-2)q^{1.5}$ points; at $q=7$, at most 4144 of the 19\,608 points of $H(7)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Count $N_h:=\#\{v\in V:Q(v)=0,\ F(v)=0\}$ with $F(v):=b(v,hv)$; then $|\mathrm{Perp}(h)|=(N_h-1)/(q-1)$. $F$ is a quadratic
form with polar form $b(v,(h+h^{-1})w)$, since $b(w,hv)=b(h^{-1}w,v)$. With $\psi$ a non-trivial additive character of $\F$,
$N_h=q^{-2}\sum_{\lambda,\mu\in\F}\sum_{v\in V}\psi(\lambda Q(v)+\mu F(v))$. The terms with $\mu=0$ give $q^{-2}\cdot q\cdot q^6=q^5$
($Q$ has $q^6$ zeros). For $\mu\ne0$, $\lambda Q+\mu F=\mu(F-\gamma Q)$ with $\gamma=-\lambda/\mu$, and for fixed $\mu$, $\gamma$ runs
over $\F$. The form $R_\gamma=F-\gamma Q$ has polar form $b(v,(h+h^{-1}-\gamma)w)$, so its rank is $7-d_\gamma$ with
$d_\gamma=\dim\ker(h^2-\gamma h+1)$. For a quadratic form of rank $r$ on $\F^7$ ($q$ odd), $|\sum_v\psi(R(v))|=q^{7-r/2}$.
Hence $|N_h-q^5|\le(q-1)\sum_{\gamma}q^{1.5+d_\gamma/2}$ and $|\mathrm{Perp}(h)|\le(q^5-1)/(q-1)+q^{1.5}\sum_\gamma q^{d_\gamma/2}$.

For $\gamma\ne\gamma'$ the polynomials $x^2-\gamma x+1$ and $x^2-\gamma'x+1$ are coprime, so the kernels are independent and
$\sum_\gamma d_\gamma\le7$. Each $d_\gamma\le4$: (i) if $x^2-\gamma x+1=(x-\nu)(x-\nu^{-1})$ with $\nu\in\F$, $\nu\ne\pm1$, the kernel
is $\ker(h-\nu)\oplus\ker(h-\nu^{-1})$, and an eigenvector $v$ with $hv=\nu v$ has $Q(v)=\nu^2Q(v)$, so $Q(v)=0$ and
$\langle v\rangle$ is a fixed point; so $d_\gamma=0$. (ii) $\gamma=\pm2$: $\ker(h\mp1)$ contains no singular vector, so it has
dimension at most 2 (a quadratic form in at least 3 variables over $\F$ has a non-trivial zero), and $\dim\ker(h\mp1)^2\le4$.
(iii) If $x^2-\gamma x+1$ is irreducible with roots $\nu,\nu^q=\nu^{-1}$ in $\F_{q^2}$, then $d_\gamma=2\dim\ker(h-\nu)\le2m(\nu)$,
$m(\nu)$ the multiplicity of $\nu$ as an eigenvalue. By Fact~\ref{fact:G2eig}, since $\nu\ne\nu^{-1}$, $\nu$ occurs at most
once for each index, so $m(\nu)\ge3$ means $\nu=x_1^{\eps_1}=x_2^{\eps_2}=x_3^{\eps_3}$, hence $\nu^{\eps_1+\eps_2+\eps_3}=1$
with $\eps_1+\eps_2+\eps_3\in\{\pm1,\pm3\}$; $\nu\ne1$ forces $\nu^3=1$, and since $3\mid q-1$ the cube roots of unity lie in $\F$,
contradicting irreducibility. So $m(\nu)\le2$ and $d_\gamma\le4$. The function $d\mapsto q^{d/2}$ is increasing and convex; for
integers $0\le d_\gamma\le4$ with $\sum d_\gamma\le7$ the maximum of $\sum q^{d_\gamma/2}$ is at $(4,3,0,\dots,0)$:
$q^2+q^{1.5}+(q-2)$. At $q=7$: $2801+907.5+343+92.6=4144.1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[geometric labellings, $q=7$]\label{thm:geomq7}
For $q=7$, every block pattern, every choice of inner classes and every geometric labelling, $\Om_7$ has diameter at least 9.
The proof uses Fact~\ref{fact:C4} ($m_2\le140$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}(a),(c) we have reading 2 or its dual in normal form; take a starved pair $(X,c')$ of the point bar
and suppose that every hub of $X$ is within distance 8 of every point of $c'$. Consider the 15 elements $g_i$ ($1\le i\le5$)
and $g_i^{-1}g_j$ ($i<j$) of $G_2(7)$. If one of them fixes a point $t$, then two of $t,y_1(t),\dots,y_5(t)$ coincide, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:coinc} gives a far vertex. So none of them has a fixed point. Then for every point $t$ the six points
$t,y_1,\dots,y_5$ are distinct, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:types} and Corollary~\ref{cor:farcount} the configuration at $t$ is of
one of the 9 kinds with $F\le0$, so at least 6 of the 15 pairs among $t,y_1,\dots,y_5$ are perpendicular. The pair $(t,y_i)$ is
perpendicular if and only if $t\in\mathrm{Perp}(g_i)$, and $(y_i,y_j)$ if and only if $t\in\mathrm{Perp}(g_i^{-1}g_j)$.
Summing over the 19\,608 points $t$: $\sum_h|\mathrm{Perp}(h)|\ge6\cdot19\,608=117\,648$ over the 15 elements $h$, while
Lemma~\ref{lem:perp} gives $\sum_h|\mathrm{Perp}(h)|\le15\cdot4144=62\,160$. This is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:geomtable}
For every table value of $\Om_q$, $(10,8)$, $(11,8)$, $(12,8)$, $(14,8)$, $(15,8)$ and $(16,8)$, no geometric labelling of the
copies gives diameter 8, under any reading of Figs.~2.21a/b, any choice of inner classes and any mix of block types. For
$(14,8)$, $(15,8)$ and $(16,8)$ the proof is entirely by hand. For $q=7,8,11,13$ (not powers of 3), reading 1 with both block
types has no geometric labelling at all (Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}(a)), so there the thesis labelling is covered by
Theorem~\ref{thm:twolabels} only.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Definition and the covering criterion}

\begin{definition}\label{def:haar}
Let $G$ be a finite group with identity $e$ and $S\subseteq G$ with $|S|=\Delta$. The \emph{Haar graph} $H(G,S)$ has vertex set
$G\times\{A,B\}$, and $(g,A)$ is adjacent to $(sg,B)$ for every $g\in G$ and $s\in S$. So
\[
N(g,A)=\{(sg,B):s\in S\},\qquad N(h,B)=\{(s^{-1}h,A):s\in S\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The name is from Hladnik, Maru\v{s}i\v{c} and Pisanski \cite{HMP02} (cyclic groups; Est\'elyi and Pisanski \cite{EP16} for
arbitrary groups). $H(G,S)$ is the regular $G$-cover of the dipole with $\Delta$ parallel edges carrying the voltages $s\in S$;
voltage lifts of small base graphs are the standard tool behind many entries of the bipartite table (Loz and \v{S}ir\'a\v{n}
\cite{LS08}; \cite[\S3.4.5]{MS13}). Joining $(g,A)$ to $(gs,B)$ instead gives an isomorphic graph (via $(g,X)\mapsto
(g^{-1},X)$ and $S\mapsto S^{-1}$). Calling the $A$-vertices points and the $B$-vertices lines, $H(G,S)$ is the incidence graph
of the development of $S^{-1}$: the line $(h,B)$ is incident with the points $(s^{-1}h,A)$, $s\in S$. For example $H(\Z_7,\{0,1,3\})$
is the incidence graph of the Fano plane.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:haarbasic}
Let $|S|=\Delta\ge1$. $H(G,S)$ is bipartite, simple and $\Delta$-regular on $2|G|$ vertices; each right translation
$\rho_h(g,X)=(gh,X)$ is an automorphism, and these act regularly on each side.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Edges join the two sides. The neighbours $(sg,B)$, $s\in S$, of $(g,A)$ are distinct, and so are the $(s^{-1}h,A)$. If $(g,A)\sim
(sg,B)$ then $(gh,A)\sim(sgh,B)$, and $\rho_h(e,X)=(h,X)$.
\end{proof}

For $X,Y\subseteq G$ write $XY=\{xy:x\in X,y\in Y\}$, $X^{-1}=\{x^{-1}:x\in X\}$, $X^0=\{e\}$ and $X^j=X\cdot X^{j-1}$.

\begin{proposition}[distances from the two base vertices]\label{prop:haardist}
Let $S\ne\emptyset$, $x\in G$ and $j\ge0$. Then $d((e,A),(x,A))\le2j$ if and only if $x\in(S^{-1}S)^j$;
$d((e,A),(x,B))\le2j+1$ if and only if $x\in S(S^{-1}S)^j$; $d((e,B),(x,B))\le2j$ if and only if $x\in(SS^{-1})^j$; and
$d((e,B),(x,A))\le2j+1$ if and only if $x\in S^{-1}(SS^{-1})^j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A walk of length $l$ from $(e,A)$ passes through $(g_0,A),(g_1,B),(g_2,A),\dots$ with $g_0=e$, $g_i=t_ig_{i-1}$ for odd $i$ and
$g_i=t_i^{-1}g_{i-1}$ for even $i$, where $t_i\in S$ is the letter used at step $i$; every choice of letters gives a walk. So
the ends of the walks of length exactly $2j$ are the $(x,A)$ with $x\in(S^{-1}S)^j$, and those of length exactly $2j+1$ are the
$(x,B)$ with $x\in S(S^{-1}S)^j$. A shortest path followed by back-and-forth steps along one edge is a walk of any larger length
of the same parity. From $(e,B)$ the same argument gives the last two statements.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[covering criterion]\label{thm:covering}
Let $S\ne\emptyset$ and $D\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $D=2\kappa$, then $\diam H(G,S)\le D$ if and only if $S(S^{-1}S)^{\kappa-1}=G$.
\item If $D=2\kappa+1$, then $\diam H(G,S)\le D$ if and only if $(S^{-1}S)^\kappa=G$ and $(SS^{-1})^\kappa=G$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover $\diam H(G,S)=\max(\ecc(e,A),\ecc(e,B))$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The last statement holds because the right translations are automorphisms acting transitively on each side
(Lemma~\ref{lem:haarbasic}): every vertex is mapped to $(e,A)$ or $(e,B)$ by an automorphism.
(a) If $\diam\le2\kappa$, then for $x\in G$ the distance $d((e,A),(x,B))$ is odd and at most $2\kappa$, so at most $2(\kappa-1)+1$,
and $x\in S(S^{-1}S)^{\kappa-1}$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:haardist}. Conversely, if $S(S^{-1}S)^{\kappa-1}=G$ then
$d((g,A),(h,B))=d((e,A),(hg^{-1},B))\le2\kappa-1$ for all $g,h$. Fix $s\in S$. Since $(g',A)\sim(sg',B)$,
$d((g,A),(g',A))\le2\kappa$; since $(h',B)\sim(s^{-1}h',A)$, $d((h,B),(h',B))\le2\kappa$.
(b) If $\diam\le2\kappa+1$, same-side distances are even, hence at most $2\kappa$, so both products are $G$. Conversely, if both
hold, all same-side distances are at most $2\kappa$, and $d((g,A),(h,B))\le1+d((sg,B),(h,B))\le2\kappa+1$.
\end{proof}

At $D=5$ ($\kappa=2$) the criterion reads: every element of $G$ is a product $a^{-1}bc^{-1}d$ and also a product $ab^{-1}cd^{-1}$
with $a,b,c,d\in S$. A shortest path never backtracks, so the same count gives the per-side Moore bound
$|G|\le\sum_{j<D}(\Delta-1)^j$ (that is, $2|G|\le\Mb(\Delta,D)$). For $\Delta\ge3$ and $D\in\{8,9,10\}$ it is strict: with
equality, the reduced words counted (those of length at most $D-1$ and of the parity that Theorem~\ref{thm:covering}
requires) have pairwise distinct values, which forces girth at least $2D$; a bipartite graph of diameter $D$ has girth at
most $2D$, and a bipartite graph of diameter $D$, girth $2D$ and all degrees $\Delta\ge3$ is the incidence graph of a thick
generalized $D$-gon of order $(\Delta-1,\Delta-1)$, which does not exist for these $D$ \cite{FeitHigman}.

\subsection{Prior work}
The type of construction is not new. Haar graphs of $\SL(2,q)$ appear in the cage literature: according to the dynamic cage
survey \cite{EJ13}, the smallest known cubic graph of girth 30, on $1\,143\,408=2|\SL(2,83)|$ vertices, is a voltage lift over
$\SL(2,83)$ of the dipole with three parallel edges \cite{EJ11}, that is, a Haar graph $H(\SL(2,83),S)$ with $|S|=3$; and Bray,
Parker and Rowley \cite{BPR00} use $\PSL(2,q)\times\Z_3$. Haar graphs of cyclic groups appear at $D=3$ in \cite{FMP13}, and
dipole lifts over metacyclic groups are probably behind Loz's values \cite{LS08}. In the sources we searched we found no use
of Haar graphs of $\SL(2,q)$ or $\PSL(2,q)$, alone or times a cyclic group, for large bipartite graphs of diameter 8, 9 or 10.
Theorem~\ref{thm:covering} is routine and we claim no novelty for it.

\section{Formal verification}\label{app:lean}
%======================================================================

Many statements of this paper are proved in Lean~4 (version 4.34.1) with Mathlib, in the library
\texttt{lean/BipartiteDD} of the repository. For every theorem listed below, \texttt{\#print axioms} reports only the
standard axioms \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound}; there is no \texttt{sorry},
\texttt{admit}, new \texttt{axiom} or \texttt{native\_decide}, and finite checks use \texttt{decide} or \texttt{decide +kernel},
which the kernel checks. A graph statement has the form ``there is a graph on \texttt{Fin $N$} that is 2-colourable, has every
vertex of degree at most $\Delta$ (or exactly $\Delta$), and has diameter at most $D$'', so the vertex count is exactly $N$.

\section{Computer checks}\label{app:checks}
%======================================================================

Every graph of Tables~\ref{tab:summary} and~\ref{tab:haar}, and every ``computer-checked'' statement, was checked as follows.
All programs and logs are in the repository\\ \url{https://github.com/dogaozden/degree-diameter}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{All-pairs check.} A C program reads an edge list, checks that the graph is simple, bipartite and connected and has
  maximum degree at most $\Delta$, and runs a bit-parallel breadth-first search from every vertex, with no symmetry assumed.
  Every graph in this paper with at most $4.1$ million vertices (all graphs of Sections~\ref{sec:d3}--\ref{sec:d7}) was
  checked this way, after being rebuilt from its definition or data file. The $D=7$ graphs were built from their data files by
  the builder that produced them; the check of the resulting edge list is independent of that builder, and three of them
  ($(7,7)$, $(9,7)$, $(10,7)$) were also checked with a second breadth-first-search program.
\item \emph{Families.} The fourth hat: built three ways (from (C1)--(C6), from the point rows and from the line rows of
  Table~\ref{tab:hat4}), for every prime power $q\le13$ and every admissible $(a,c)$; degrees, both covering properties and exact
  diameter of $G$ and $R$; a separate program rebuilt it for $q=4,5,7,8,9,11,13$ and compared every row of
Table~\ref{tab:hat4} with the computed neighbourhoods.
  Theorem~\ref{thm:A}: line sums and the graph for $q=3,5,7,9$. Proposition~\ref{prop:C}: exactly the pairs $\{a,q-a\}$ give
  2-ovoids, for $q=3,5,7,9,11,13,25,27$; Corollary~\ref{cor:C1} for $q=3,5$. Section~\ref{sec:d6}: the graphs for
  $q=5,7,9,11,13$ (and $q=13$, $m=3$), exactness of $W$ on all free points for $q=3,5,7$ and $q=4,8,16$, the identities of
  Lemmas~\ref{lem:Jz}, \ref{lem:key}, \ref{lem:Jz-even}, \ref{lem:key-even} re-checked symbolically, and the five regular graphs
  of Theorem~\ref{thm:R}.
\item \emph{Diameter 7, small $q$.} Every theorem of Section~\ref{sec:d7} was tested on $H(q)$ for $q=2,3,4,5$, built from
  \eqref{eq:tits}, with positive tests and negative controls (adding one turning point to a segment set, or breaking (H3) at
  one point, gives diameter 8).
\item \emph{Diameter 8.} The hexagons $H(q)$, $q\le13$, three times by separate programs; the exact diameters and
  eccentricities stated after Corollary~\ref{cor:thesisD8}; Fact~\ref{fact:C4} by two separate programs; the rows of
  Table~\ref{tab:types} by three programs.
\item \emph{Haar graphs.} By Theorem~\ref{thm:covering}, two breadth-first searches, from $(e,A)$ and $(e,B)$, decide the
  diameter. Each graph of Table~\ref{tab:haar} was accepted by at least two separately written programs (each building the
  group from scratch, including the finite field and the matrix group), or by one program and a Lean proof; twelve of them (up
  to 540\,360 vertices) also passed the all-pairs check. Negative controls (one generator altered, or $D$ lowered by
  one) are rejected.
\end{itemize}
