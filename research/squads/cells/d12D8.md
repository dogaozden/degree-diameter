## The table value
4,650,100 (DS14v2 Table 4; CombinatoricsWiki not readable from the container). It is 2·35·66,430 = 7 × (12,7) =
7 × 664,300, and 664,300 = 2·5·|H(9)| is Delorme's D = 7 graph X_2(M): five copies of the generalized hexagon H(9)
(q+1 = 10 edges per vertex) plus two "hop" matchings between levels (2 more edges). The same decoding fits (10,8),
(11,8), (14,8), (16,8) (factor ⌊Δ/2⌋+1 over the (Δ,7) value) and (13,8) (factor 5 over X_1, Δ = q+2 = 13). So the
record is an algebraic construction, a lift by a group of order 35 of "H(9) + 2 extra edges per vertex". Random-like
methods (Haar graphs, random lifts) are far below it: SIZE_LAW predicts ~3.6M for Haar graphs; random lifts of
H(9) + 2 hops reach group order 12–14 (≈ 1.7–1.9M vertices).

## Tried (filled in as the job goes)

### Structure of the problem (Opus analysis, round 1)
- A lift of a base multigraph B (points | lines of H(9), 12 edges per vertex) by a group A has diameter ≤ 8 iff for
  every point p, line l and a ∈ A some non-backtracking walk p → l of length ≤ 7 in B has voltage a. From a point there
  are 12·11^6 + … ≈ 21.4M such walks for 66,430·|A| targets; |A| = 36 needs every (p,l) pair's ≈ 322 walks to cover
  36 values. Random voltages miss ≈ |A|·e^(−322/|A|) values per pair, i.e. ~10^7 misses over all pairs: the table
  graph is a zero-waste design, not a lucky lift.
- Walk counts (tools/nbwalks): in X_2 with arbitrary hop matchings the poorest targets are lines at hexagon distance 1
  or 3 at the same level (9–20 walks; 6 for some sources). Putting both hops on one perfect matching of the incidence
  graph (tripled edges) raises the minimum to 25. But the misses of random designs are bulk: 98% are far lines
  (hexagon distance 5), about one missing element per far line (cards c4, c14).
- What a structured design must do for far pairs: they have two spare steps, so their walks are the geodesic, one
  "bounce"/hop excursion, and the q² = 81 apartment walks (length 7). For levels reached through a hop at the source
  (p → M(p) ⇝ l), the walks are the q+1 geodesics between the opposite lines M(p) and l; their voltages must cover
  the second factor. So a natural sufficient condition is (B): for every pair of opposite elements of H(9), the q+1
  geodesics between them have voltages covering the second factor (random voltages satisfy this for only ~10% of
  pairs at |A_2| = 7).

### Why the cell did not fall
- The table graph is a structured (zero-waste) lift: every one of the 66,430² point-line pairs has to see all 35
  elements of the group among its ≈ 322 short walks. Random or locally optimised voltages miss about one element per
  far line at order 35. Symmetry does not rescue them: even a semiregular group of order 91 (the Singer torus of
  SL(3,9) ⊂ G_2(9), available in hexgen's coordinates) divides the ~6·10^7 expected misses only by 91.
- Every unstructured family tried plateaus at about 2.0–2.3M vertices (H(7), H(8), H(9) bases, GQ(11)), below even
  Haar graphs (SIZE_LAW: ~3.6M), because a non-vertex-transitive lift must cover from every base vertex. Haar graphs
  themselves stop at the first-moment ceiling 2N_h ≈ 4.53M < 4.65M.
- So beating 4,650,100 needs an algebraic design at least as good as Delorme's. Reconstructing his (≥ 1985) lift was the
  main open step; its paper could not be read here, and no rule we tried reproduces it.

- With the table graph in hand, the "hub" surgery of research/squads/exp2/hexd7 (+2 vertices per freed pair at D = 7)
  is the obvious way to add a few vertices at D = 8.
- Isometric hops (dualities) are the one structural ingredient that measurably helped (c12).
