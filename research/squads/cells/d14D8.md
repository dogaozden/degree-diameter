## 1. What the table graph is

14,172,480 = 40·|H(11)|, H(q) = incidence graph of the split Cayley hexagon (2(q^6−1)/(q−1) vertices, degree q+1,
diameter 6). The even-Δ D = 8 table values fit 5(q+5)/2·|H(q)| with q = Δ − 3 for Δ = 10, 12, 14, 16 (m = 30, 35, 40, 45;
(11,8) = 30·|H(8)|), and the D = 7 values for Δ = 12, 14, 16 are 5·|H(q)|. The D = 7 graph is known exactly (cell notes
d12D7.md, d16D7.md): five levels i ∈ Z5 of H(q) and (p,i) ~ (φ(p), i+1), (φ(p), i+2) for one bijection φ (any φ works).
The D = 8 graph is (q+5)/2 = 8 times larger. We could not find the D = 8 paper's construction (web search, Oct 25), so
the family below is a reconstruction attempt, not the table construction itself.

## 2. Reformulation used throughout

A bipartite graph has diameter ≤ 8 iff every vertex of one side is within distance 7 of every vertex of the other side
(same-side pairs then follow: d(a, a′) ≤ 1 + d(a, b) for a neighbour b of a′). So the D = 8 problem is a pure
"cross-distance ≤ 7" covering problem. For a Z_m-lift of H(q) + 2 perfect matchings (degree q + 3) it is checked
exactly by bitmask walks from the N point-copies (p, 0) (research/squads/cells/d14D8_work/lifteval.c).

## 3. Why the cell is hard (counting)

From a point, there are 14·13^6 ≈ 67.6M non-backtracking walks of length 7. Beating the table needs > 7,086,240
targets per side, i.e. fewer than 9.5 walks per target; the table graph achieves 9.5. Random voltage designs need about
ln(#targets) ≈ 16 walks per target (coupon collector), so they stop near m ≈ 16 at q = 11 (round 1). Haar graphs:
our best D = 8 Haar graphs sit at W/N ≈ ln N − 3.8 (Δ = 7..15: 6.8, 7.9, 8.7, 9.7, 11.1, 12.5); at Δ = 14 this gives
about 11.5M vertices, 19 % short (SIZE_LAW.md predicts 10.4M for random S; orbit search adds ~8–12 % elsewhere).

## 5. Why these methods stop (the capacity picture)

- **Random and annealed designs** are coupon-collector limited: a target needs about ln(#targets) walks, so at q = 11 the
  ~250 reduced walks per line give m ≈ 14–17; annealing gains ×1.3–1.4 (q = 7: 12 → 17; q = 11: 14 → 17–18) and then
  stalls. The table's 40 is ×2.5 beyond that. No structured family above beat uniform random by more than noise.
- **Walk counts per class** (research/squads/cells/d14D8_work/nblev.c, exact non-backtracking walk counts from (p, 0) to
  every (line, level), q = 11, 3–4 sources): in the D7 skeleton (5 levels, doubled random φ) the class "level 0 at
  hexagon distance 3" has only 14 walks per target (min; mean 31) and the far classes ±1, ±2 have 29–34 (mean 56–65).
  With φ along hexagon flags the distance-3 class rises to 77 but the far classes stay at 31. Two-level skeletons
  (both jumps +1) have about 122 per class and level. If the table graph is D7 × Z_8 it maps 14–31 walks onto 8 values
  with almost no collisions; nothing we built comes within a factor 2 of that hashing quality, and walks that differ
  only by order give equal voltages in abelian lifts, so the true number of distinct options is lower still.
- **Haar graphs**: best D = 8 Haar graphs satisfy W/N ≈ ln N − 3.8 for Δ = 7..15 (W = Δ(Δ−1)^6 words, N = group order);
  at Δ = 14 that is about 11.5M vertices (−19 %). SIZE_LAW.md predicts 10.4M for random S; orbit-symmetric S added
  8–12 % in every other D = 8 cell. Not run at this cell (it cannot reach 14.17M on that evidence).

## 6. Verdict and what would be needed

The cell will not fall to random, annealed, symmetric or "obviously structured" lifts, nor to Haar search. The (14,8)
value belongs to a family (Δ = 10, 12, 14, 16 at D = 8; all four unbeaten) whose construction we have not reconstructed:
its hashing efficiency is about 2.5× that of the best optimized designs. The way in is the construction itself: once a
graph of 40·|H(11)| vertices is in hand, the D = 7 experience (cells 12-7, 16-7: freeing matching edges, Haar hubs)
suggests a +0.5–4 % surgery. Leads, none tested to a result:
1. Find the D = 8 paper (likely the same group that did the 5·|H(q)| D = 7 graphs; CombinatoricsWiki colour key), or
   derive a provable D7 × Z_{(q+5)/2} design; the multiplier (q+5)/2 = (q+1)/2 + 2 hints at 2-to-1 bundle voltages
   plus two extra routes per level.
2. Test any candidate first at q = 7 (exact check 40 s): m = 31 there is also a (10,8) record.
3. research/theorems/REGULARITIES.md §2.1 (another session, same conclusion) fits all six such D = 8 cells with
   m = (2j+1)(⌊(q−1)/2⌋ + 3(j−1)), j = number of extra edges: (13,8) = 3·(q−1)/2·|H(11)| with ONE matching. That j = 1
   case is the cleanest test bed. If the construction also works at small q, its targets are m = 6 at q = 5 and 9 at
   q = 7, while one random matching with random voltages already fails at m = 5 (exact cost 1,178 at q = 5 and 5,206 at
   q = 7, this session), and the 3-level skeleton Z3 × Z2 (m = 6) with random sub-voltages is worse still (cost
   7,045–39,107 at q = 5, random or flag-type φ). So the structure, if it scales down, is invisible to random search but
   can be tested in seconds at q = 5, 7. The (q−1)/2 suggests sub-voltages in F_q*/±1:
   a Möbius coordinate on a line or pencil is 2-to-1 onto F_q*/±1 (plus 0 and ∞), so a route family indexed by a line,
   a pencil or a geodesic bundle would cover that group automatically if its voltages are Möbius functions of the family
   parameter. Plain projective parameters reduced mod m (r2 c3) are not this: they behave like random labels.
