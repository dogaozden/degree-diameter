Problem statement

Build the biggest network you can, under three rules. Nobody knows the true maximum for most sizes, so mathematicians keep a table of the best ones anyone has found. Beating an entry means building a bigger graph that still follows all three rules.

Two sides

Every vertex is brass or blue, and every edge joins a brass vertex to a blue one. No edge stays inside one side. This is what "bipartite" means.

A handshake limit Δ

No vertex may have more than Δ neighbours. In the table, Δ runs from 3 to 16. Our records are at Δ = 4, 5, 7, 11, 13 and 15.

Short chains D

Any two vertices must be joined by a path of at most D edges. This is the diameter. A small D forces every vertex to be close to all the others.

Try it · a perfect small answer

The Heawood graph: Δ = 3, D = 3, 14 vertices

Click any vertex. It turns white, and the rest are coloured by how many steps away they are. Everyone is within 3 steps. No bipartite graph with these rules can have more than 14 vertices, so this one is a perfect answer. The big 3D view at the top does the same thing to our record graphs, with up to 1,774 vertices.

Why it is hard

Every step can multiply the reach by Δ − 1, at most

From one vertex, step 1 reaches at most Δ vertices. Each of those has at most Δ − 1 new neighbours, and so on: a tree. That caps how big any graph can be. Real graphs fall short because paths collide and reach the same vertex twice. The reach bars in the 3D panel compare each step with this tree.

most at step k = Δ·(Δ−1)k−1 · ceiling for two sides = 2·(1 + (Δ−1) + … + (Δ−1)D−1)
